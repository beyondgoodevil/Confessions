---

The New Testament word for repentance is **metanoia** (μετάνοια), from *meta* ("change", "after") and *nous* ("mind", the inner eye of the soul). Repentance is therefore not first a feeling of regret or a penalty paid, but a **turning of the whole person** back toward God. Here's what that means:

### 1. The Word Itself

"From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.'" (Matthew 4:17)

- **Significance:** the first word of Christ's preaching is *metanoeite*, "change your mind". It is a call to see differently, because the kingdom is near, not a demand to settle an account.
- Since the **nous** is the faculty by which a person perceives God (see [[Nous]]), metanoia is a change in what one sees and loves, not only in what one does.

### 2. Coming to Oneself

"But when he came to himself, he said, 'How many of my father's hired servants have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger!'" (Luke 15:17)

- **Significance:** in the parable of the prodigal son, repentance begins when the son **comes to himself**. Sin is a kind of exile from one's own nature; repentance is waking up and returning home.
- The father runs to meet him before he has finished his confession. Repentance is met by love, not by a ledger.

### 3. Godly Sorrow and Worldly Sorrow

"For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death." (2 Corinthians 7:10)

1. **Godly Sorrow**: grief that turns toward God and leads to change and life.
2. **Worldly Sorrow**: grief that turns in on itself, as remorse, despair or wounded pride. Judas regretted; Peter repented.

The Fathers call true repentance **"joyful sorrow"** (*charmolypē*), the tears of mourning (*penthos*) that wash the soul and bring consolation. St. John Climacus devotes a step of the *Ladder* to it (step 7).

### 4. Repentance as a Way of Life

- St. John Climacus: "Repentance is the renewal of baptism" (*Ladder*, step 5). Repentance restores what baptism first gave.
- It is not a single event but the **whole Christian life**. Even Luther's first thesis says the same: when Christ said "Repent", He willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance.
- This is why the Jesus Prayer ends "have mercy on me, a sinner" (see [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]]), and why Psalm 51 is read in so many services: "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, A broken and a contrite heart— These, O God, You will not despise." (Psalm 51:17)

### 5. How the Latin West Shifted the Emphasis

1. **"Do Penance"**: the Latin Vulgate rendered *metanoeite* as *paenitentiam agite*, "do penance". This drew attention to acts performed to make up for sin.
2. **Satisfaction**: medieval Western theology came to describe the sacrament as contrition, confession and **satisfaction**, the penalty that repairs the debt owed to God's justice.
3. **The Reformers' Reaction**: Erasmus pointed out that the Greek means a change of mind, and the Reformers rejected the system of penances, but they kept the framework of a debt to justice, now paid by Christ (see [[Penal Substitution Leads to Nestorianism]]).

The Orthodox emphasis stays with the Greek: sin is a **sickness** of the soul, and repentance is the beginning of its **healing**.

### 6. Confession as Healing

- Christ gave the apostles authority to forgive sins (see [[Apostles Have the Authority to Forgive Sins: Book of John]]), and the Church exercises it in confession.
- In the Slavic practice, the priest reminds the penitent that Christ stands there invisibly to receive the confession, and that he himself is **only a witness**.
- Confession is described as coming to a **hospital**, not a courtroom. The "penance" (*epitimion*) given is medicine for the soul, not payment of a fine.
- Repentance cooperates with grace: God heals, and the person turns and consents (see [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]]).

### Conclusion

Metanoia is a change of the nous: a turning of mind, heart and life back to God. Scripture presents it as the prodigal coming to himself and as godly sorrow that leads to salvation; the Fathers describe it as the renewal of baptism and as joyful mourning. Where the Latin tradition came to stress penance and satisfaction, Orthodoxy keeps the language of sickness and healing, and treats repentance not as a payment made once but as the shape of the whole Christian life.
