Every Chinese dynasty after 221 BCE had to answer the same question: why should *this* family rule? The answer the Han worked out and everyone after them repeated had two parts. The **Mandate of Heaven** (*tianming*) said that Heaven gives the throne to a virtuous house and takes it away from a wicked one. The **Five Phases** (*wuxing*: wood, fire, earth, metal, water) said that history moves in a fixed cycle, so each dynasty rules under one phase, with its own colour, and must give way to the next. Together they were backed by **omens**, by the rituals of **abdication**, and by great sacrifices to Heaven. This note follows the idea from the Qin to the Song, using the Cambridge Histories of China (vols. 1, 2, 3 and 5) and Dieter Kuhn's *Age of Confucian Rule*, with the early texts checked against Chan's *Source Book* and Van Norden's *Mengzi*.

### 1. The Zhou Inheritance

The Mandate began as a Zhou idea, used to justify the conquest of the Shang (see [[Early China: From the Jade Age to the Zhou Conquest]]). The Zhou poets warned their own descendants that the gift could be lost:

> Heaven's Mandate is not constant. ... In Yin you should see as in a mirror / That the great mandate is not easy [to keep]. (*Book of Odes* no. 235, "King Wen", in Chan, pp. 6–7)

In the *Book of History* the Duke of Zhou is even blunter: "Heaven is not to be trusted" (Chan, p. 6). The Mandate was a reward for virtue that had to be kept up, not a permanent possession.

Mencius gave the idea its sharpest edge. Asked whether King Wu had been allowed to kill his ruler, the last Shang king, he answered:

> I have indeed heard of the execution of this one fellow Zhou, but I have not heard of it as the assassination of one's ruler. (*Mengzi* 1B8, Van Norden)

Zhu Xi's later comment sums up the logic: "If all within the four seas turn toward someone, then he becomes the Son of Heaven. If the world turns against him, then he becomes 'a mere fellow.'" Mencius also explained how Heaven makes its choice known. Heaven "does not speak but simply reveals the Mandate through actions and affairs" (5A5). When Yao died, the lords, the litigants and the singers all went to Shun rather than to Yao's son, and that was the sign. Then comes a line that later usurpers knew by heart:

> Had he simply occupied the palace of Yao and exiled the son of Yao, this would have been usurpation, not Heaven giving it to him. The "Great Announcement" says, "Heaven sees as my people see; Heaven hears as my people hear." (5A5)

Two things from this inheritance matter for everything that follows. The Mandate could justify **rebellion** against a bad ruler, and the model for a *peaceful* transfer was **Yao handing the throne to Shun**.

Loewe points out that during the Warring States the doctrine was nearly useless, since a Zhou king still technically existed until 256 BCE and no rival could claim the mandate while he lived. All that survived, in his words, was "the ideal or fiction that the kings of Chou constituted repositories of moral virtues" (*CHC* 1, pp. 728, 733).

### 2. Zou Yan and the Cycle of Five

The second strand is cosmological. The *Book of History*'s "Great Norm" already lists the Five Agents as the first of nine categories Heaven gave to Yu: "Water, Fire, Wood, Metal, and Earth" (Chan, p. 8). Yu's father Kun had failed because by damming the flood he "created a chaos among the Five Agents".

The man who turned the Five Phases into a theory of *history* was **Zou Yan** (3rd century BCE). Kramers calls him "the first great exponent of the idea that the vicissitudes of human history are determined by the successive domination of the so-called Five Phases", generated by yin and yang (*CHC* 1, p. 750). Sima Qian thought his theories fantastic, but recorded that according to Zou Yan, "ever since the separation of heaven and earth the Five Powers (Five Agents) have been in rotation" (*Shiji* 74, in Chan, pp. 246–47). Zou Yan belonged to the *fangshi*, the experts in esoteric arts, and Kramers stresses that the line between them and the Confucian *ru* was blurred.

Loewe's chapter on Han cosmology explains why the idea spread so widely. Once a cycle of five was accepted, it was "applied excessively, arbitrarily, and indiscriminately to all orders and manner of being": five seasons, directions, colours, emotions, organs. By Wang Mang's time it was "playing a major role in public life", and it was "invoked as a means of procuring intellectual support for the establishment of imperial dynasties" (*CHC* 1, pp. 689–91).

There were two different orders for the cycle, and the choice between them mattered politically:

| Order | How one phase follows another | Sequence | Implied view of the last dynasty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conquest** ("overcoming") | Each phase defeats the one before | Earth → wood → metal → fire → water → earth | Your predecessor was beaten |
| **Generation** ("producing") | Each phase grows out of the one before | Wood → fire → earth → metal → water → wood | Your predecessor gave birth to you |

The *Lüshi chunqiu*, compiled in Qin around 240 BCE, gives the conquest version and ties it to omens. Before the Yellow Emperor, Heaven showed worms, so he chose earth and yellow; before Yu, grass that did not die in winter, so wood and green; before Tang, metal blades coming out of liquid, so metal and white; before King Wen, "scarlet birds holding texts inscribed in red", so fire and red. Then comes the prediction: "It will of course be the energy of water that must displace that of fire" (quoted in *CHC* 1, p. 730). Loewe notes that the passage quietly combines two inconsistent ideas, an arbitrary Heaven that chooses and a cycle that runs by itself. Reconciling the two kept Han thinkers busy for two centuries.

### 3. Qin: Conquest and the Power of Water

The First Emperor took the *Lüshi chunqiu*'s prediction at its word. *Shiji* 6, as translated by Bodde:

> The First Emperor advanced the theory of the cyclical revolution of the Five Powers. He maintained that inasmuch as the Chou had held the power of fire, and Ch'in had supplanted Chou, ... now was the beginning [of the flourishing] of the power of water. (*CHC* 1, p. 77)

Everything followed from water:
- **Black**, water's colour, for clothing, pennons and flags.
- **Six**, water's number, as the standard: tallies and official hats of six inches, chariots of six feet, six horses to a team.
- The Yellow River was renamed the **Powerful Water**.
- Most strikingly, harsh law: "With harshness, violence and extreme severity, everything was determined by law (*fa*) ... there would come an accord with the numerical succession of the Five Powers."

Water goes with winter, the season of darkness and death, so a severe government was the cosmically correct one. Bodde notes that some scholars think this report is anachronistic (p. 77), but either way the Han believed it.

What Qin did *not* do was claim a mandate. Loewe stresses that the inscriptions the First Emperor set up after 221 credit his success "almost entirely in terms of the personal credit that was his due": conquest of rivals, pacification and a single code of laws, with little reference to any superhuman power (*CHC* 1, p. 731). The title *huangdi*, "August Thearch", was meant to last forever. The contrast with 220 CE is Loewe's starting point. In 221 BCE it was enough to boast of conquest. In 220 CE Cao Pi had to show that "his succession followed the will of superhuman powers" (p. 727).

### 4. Early Han: A Victory Without a Mandate

The Han founder, Liu Bang, was a commoner who won by force, and early Han writers did not yet say he had received the Mandate. Loewe finds several statements crediting Heaven with Gaozu's victory, but "no statement of the specific bestowal of the mandate on his dynastic house". One text even observes that Zhou had received the mandate but Han had not (*CHC* 1, pp. 733–34).

The first Han political writers concentrated on a more practical question: how had the mighty Qin been destroyed by "a band of weak, badly led, and badly armed insurgents", and how could the Han avoid the same fate (p. 732)?
- **Lu Jia**'s *New Analects*, written at Gaozu's own order, blamed Qin's excessive punishments, arrogance and extravagance.
- **Jia Yi**'s three-part essay on Qin's faults stressed respect for ethical values.
- **Jia Shan** urged Emperor Wen to imitate the Zhou kings, who cared for their subjects and listened to criticism.

The theory of rulership that Qin had inherited from **Xunzi** and the *Lüshi chunqiu* explained kingship as a human necessity: people must group together under a leader to survive. Xunzi also held that personal qualities matter more than heredity, which suited conquerors like the First Emperor and Gaozu but sat badly with a hereditary dynasty (pp. 729–31).

### 5. Dong Zhongshu: Heaven Speaks Through Omens

The great synthesis came from **Dong Zhongshu** (c. 179–104 BCE). According to Loewe, his scheme joined three ideas: the authority of Heaven, the cycle of the Five Phases, and an elaborate theory of omens. No other system of the time explained "imperial rule as an integral part of a major system of the universe" (*CHC* 1, p. 733). Dong also revived the old Zhou term "mandate", though it only became central after his death.

Dong explained omens as natural resonance, not as the moods of spirits. In the *Chunqiu fanlu*:

> When the note F in the one is struck, the note F on the other will respond to it. ... Similarly, when an emperor or a king is about to rise, auspicious omens will first appear, and when he is about to perish, unlucky omens will first appear. (ch. 57, in Chan, p. 283)

Chan comments that what was new was explaining portents "in terms of natural law": they are results of yin and yang, not "expressions of the pleasure or displeasure of spiritual beings". In a memorial to the throne Dong put the ruler at the centre of this system: "The ordinances of Heaven are termed destiny and these cannot be put into operation except by a man of saintly qualities" (*Hanshu* 56, trans. Loewe, *CHC* 1, p. 734).

Two further contributions lasted.
- **History as a key to the present.** Dong wrote that "the method of the Spring and autumn annals is to cite events of the past in order to explain those of the future". Loewe traces a line from this to Sima Guang's use of history as a mirror a thousand years later (*CHC* 1, pp. 712–13).
- **A moral verdict on Qin.** Earlier critics said Qin had made practical mistakes. Dong arraigned it "on moral grounds", as a regime that had struck at civilization itself, and "set a pattern of criticism of Ch'in that only exceptional historians and statesmen have been ready to discount" (p. 734).

The omen theory quickly became a weapon for officials. An excess of yin, shown in floods or eclipses, could be read as too much female influence at court. When a solar eclipse and earth tremors coincided in 29 BCE, specialists "were quick to seize on the events as a means of criticizing current policies" (p. 221). Jing Fang read a fallen tree that stood up again in 4 BCE as a sign that "the imperial consorts monopolise privilege" (p. 712). Good omens were collected just as eagerly: birds roosting on the palace, sweet dew and golden dragons under Emperor Xuan became the names of reign periods. Loewe's verdict is that these reactions "were arbitrary and due to a desire to fasten on particular events for propaganda purposes" (p. 713).

There was one famous sceptic. **Wang Chong** (27–c. 100 CE) argued that Heaven has "neither the will nor the means" to warn mankind, and that thunder comes from heat, not from Heaven's anger. Loewe compares him to Lucretius (pp. 699, 713). Wang Chong still spoke of the Han founders' destiny (*ming*), but he never used the phrase *tianming* (p. 739).

### 6. Choosing the Patron Element

For the Han, picking a phase was "a declaration of faith that the dynasty was entitled to its appropriate place in the universal and unbreakable sequence", and it also said what you thought of the dynasty before you (*CHC* 1, p. 737). The Han changed its mind twice, and its rivals made their own choices:

| Who | When | Phase | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qin | 221 BCE | Water (black) | Water conquers Zhou's fire |
| Early Han | 206 BCE on | Water, unofficially | Kept Qin's arrangement; proposals to switch to earth in c. 180 and 166 BCE rejected |
| Emperor Wu | 104 BCE | **Earth** (yellow) | Conquest order: earth defeats Qin's water. Announced with a new calendar and reign title in a year of imperial pride |
| Late Former Han | 1st c. BCE | **Fire** (red) | Generation order: Han had "really" been fire all along |
| Wang Mang's Xin | 9 CE | Earth | Generation order: fire (Han) gives birth to earth |
| Gongsun Shu in Sichuan | 25 CE | Metal | Treated Former Han as earth, so metal came next |
| Emperor Guangwu, Later Han | 25 CE | **Fire** | Continuity with Former Han and with Yao, leaving Wang Mang out |

The last choice is the important one. By counting from Former Han straight to himself, Guangwu "was branding the Hsin dynasty as the regime of a usurper, which merited no proper place in the natural sequence" (pp. 738–39). The cycle could thus erase a dynasty from legitimate history. That is the beginning of the later theory of **legitimate succession** (*zhengtong*), which decided which of several rival regimes really held the Mandate. Loewe notes that "some of the most brilliant minds of the Sung period bent their intellects" to this problem (p. 740).

### 7. Wang Mang and the Manufacture of Omens

**Wang Mang** (45 BCE–23 CE) was the nephew of a long-lived empress dowager. He became regent in 8 BCE, put a baby on the throne in 6 CE and made himself "acting emperor". Bielenstein explains that Wang shared his age's belief in the Mandate and the Five Phases. Since many people already felt that Han's fire was exhausted, "all Wang Mang needed to do was persuade the general public by skillful propaganda that the moment of change had come. This was achieved by the fabrication of auspicious omens" (*CHC* 1, p. 230).

From 6 CE, and faster after 8 CE, the throne received a flood of signs: inscribed stones, a stone ox, Heaven's envoy in a dream, a well that opened by itself, and a bronze casket with two inscribed covers. They all said the same thing. A genealogy had already been invented linking Wang to Shun and the **Yellow Emperor**; yellow is the colour of earth, and the ox is earth's animal. "It all added up, as, of course, it was intended to do" (p. 231). On 10 January 9 CE he declared the Han finished and founded the Xin ("New") dynasty.

Bielenstein's key point is about the historians, not Wang Mang:

> Wang Mang's manipulation of the public and the methods later used to support the restoration of the Han dynasty were identical. ... The victory of the Later Han legitimized its earlier propaganda as new state orthodoxy. Fabricated prophecies changed into true messages from Heaven proving the worthiness of the founder. Wang Mang became a usurper. (p. 231)

The Han founder's own descent from Yao was just as invented: such genealogies were "mere propaganda, intended to legitimize new dynasties" (p. 225). Bielenstein also argues that most of Wang Mang's policies simply continued Former Han practice, and that he fell because the Yellow River changed course, not because Heaven turned against him (pp. 233–40). Loewe adds a striking judgement: later dynasties "could not have asserted their right to rule without appealing to the same type of religious and intellectual support that had taken manifest shape during Wang Mang's dispensation" (p. 740).

### 8. Ban Biao: "The Destiny of Kings"

The fullest Han statement of the theory was written right after the civil war that ended Wang Mang. **Ban Biao** (c. 3–54 CE), father of the historian Ban Gu, wrote *Wang-ming lun* ("The Destiny of Kings") to persuade warlords like Gongsun Shu that the house of Liu alone was entitled to rule. Loewe calls it "perhaps the most complete and clear statement of political principles that had yet appeared in Chinese literature" (*CHC* 1, p. 735). Its argument runs:
- Yao, Shun and Yu all received their charge from Heaven, "in response to Heaven and in conformity with the will of the people".
- The line runs unbroken from Yao to the Liu family under the power of **red**.
- The imperial seal is "the property of the mandate" and cannot be won "by the exercise of intelligence or strength". People who think the Han won by luck and force are the ones who become rebels.
- Everyone, emperor or pauper, has an appointed destiny.
- Five signs showed that Gaozu had it: descent from Yao, marks on his body, proven spiritual and martial qualities, generosity, and judgement of men.

It ends with a warning to readers not to aspire to positions that are not theirs (pp. 736–37). The argument is circular: whoever wins proves that Heaven chose him, and whoever tries and loses proves he lacked the destiny.

### 9. The Yellow Heaven

By the second century CE the cycle was turned against the Han. Rebels proclaimed themselves "Yellow Emperors" in 145 and 148, and a "Black Emperor" in 145. Mansvelt Beck reads these titles as announcements that the reign of fire was over and earth or water had begun (*CHC* 1, p. 337).

The **Yellow Turbans** of 184 were the most explicit. Their slogan was: "The green Heaven is already dead, the yellow Heaven will take its place." In 192 they wrote to Cao Cao: "The element of Han is already exhausted, a yellow house will be established" (p. 368). Their leader Zhang Jue called his movement the "Way of Great Peace" and timed the new age for 184, the start of a new sixty-year cycle (*CHC* 2, ch. 25). Fukui and Demiéville read the slogan as a religious hope, not only a political one. In 1978 archaeologists found the same hope for the death of Blue Heaven scratched by labourers building the Cao family's tombs in 170 (*CHC* 1, p. 876).

The Yellow Turbans had vanished by 207, but Mansvelt Beck thinks their "indirect role was perhaps more important": they had spread the conviction that the Han's time was up (p. 368).

### 10. Abdication as Ritual: Cao Pi and Sima Yan

The Han ended not in battle but with an abdication. Cao Cao made himself Duke of Wei in 213 and King of Wei in 216 and married his daughters into the palace, but he presented himself as a loyal regent. His son **Cao Pi** received the abdication of the last Han emperor on 11 December 220. According to de Crespigny, the transfer "was accompanied by memorials and petitions urging the new ruler to accept the Mandate of Heaven, and justifying the imperial claim with omens and prophecies". The old theory that Han's fire would yield to "the Yellow of Earth" was ready to hand (*CHC* 2, ch. 1). One of the abdication documents states the logic of the system plainly:

> The mandate of Heaven brooks no refusal and the holy instrument of office may not be abandoned for long. The servants of state may not be left without a master. (*Sanguozhi* 2, trans. Loewe, *CHC* 1, p. 737)

The forms had been worked out under the Han. Loewe describes how new emperors made "solemn protestations of their inability" to rule, often three times, before bowing to the officials' wishes (*CHC* 1, p. 741).

When the Sima family replaced the Cao in 266, the procedure was even more formal. Chaussende describes three stages: an imperial conferral (*ce*) from the last Wei emperor, equal to abdication; a sacrifice in which **Sima Yan** announced to Shangdi, the supreme deity, that he accepted the mandate; and a decree at the ancestral temple that gave the old emperor a fief and announced a new reign title. The last Wei ruler played Yao and Sima Yan played Shun, "protesting aloud that he was accepting the title in spite of himself" (*CHC* 2, ch. 4). Mencius had said that occupying Yao's palace would be usurpation. These rituals were designed to make it look like Heaven's gift.

The result was a precedent with dangerous consequences. Chittick explains that ritual abdication, "firmly established by the Wei and Jin dynasties", allowed a non-relative to found a new dynasty if he could show the old one had "lost the Mandate". All he needed was control of a child emperor, the elimination of imperial relatives and enough troops: "almost any man could engineer a legitimate seizure of the throne" (*CHC* 2, ch. 11). The southern dynasties Song, Qi, Liang and Chen were all founded this way.

### 11. Many Heavens in a Divided China

After 220 there were usually several emperors at once, and each needed a claim.
- **Three claimants.** Liu Bei's Shu-Han claimed the legitimate Han succession by blood; Sun Quan took the imperial title in 229 (*CHC* 2, ch. 1). Later historians argued for centuries about which of the three really held the Mandate.
- **A Xiongnu "Han".** In 304 **Liu Yuan**, a Xiongnu noble who had studied with a Confucian teacher and bore the Han imperial surname, rebelled against the Jin. "Claiming that the Mandate of Heaven was based on merit alone, and that the legendary founders of the ancient ('Chinese') Xia and Zhou dynasties had also sprung from non-Chinese peoples", he named his state "Han" (*CHC* 2, ch. 6). The Mencian argument from virtue was being used by a steppe chieftain.
- **Two Heavens for the Northern Wei.** Pearce shows that the Tuoba Xianbei worshipped both the Inner Asian sky god **Tengri** and the Chinese **Shangdi**. Emperor Daowu sacrificed to Tengri at a western altar, took the title *huangdi* in 398 and chose the reign name *Tianxing*, "Heaven Prevails", which could also be read as "Tengri Prevails". He then sacrificed to Shangdi in the southern suburb in Chinese style. Shamanesses beating drums took part in the Tengri rites, which were held before campaigns until Emperor Xiaowen moved the capital to Luoyang in 494. Emperor Taiwu even adopted a Daoist title in 440, "Perfect Ruler of Great Peace" (*CHC* 2, ch. 8).
- **Titles abroad.** The founder of the southern Song dynasty, "in his haste to create an image of his own legitimacy", gave titles to the rulers of Paekche, Koguryŏ and Japan within a month of usurping the throne in 420 (*CHC* 2, ch. 13).

Knapp adds that the ritual centre of the Mandate in this period was the **suburban sacrifice** (*jiaosi*) to "August Heaven the Emperor on High" on a round altar south of the capital, matched by a square altar to Earth in the north. Performing it "indicated the dynasty's receipt of the Heavenly Mandate", and nearly every founder did it in person. But "no Six Dynasties emperor felt worthy enough" to perform the **feng and shan** at Mount Tai, the sacrifice that announced complete success (*CHC* 2, ch. 22).

### 12. Sui and Tang: Ceremony and the Holy Mountain

The Sui founder **Yang Jian** came to power by a coup in 581. He took the imperial insignia and robes, held his first dawn audience, declared an amnesty and proclaimed a reign title: "With these and other ceremonial acts of great antiquity, he let it be known that he now legitimately held the Mandate of Heaven." By the end of the next summer all fifty-nine Northern Zhou princes had been murdered (Wright, *CHC* 3, p. 60). A Confucian scholar, Li Delin, was kept on only as long as he was needed "to provide the articles of legitimation for the usurping Sui" (p. 72). Yang Jian was touchy about precedents: when a minister seemed to hint at a comparison, he swore an oath and demanded, "Would you make of me a Wang Mang?" (p. 62).

Once the empire was reunified, the feng and shan came back. Wechsler describes them as "a symbolic announcement to heaven and earth that the emperor's tasks on earth had been brought to a successful conclusion". They had been held only six times in all Chinese history, the last in 56 CE, because emperors feared that holding them without good grounds would be hubris (*CHC* 3, p. 258). Taizong planned them three times:
- In 632 Wei Zheng talked him out of it.
- In 641 a comet stopped him at Luoyang.
- In 648 a flood cancelled them.

His son **Gaozong** finally performed them on New Year's Day 666, after scholars had reconstructed the forgotten ritual. Empress Wu, "defying all tradition", led a second procession of imperial women up the mountain to show she was the emperor's equal partner (pp. 258–59). **Xuanzong** repeated the ceremony in 725 with Turkish and other foreign envoys in his train; the cortège stretched for miles and laid waste the countryside wherever it stopped (p. 387).

### 13. Empress Wu: A Mandate for a Woman

The hardest case for the theory was a woman claiming the throne in her own name. **Empress Wu** built her claim from every available source (Guisso, *CHC* 3, ch. 6):
- **A dynastic name.** She called her dynasty **Zhou**, after the most admired dynasty of antiquity. The Li family had claimed Laozi as their ancestor; she took the Duke of Zhou's family as hers. Guisso notes that "ambitious rulers before her, and not only usurpers like Wang Mang, had used the same tactic" (p. 304).
- **An omen.** In 688 a white stone was found in the Luo River with the prophecy "A Sage Mother shall come to Rule Mankind; and her Imperium shall bring Eternal Prosperity". All sources agree her nephew Wu Chengsi fabricated it. She declared the river sacred and took the title "Sage Mother, Sovereign Divine" (pp. 302–3).
- **New characters.** She issued twelve new forms of common characters. The one for her own name showed the heavenly bodies shining over the void, a symbol that "would make all men come [to submit] to Us" (pp. 304–5).
- **A Buddhist prophecy.** For the mass of the people, the answer was Buddhism. The *Great Cloud Sutra* foretold that Maitreya would be reborn as a female ruler of the world: "At that time all her subjects will give their allegiance to this woman as the successor to the imperial throne." Her monk-lover Xue Huaiyi and his colleagues wrote a commentary identifying her as that woman. She founded a Great Cloud temple in every prefecture and added "Maitreya the Peerless" to her title (pp. 305–6).

In 690 she refused three petitions, one with more than sixty thousand names, until a phoenix was seen above the palace and vermilion birds fluttered in the throne room. Then "heaven could no longer be denied", and she became "Holy and Divine Emperor" (p. 306). Even the son she had kept from the throne, restoring the Tang in 705, declared that she had "responded to the needs of the times" and that nothing had really changed (p. 306). This is the abdication ritual in its most complete form, with a new religion added to the old omens.

### 14. Song: Fire Again, and the Last Feng and Shan

After the Tang collapsed, five dynasties followed each other in the north in fifty-three years. The Song began in exactly the same way. In 960 **Zhao Kuangyin**'s troops proclaimed him Son of Heaven at the Chen Bridge relay station, "following an established procedure for usurpation". He refused three times, "part of the ritual", and the seven-year-old Later Zhou emperor abdicated (Kuhn, p. 30). Legend gave him the right birth: "a rosy glow filled the chamber and purple mist wafted to the rooftops" (p. 31). The *Song History* later summed up his reign in the old vocabulary: "When the Cultivated [and Virtuous] Progenitor changed the mandate, he first employed civil officials and took power away from the military officials" (p. 31).

His brother **Taizong**, whose own succession was suspicious ("the sound of the axe in the shadow of the flickering candle"), was backed by a Daoist prophecy that amounted to "a divine justification" (p. 36). In 984 he settled the dynasty's cosmic position. He acknowledged the Five Dynasties as a proper historical period and "adopted the symbolic element of fire and the corresponding color red for the Song, thus legitimizing the dynasty in the cosmic order" (p. 36). So the Song took the same phase as the Han, a thousand years after Guangwu.

The last great performance of the old theory followed a diplomatic humiliation. The **Treaty of Shanyuan** (1005) made the Song pay silk and silver to the Liao and treat its emperor as a "brother". Kuhn writes that the idea of the Chinese emperor as "the only ruler under Heaven was in danger of becoming mere rhetoric" (p. 46). **Zhenzong** asked Wang Qinruo "how best to wash away the shame of the Shanyuan agreement", and the answer was Heaven-sent letters, following a Tang precedent. They were "discovered" in the palace in 1008, and the reign was renamed "Great Centrality and Auspicious Talisman" (p. 47). That November, petitioned by 24,375 officials, clergy, foreign envoys and elders, Zhenzong performed the **feng and shan** on Mount Tai. Kuhn notes they were "the last of this type in Chinese history" (p. 47).

Hymes shows how far the affair reached. A Temple of Heavenly Felicity was built in every prefecture. In 1012 the dynasty's "Holy Ancestor" appeared to Zhenzong as an avatar of the Yellow Emperor, which gave the Song "a Taoist genealogy even higher than T'ang emperors' claimed descent from Lao-tzu" and "perhaps asserted legitimacy" at a moment when Shanyuan seemed to weaken the claim to rule all China. The revelations came through a spirit medium from Fujian. Hymes adds that "there is no certainty of bad faith for any participant" (*CHC* 5.2, pp. 604–5).

### 15. The Song Doubters

At the same time, the leading Song scholars were dismantling the cosmology on which all this rested.
- **Ouyang Xiu** argued that "The ancient Classic of Confucius has been lost" and that Confucius had nothing to do with the cosmological speculation in the *Book of Changes*. The sages, he held, were guided by human needs, "rather than by an effort to fit themselves to the patterns of Heaven-and-Earth" (Bol, *CHC* 5.2, p. 677).
- **Sima Guang**, in the *Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government* (covering 403 BCE–959 CE, submitted 1084), "rejected traditional efforts to define a succession of dynasties as legitimate possessors of Heaven's mandate in accord with the Five Phases, and instead held that dynasties were the result of struggles for power" (Bol, p. 690). What he looked for instead was a constant set of causes of order and disorder: "The way of order and disorder is threaded by consistency through past and present" (p. 690). Chaffee sums it up: Sima Guang "swept away much of the Han cosmological framework that had informed earlier histories" (*CHC* 5.2, p. 11).
- Sima Guang also moved Heaven's voice from omens to officials. In 1065 he argued that since humans and Heaven share the same nature, "the will of the majority must represent what Heaven wants". The emperor, who is inferior to Heaven, therefore had to listen to the consensus of his top officials (Hartman, *CHC* 5.2, p. 38). This is Mencius's "Heaven sees as my people see", narrowed to the literati.
- The Neo-Confucians turned Heaven inward. For Hu Hong, a mind that is preserved and extended "could be extended to become one with *t'ien*", and the goal of self-cultivation was oneness with Heaven, not reading portents (Tillman, *CHC* 5.2, pp. 736–37).

So the Song did two contradictory things at once. The court adopted fire, staged the Heavenly Letters and climbed Mount Tai, while its best historians and philosophers argued that the Five Phases had nothing to do with legitimacy.

### 16. Assessment: Belief or Propaganda?

It is tempting to read the whole history as cynical propaganda. Fabricated omens appear in every period: Wang Mang's stone ox, Wu Chengsi's white stone, the Heavenly Letters of 1008. Bielenstein's point that the winning side's fabrications became "true messages from Heaven" is hard to answer.

The sources suggest something more complicated:
- **People believed in the cycle.** The Yellow Turbans died for "Yellow Heaven", and tomb labourers scratched the same hope into bricks. Bielenstein thinks Wang Mang and the Later Han founders, "being rational and superstitious at the same time, ... eventually came to believe their own propaganda" (*CHC* 1, p. 231).
- **The Mandate was also a check on rulers.** Omens let officials criticize the throne. Mencius's "mere fellow" passage could be turned against any tyrant, and Sima Guang turned Heaven's will into the majority opinion of the bureaucracy.
- **The rituals mattered because they were hard to fake.** Taizong never climbed Mount Tai, though he wanted to three times. The Six Dynasties emperors did not dare to at all. The feng and shan were held only when a ruler could plausibly claim his work was complete.

Loewe's conclusion explains why the theory lasted. By the end of the Han the emperor had changed from "a successful conqueror who had established his regime by force" into "an instrument of permanence", whose accession and death were stages in the cycle of the Five Phases (*CHC* 1, p. 743). The emperor might be a child or a puppet, but the formal authority of his position had to come from Heaven, or nobody's government was anything more than "the exploitation of the expedient". That is why every usurper from Cao Pi to Zhao Kuangyin went through the same elaborate refusal three times before accepting.

### Conclusion

The Zhou left the Mandate as a moral idea: Heaven backs virtue and abandons wickedness, and it shows its choice through the people. Zou Yan added a machine, the cycle of five phases, which told each dynasty its colour, its number and even its legal style. Qin used the machine without the morality, ruling by water and boasting of conquest. The Han put the two together. Dong Zhongshu explained omens as cosmic resonance, the court changed its patron element twice, Wang Mang manufactured omens, Ban Biao made the Mandate the only legitimate title to rule, and Guangwu used the cycle to write Wang Mang out of history. From 220 the Mandate was transferred by ritual abdication, which made usurpation respectable. The divided centuries multiplied claimants and even Heavens, from Liu Yuan's Xiongnu "Han" to the Tuoba's Tengri. The Sui and Tang brought back the feng and shan, and Empress Wu added a Buddhist prophecy to the old omens. The Song went back to fire and held the last feng and shan in 1008, just as Ouyang Xiu and Sima Guang were concluding that dynasties rise by power, not by the turning of the phases.
