The is-ought distinction refers to the idea that one cannot logically derive what ought to be (prescriptive statements about what is morally right or wrong, desirable, or recommended) from what is (descriptive statements about the way things actually are). This concept was famously introduced by the philosopher David Hume in his *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1738), where he pointed out that many moral philosophers make a leap from describing the world (what is) to prescribing what should be done (what ought to be), without providing a proper logical justification for such a move.

## Key points of the is-ought distinction

1. **Descriptive vs. Normative:** Statements about what is describe facts or states of affairs (e.g., "The sky is blue," "Humans are mortal"). What ought refers to normative statements that prescribe actions, behavior, or values (e.g., "People ought to be kind," "One should not lie").
2. **The Problem of Inference:** According to Hume, there is no direct logical bridge between descriptive facts and normative conclusions. Just because something is the case, does not mean it ought to be the case. For example, the fact that "humans sometimes lie" does not automatically imply that "humans ought to lie" or that "lying is acceptable."
3. **The Challenge:** This distinction poses a challenge for moral philosophy, as it asks for an explanation of how we transition from a description of the world to an ethical conclusion. It raises the question of how we justify moral principles or commands if they are not based on objective facts but on subjective reasoning or external norms.
4. **Responses:** Some philosophers attempt to bridge the gap between is and ought. For instance, natural law theorists argue that moral norms can be derived from human nature or the natural world. Immanuel Kant, on the other hand, argues that the "ought" can be derived from reason and the concept of duty, not simply from empirical facts.

In summary, the is-ought distinction emphasizes the difficulty, if not the impossibility, of logically moving from a description of the world to a prescription of how things should be, and it remains a critical concept in discussions of ethics and moral philosophy.

## Hume's Actual Words (*Treatise* 3.1.1)

> "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning... when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is however, of the last consequence."

Hume asks only that "a reason should be given" for how *ought* is deduced from *is*. Whether he meant it can **never** be done is debated (MacIntyre argues he didn't: Hume himself derives obligations from what is useful and agreeable).

## Moore's Naturalistic Fallacy (1903)

- G. E. Moore: **good** can't be defined by any natural property (pleasure, survival, what we desire).
- **Open question argument**: for any natural property N, "this is N, but is it good?" is always an open, meaningful question. If "good" meant N, it would be like asking "this is N, but is it N?"
- So good is a simple, non-natural property, known by intuition.

## Replies

**MacIntyre: functional concepts** (*A Short History of Ethics*; later *After Virtue*)
- From "this watch is grossly inaccurate and irregular" it **does** follow that "this is a bad watch". Because "watch" is a **functional** concept: a watch is for telling time.
- Likewise "farmer", "sea captain", "father": defined by roles and purposes, so facts about how one performs entail evaluations.
- The is-ought gap appears only when **man** is no longer understood as having a function or end (*telos*). That happened when modern philosophy dropped Aristotelian teleology. The gap is a symptom, not a timeless truth.

**Searle's promising** (1964)
1. Jones uttered the words "I hereby promise to pay you five dollars."
2. So Jones promised to pay Smith five dollars.
3. So Jones placed himself under an obligation.
4. So Jones ought to pay Smith five dollars.

"Institutional facts" (promises, contracts) bridge is and ought.

**Philippa Foot, *Natural Goodness***
- "Owls see in the dark"; an owl that can't is **defective**. "Bees have stings"; a bee without one is a bad bee. Natural norms for living things.
- Human virtues are the same kind of thing: what humans need to flourish as the kind of creature they are. A human without courage or justice is defective in the same logical sense.

**Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity***
- Asks the "normative question": not what morality says but why it **binds** us.
- Reviews four answers: **voluntarism** (God's or a sovereign's command: Hobbes, Pufendorf), **realism** (moral facts: Clarke, Price, Moore), **reflective endorsement** (Hume: we approve on reflection), and her own **Kantian** answer: normativity comes from the structure of rational agency itself: we need reasons to act, so we must legislate laws for ourselves.
- She accepts that is doesn't give ought **from outside**; ought comes from the agent's own reflective self-legislation.

## The Classical / Theistic Answer

*Mine.*

- If things have **natures** with ends (final causes), then facts about what something is already contain what it should be. The gap only exists if nature is purposeless matter. See [[Edward Feser: The Last Superstition]], [[Thomistic Metaphysics]].
- For theism, the ultimate "is" is God, who **is** the Good. So the deepest fact is also the deepest norm. See [[The Euthyphro Dilemma and the Orthodox Answer]].
- Orthodoxy: man's nature has a **logos** and a goal (theosis). "Ought" is "become what you are": the image called to likeness. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
