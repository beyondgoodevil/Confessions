The is-ought distinction refers to the idea that one cannot logically derive what ought to be (prescriptive statements about what is morally right or wrong, desirable, or recommended) from what is (descriptive statements about the way things actually are). This concept was famously introduced by the philosopher David Hume in his *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1738), where he pointed out that many moral philosophers make a leap from describing the world (what is) to prescribing what should be done (what ought to be), without providing a proper logical justification for such a move.

## Key points of the is-ought distinction

1. **Descriptive vs. Normative:** Statements about what is describe facts or states of affairs (e.g., "The sky is blue," "Humans are mortal"). What ought refers to normative statements that prescribe actions, behavior, or values (e.g., "People ought to be kind," "One should not lie").
2. **The Problem of Inference:** According to Hume, there is no direct logical bridge between descriptive facts and normative conclusions. Just because something is the case, does not mean it ought to be the case. For example, the fact that "humans sometimes lie" does not automatically imply that "humans ought to lie" or that "lying is acceptable."
    - Hume's actual words (*Treatise* 3.1.1):

        > "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning... when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is however, of the last consequence."

    - Hume asks only that "a reason should be given" for how *ought* is deduced from *is*. Whether he meant it can **never** be done is debated (MacIntyre argues he didn't: Hume himself derives obligations from what is useful and agreeable).
        - MacIntyre on what Hume was aiming at: popular devotional books like *The Whole Duty of Man* (probably by Allestree), which argued from facts about God ("God hath given me abilities... for the advantage of others") straight to duties.
        - MacIntyre's own reading: "ought" was originally always **backed by a reason**: "you ought to, if you want X", or "you ought to, because you are a chief, tutor or night watchman". As shared ideals and roles faded in the age of individualism, there appeared a bare "you ought" with no backing; asked "why?", the only answer is "you just ought". *That* ought can't be deduced from any is, and "perhaps it is of this ought that Hume is speaking".
        - Hume's own system gets from is to ought through **utility** and **sympathy**: justice is an "artificial virtue" we keep because without rules there'd be no stable property, and we feel how much we're harmed when others break them. But MacIntyre adds that "Hume is a notoriously inconsistent author".
3. **The Challenge:** This distinction poses a challenge for moral philosophy, as it asks for an explanation of how we transition from a description of the world to an ethical conclusion. It raises the question of how we justify moral principles or commands if they are not based on objective facts but on subjective reasoning or external norms.
    - **G. E. Moore's naturalistic fallacy** (1903): **good** can't be defined by any natural property (pleasure, survival, what we desire).
    - **Open question argument** (Moore): for any natural property N, "this is N, but is it good?" is always an open, meaningful question. If "good" meant N, it would be like asking "this is N, but is it N?"
    - So good is a simple, non-natural property, known by intuition.
    - MacIntyre's two objections to Moore: (1) we use a simple-property word like "yellow" by learning standard examples, but "how could having learned to recognize a good friend help us to recognize a good watch?"; (2) Moore leaves it inexplicable why something's being good "should ever furnish us with a reason for action". And the naturalistic fallacy isn't the same as the is–ought thesis, though the second follows from the first: for Moore, "good" can't mean "commanded by God" any more than "pleasant".
    - The **intuitionists** (Prichard, Ross, Carritt): Prichard's "Does Moral Philosophy Rest upon a Mistake?" (1912) says asking for a justification of duty is misconceived; we apprehend duty immediately. MacIntyre: on their own view they're only "telling us... what we all know already".
    - **Emotivism**: Ayer sorts judgments into logical, factual and emotive. Moral sentences "are purely expressions of feeling", unverifiable like "a cry of pain or a word of command" (quoted by Adler). Russell: "Ethics is the art of recommending to others what they must do to get along with ourselves."
    - **Prescriptivism** (Hare): "ought" sentences entail imperatives, and no evaluative conclusion follows from premises without at least one evaluative premise. So "I ought to help this man because he is starving" hides the premise "I ought to help the starving", and in the end every chain stops at a principle I simply choose. Hare later used **universalizability** as a weapon: would you accept the rule if it were applied to you?
    - Korsgaard (*The Sources of Normativity*) asks the "normative question": not what morality says but why it **binds** us.
4. **Responses:** Some philosophers attempt to bridge the gap between is and ought. For instance, natural law theorists argue that moral norms can be derived from human nature or the natural world. Immanuel Kant, on the other hand, argues that the "ought" can be derived from reason and the concept of duty, not simply from empirical facts.

In summary, the is-ought distinction emphasizes the difficulty, if not the impossibility, of logically moving from a description of the world to a prescription of how things should be, and it remains a critical concept in discussions of ethics and moral philosophy.

## Replies

**From function and nature** (the natural-law line)
- **Functional concepts** (MacIntyre, *After Virtue*): from "this watch is grossly inaccurate and irregular" it **does** follow that "this is a bad watch". Because "watch" is a **functional** concept: a watch is for telling time.
- Likewise "farmer", "sea captain", "father": defined by roles and purposes, so facts about how one performs entail evaluations. The *Short History* already tells the story behind this: Greek and early English morality judged a man "as farmer, as king, as father"; then evaluation came loose from roles and people started asking what makes "a good man" as such, or one's duty "as a man". Jane Austen's naval officers are a touchstone of duty because for them "the link between duty and duties has not been broken".
- The is-ought gap appears only when **man** is no longer understood as having a function or end (*telos*). That happened when modern philosophy dropped Aristotelian teleology. The gap is a symptom, not a timeless truth (MacIntyre).
- **Natural goodness** (Philippa Foot, *Natural Goodness*): "Owls see in the dark"; an owl that can't is **defective**. "Bees have stings"; a bee without one is a bad bee. Natural norms for living things.
- Human virtues are the same kind of thing: what humans need to flourish as the kind of creature they are. A human without courage or justice is defective in the same logical sense (Foot).

**From institutional facts** (Searle's promising, 1964)
1. Jones uttered the words "I hereby promise to pay you five dollars."
2. So Jones promised to pay Smith five dollars.
3. So Jones placed himself under an obligation.
4. So Jones ought to pay Smith five dollars.

"Institutional facts" (promises, contracts) bridge is and ought.

**From right desire** (Adler, *Ten Philosophical Mistakes* ch. 5)
- Adler grants Hume's point: no set of purely descriptive premises, however complete, yields a single prescriptive conclusion. The way out is a **prescriptive first premise** that is **self-evident**.
- Truth of a different kind: Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics* VI) says practical judgments are true by conformity with **right desire**, not with the way things are. Ayer is right that "ought" statements don't correspond to facts, but wrong to conclude that the only kind of truth is correspondence.
- **Needs vs wants**: natural desires are the same in all humans and always for real goods; acquired wants may be for merely apparent goods.
- First principle: "**We ought to desire whatever is really good for us and nothing else.**" You can't think the opposite.
- The syllogism: we ought to desire what is really good for us (prescriptive, self-evident); humans by nature need knowledge, so knowledge is really good for us (descriptive); so we ought to seek knowledge.
- Against Kant: the categorical imperative isn't self-evident, and it "boils down to the golden rule", which leaves the real question unanswered: what *ought* one to wish others to do to oneself? That needs facts about human nature, which Kant excluded. And saying the only good is a good will "flies in the face of the facts".

**From reason** (the Kantian line: Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity*)
- Korsgaard reviews four answers to the normative question: **voluntarism** (God's or a sovereign's command: Hobbes, Pufendorf), **realism** (moral facts: Clarke, Price, Moore), **reflective endorsement** (Hume: we approve on reflection), and her own **Kantian** answer: normativity comes from the structure of rational agency itself: we need reasons to act, so we must legislate laws for ourselves.
- She accepts that is doesn't give ought **from outside**; ought comes from the agent's own reflective self-legislation.
- The **normative question** is first-personal. An explanation of why people act morally isn't a justification to the agent asking "why should I?" "If I claim that you ought to face death rather than do a certain wrong action, I had better be prepared to back that claim up."
- Hume's **reflective endorsement**: "we do not disapprove the action because it is vicious; instead, it is vicious because we disapprove it." Korsgaard thinks this is a real test: morality is justified if we still endorse it once we know the truth about where it comes from. But it isn't enough on its own.
- Her answer: normativity arises from the **reflective structure** of human consciousness. Once we can step back from our impulses we need reasons, and "reasons exist because we need them". We act from **practical identities** (parent, citizen, Quaker), and valuing any of them commits us to valuing our **humanity** as such, which is moral identity. "The obligatory is like the visible: it depends on how much of the light of reflection is on."
- The commentators push back: Cohen's Mafioso who reflectively endorses a code without "do unto others"; Geuss asking whether a Kantian can give a reason to reject someone like Schlegel; Williams doubting that the first person should have the last word.

## The Classical / Theistic Answer

*Mine.*

- If things have **natures** with ends (final causes), then facts about what something is already contain what it should be. The gap only exists if nature is purposeless matter. See [[The Last Superstition]], [[Thomistic Metaphysics]].
- For theism, the ultimate "is" is God, who **is** the Good. So the deepest fact is also the deepest norm. See [[The Euthyphro Dilemma and the Orthodox Answer]].
- Orthodoxy: man's nature has a **logos** and a goal (theosis). "Ought" is "become what you are": the image called to likeness. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
