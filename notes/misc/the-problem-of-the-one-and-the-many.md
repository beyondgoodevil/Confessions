Reality looks like both: one world, many things. One human nature, many humans. Which is basic? If unity is ultimate, how is there real difference? If plurality is ultimate, what holds anything together? The claim in presuppositional apologetics is that every non-Christian system ends up sacrificing one to the other, and only a God who is Himself one and many doesn't have to.

## 1. The Problem

Not one question but a family:

| Area | The "one" | The "many" | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Being | reality as a whole | individual things | is the world really one thing or really lots? |
| Universals | humanity | Peter, Paul, John | what do the many share? |
| Change | what stays the same | the successive states | is it one thing through time? |
| Knowledge | concepts, laws | particular facts | how does a general law apply to cases it has never met? |
| Society | the state, the community | individuals | which exists for which? |

Two ways to fail:

- **All one** (monism): differences are appearance. Individuals, change, evil, freedom all become illusion.
- **All many** (pluralism, atomism, nominalism): only particulars. Then there are no real kinds, no laws, nothing common, and knowledge is a heap of unrelated facts.

What's wanted: unity and plurality **equally ultimate**, neither derived from the other.

## 2. The Greeks

**Parmenides: all is one.**

- What is, is. What is not can't be thought or spoken of.
- Difference would need "is not" (A is *not* B). So no difference. No change either.
- Being is one, ungenerated, motionless. The many are "the way of opinion".
- Zeno's paradoxes are there to defend this.

**Heraclitus: all is many (flux).**

- "You cannot step into the same river twice."
- War is father of all. Strife of opposites.
- But there's a *logos* running through the change, so he isn't purely on the "many" side. See [[Nietzsche and the Greeks]] sec. 7, and [[Logic Before Aristotle]] sec. 4 for the hostile reading.

**The atomists** (Democritus): many little Parmenidean ones in the void. Pluralism with the unity pushed down into each atom.

**Plato.**

- The Forms give the one over the many: many beautiful things, one Beauty.
- Problems he raises himself in the *Parmenides*:
    - how does one Form get to be "in" many things? (whole in each, or a part in each?)
    - the **Third Man**: if the many men and the Form Man are alike, is there a further Form over them?
- And the Forms are themselves many. So what unifies *them*? The Good, "beyond being". See [[The Good in Plato]].
- Late Plato (*Sophist*, *Philebus*) says being has to include both sameness and difference, limit and unlimited. Closest the Greeks come to "both ultimate", I think.

**Aristotle.**

- Brings the form down into the thing: this man = form + matter.
- Form is the one (shared by the species), matter individuates the many.
- Cost: what makes Socrates *Socrates* is his matter, which is the unintelligible bit. So the individual as such isn't knowable. "Science is of the universal."
- At the top, pure form with no matter, thinking itself. See [[The Unmoved Mover]].

**Plotinus.**

- The **One**, absolutely simple, beyond being and thought. No distinction of any kind in it.
- Everything else flows out by **emanation**: Intellect, Soul, matter. Each step down is more plural and less real.
- So multiplicity = a falling away. The many are a lesser thing, and salvation is return to the One, "flight of the alone to the Alone".
- See [[Emanationism]], [[Neoplatonic Idea of Divine Simplicity]].

Pattern: each time, one of the two is made primary and the other has to be explained as derived, lesser, or unreal.

## 3. After the Greeks

- **Realism vs nominalism** is the same fight about universals. See [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]].
- **Spinoza**: one substance. Things are modes of it.
- **Leibniz**: infinitely many monads with no windows, coordinated from outside by pre-established harmony.
- **Hume**: only separate impressions. No self, no necessary connection. See [[Critique of Empiricism]].
- **Kant**: the mind supplies the unity (categories) to the manifold of sense. So unity is ours, not the world's.
- **Hegel**: the Absolute realises itself through its differences. Arguably monism again with a story attached.
- **Eastern religions**: Advaita Hinduism, Brahman alone is real and plurality is *maya*. Opposite pole, polytheism: many gods and no final unity.
- **Islam and unitarian monotheism**: God is a bare unity (*tawhid*). Then plurality has no ground in God. The apologetic claim is that this ends up like the One of Plotinus: either the many are unexplained, or God needs the world in order to have anything to know or love.

## 4. Outside Metaphysics

Rushdoony's book *The One and the Many* is mostly about this.

- **Politics.** One over many = totalitarianism, the individual exists for the state. Many over one = anarchy, or atomised individualism. He argues Western liberty depended on Trinitarian (and Chalcedonian) theology for refusing both.
- **Church.** Papal monarchy puts the one over the many. Protestant individualism puts the many over the one. Conciliarity, bishops in communion, is supposed to be both. See [[Nicene Creed Canon 6: First Among Equals Explanation]].
- **Marriage, family.** "The two shall be one flesh" without ceasing to be two.
- **Knowledge.** Science needs both: real individuals to observe, real laws that hold across them. Pure empiricism gets facts with no law. Pure rationalism gets law with no facts. See [[Transcendental Categories Are Required for Science and Knowledge]].

## 5. The Trinitarian Answer

Van Til's phrase: in God, unity and plurality are **equally ultimate**.

- One essence, three hypostases. The oneness isn't prior to the threeness, the threeness isn't prior to the oneness.
- St. Gregory the Theologian (Oration 40): "No sooner do I conceive of the One than I am illumined by the splendour of the Three; no sooner do I distinguish them than I am carried back to the One."
- So in the source of everything, neither unity nor difference is a defect or a derivation. Then a created world that is one-and-many is what you'd expect: it reflects its maker.
- The created one-and-many isn't the same as the uncreated one-and-many (Van Til's "eternal" and "temporal" one and many). It depends on it.

**The Orthodox version is more specific than Van Til's**, and I think stronger:

1. **Where the unity comes from.** Not from an abstract essence standing behind the Persons but from a Person: the Father, source of the Son and the Spirit (**monarchy**). Unity is personal from the start. See [[Monarchical Trinitarianism]].
2. **Perichoresis.** The Persons indwell one another without merging. Maximum unity with no loss of distinction. See [[St. Cyril of Alexandria on Perichoresis]].
3. **Essence and energies.** God is one in essence and *really many* in His energies (goodness, wisdom, life...) without being divided. So multiplicity in God isn't only the three Persons. See [[Essence-Energies Distinction]].
4. **Logos and logoi.** St. Maximus: "the one Logos is many logoi and the many logoi are one Logos." The plurality of creatures has its ground in God, as willed, and creatures are many *by intention*. Multiplicity isn't a fall (against Origen and Plotinus). See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
5. **Christ.** One hypostasis, two natures, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation". Chalcedon is a one-and-many formula. So is the Church as one body with many members (1 Cor 12).
6. **Nature and hypostasis** generally. One human nature, many human persons: the pattern in us is an image of the pattern in God. See [[Nature and Person Distinction in Christology]].

**Against absolute divine simplicity.** If God is so simple that essence = attributes = will = act, then God is a Plotinian One with a Trinity added on, and the many again have no real ground in Him. That's the Orthodox complaint about Thomism: it lands on the "one" side. See [[Thomism]], [[God as Pure Act: RC vs EO]], [[Thomism Leads to Eternal Creation]].

## 6. The One-and-Many Proof of God: Feser's Neo-Platonic Argument

Added from Feser, *Five Proofs of the Existence of God*, ch. 2. This is the Plotinus of sec. 2 turned into a proof. Worth having here because it argues from the many to the One and lands exactly where sec. 5 says not to.

**The argument**

1. Things we meet are **composite**: made of parts.
2. A composite exists at any moment only because its parts are combined at that moment.
3. It can't be the cause of its own parts being combined (circular: the whole depends on the parts being together).
4. So every composite has a cause of its existence *right now*.
5. If that cause is composite, it needs one too. This is a hierarchical series (everything acting at once), so it must end.
6. It can only end in something **absolutely simple**, with no parts of any kind.

"Parts" is meant widely. Not only physical bits:

- matter and form
- **essence and existence**
- substance and attributes
- act and potency

**Then the attributes**

- **One**: two simple things would need a feature to tell them apart, and having a feature besides what you are = having parts.
- **Immutable**, **eternal**, **uncaused**: each would need parts.
- **A mind**: everything is a mind, a mental content, a material thing or an abstract object. Abstract objects cause nothing, matter has parts, contents need a mind. So it's a mind.
- **Purely actual**: act + potency would be two parts. From there the rest follows as in the Aristotelian proof. See [[The Unmoved Mover]] sec. 6.

**Objections he takes**

- *"What caused God?"* Only composites need causes. A simple thing couldn't have one.
- *Why can't one of the parts unify the others?* Then what unifies *that* part with the rest? Regress. (Vallicella. Same shape as the instantiation regress in [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]] sec. 6.)
- *Why can't a thing's unity be a brute fact?* That isn't an alternative explanation. It's declining to give one.
- Like the Aristotelian proof, nothing here depends on the universe having a beginning.

**Why it matters for this note**

- It's a direct argument that the many presuppose a One. So it supports half of sec. 5 (bare plurality can't be ultimate).
- But its conclusion is a One with **no distinctions whatsoever**. Feser means that: for him this is where absolute divine simplicity comes from.
- Then the uniqueness step ("no differentiating feature without parts") looks like it rules out three Persons, and many energies, as much as it rules out two gods. The Thomist answer is that the Persons are distinguished only by relations of opposition, which aren't "parts". Whether that's enough is the old argument. See [[Neoplatonic Idea of Divine Simplicity]], [[EO vs RC Trinity]].
- The Orthodox move would be to question premise 6's wide sense of "part". A real distinction isn't automatically a *composition*. Essence and energies are really distinct and God isn't assembled out of them, any more than the sun is assembled out of itself and its rays. Three hypostases aren't three parts of God. So you can have: not composite, hence uncaused, **and** really many. Palamas says this in so many words: God is not composite on account of the energies.
- If that works, the Neo-Platonic proof still goes through as far as "the first cause is not put together from parts", and stops short of Plotinus.
- Feser's opening is a nice statement of the one-and-many in miniature, too: parts are understood through the whole (a leg is *for* walking), yet the whole depends on the parts. Each is prior in a different way.

Not checked: the exact place in Palamas (I think the *One Hundred and Fifty Chapters*).

## 7. How It Works as an Argument

In TAG form:

1. Knowledge and predication need real unity (universals, laws) and real plurality (individuals, facts), related and neither reducible.
2. A worldview whose ultimate is a bare one can't ground the many. A worldview whose ultimate is a bare many can't ground the one.
3. Only where the ultimate is itself one and many is the combination not a brute accident.
4. So the possibility of knowledge presupposes the Triune God.

See [[TAG]], [[Why Only the Orthodox Paradigm Can Account for Knowledge]].

## 8. Objections

I don't think this argument is as finished as it's usually presented, so listing what a critic would say.

- **"It's not one problem."** The table in sec. 1 lumps together universals, persistence, composition, politics. A philosopher would say these get different answers. (Reply: maybe, but the *pattern* of failure recurring across them is the point.)
- **"The Trinity is three Persons in one nature. The problem of universals is many individuals of one kind. Peter, Paul and John are three men. So by analogy, three gods?"** The Cappadocians faced exactly this (Gregory of Nyssa, *To Ablabius: On Not Three Gods*). The answer has to be that divine unity is far more than shared kind: one will, one energy, one source, mutual indwelling. Which means the Trinity isn't simply "the solution to universals". It's a unity-in-plurality of its own sort that created cases reflect at a distance.
- **"Why three?"** The argument, if it works, gets you plurality in God. It doesn't get you *three*. That comes from revelation. So it's better seen as showing the Trinity *fits* what knowledge requires than as deriving the Trinity.
- **"Just calling something 'equally ultimate' is naming the solution, not giving it."** Fair. The answer is that with an ultimate you can only ask whether it has the features needed, not what's behind it. Compare the stopping-point reply in [[The Euthyphro Dilemma and the Orthodox Answer]] sec. 5.
- **"A Platonist can have one and many both basic"** (late Plato, or a modern realist with universals and particulars as two irreducible categories). True, and this is the hard one. The reply is about *why* they go together: two unrelated brute categories that happen to fit, vs a personal source in whom they are united. And Conee's problems for universals (location, the instantiation regress) show that fit isn't free. See [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]] sec. 6.
- **Relative identity.** Someone might say the logic of "one God, three Persons" needs sorting out before it can ground anything. See [[Identity and Leibniz's Law]] sec. 8.
