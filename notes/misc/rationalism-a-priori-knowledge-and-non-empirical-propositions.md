The idea that a proposition's content is not found in sense data is often associated with rationalism, a priori knowledge, and non-empirical propositions. Philosophers distinguish between different kinds of propositions based on how their truth is known or justified. Below are key terms that relate to this concept:

## 1. A Priori vs. A Posteriori Propositions

- A Priori: Propositions known independently of experience (e.g., "2 + 2 = 4").
- A Posteriori: Propositions known through experience (e.g., "Water boils at 100°C").

If a proposition’s content is not found in sense data, it likely falls under a priori knowledge, meaning it is derived from reason rather than empirical observation.

## 2. Analytic vs. Synthetic Propositions

- Analytic: True by definition, such as "All bachelors are unmarried."
- Synthetic: True based on how the world actually is, such as "The cat is on the mat."

A proposition whose content is not found in sense data could be analytic a priori, like in logic and mathematics. However, there are also synthetic a priori claims (e.g., Kant’s metaphysical principles).

## 3. Rationalism vs. Empiricism

- Rationalism (Plato, Descartes, Leibniz): Knowledge is primarily derived from reason and innate ideas rather than sensory experience.
- Empiricism (Locke, Hume): Knowledge comes from sense data and experience.

A rationalist would argue that some truths exist independently of sense perception—such as mathematical truths, logical principles, and metaphysical claims.

## 4. Examples of Non-Empirical Propositions

- Mathematical truths: "The sum of interior angles in a triangle is 180 degrees."
- Metaphysical claims: "God exists as a necessary being."
- Ethical principles: "Murder is wrong."
- Logical truths: "If A = B and B = C, then A = C."

These are not found in sense data because they are either abstract, conceptual, or necessarily true regardless of experience.
