The **problem of universals** asks whether the general things we speak of, such as *humanity*, *redness* or *justice*, exist in reality or only in our minds and words. Socrates and Plato are both men: is there something real, "human nature", that they share?

## 1. Particulars and Universals

- **Particular**: an individual thing, existing in one place at one time. *This* man, *this* red apple.
- **Universal**: what many particulars have in common, and what a general word names. *Man*, *red*.

The question is what, if anything, a universal *is*.

## 2. The Main Positions

1. **Extreme (Platonic) Realism**:
    - Universals exist on their own, apart from particulars and apart from any mind, as eternal Forms.
    - Particulars are what they are by participating in the Forms.
    - *Latin tag:* universals *ante rem*, "before the thing".
2. **Moderate (Aristotelian) Realism**:
    - Universals are real, but they exist only in particulars, as their shared nature or form.
    - The mind abstracts the universal from the things it meets.
    - *Latin tag:* universals *in re*, "in the thing".
3. **Conceptualism**:
    - Universals exist only as concepts in the mind. There is nothing common in things themselves, only resemblance that the mind groups together.
4. **Nominalism**:
    - Only particulars exist. A universal is merely a name (*nomen*) or sign applied to many individuals.
    - Roscelin (11th century) called universals "a breath of the voice". **William of Ockham** (14th century) gave the view its lasting form.
    - *Latin tag:* universals *post rem*, "after the thing".

| Position | Where universals exist |
|---|---|
| Platonic realism | In themselves, apart from things |
| Aristotelian realism | In things, as their common nature |
| Conceptualism | In the mind only |
| Nominalism | Nowhere; they are names |

## 3. The Christian Synthesis

- Christian thinkers placed the Platonic Forms in the mind of God: universals exist *before* things as divine ideas, *in* things as their natures, and *after* things as concepts in the human mind.
- In the East this is expressed through the **logoi**: each nature has its uncreated logos in the divine Logos (St. Maximus the Confessor).
- St. John of Damascus, in the *Dialectica*, sets out the vocabulary: *ousia* or *physis* is what is common, *hypostasis* is the particular.

## 4. Why It Matters for Theology

1. **The Trinity**:
    - Three hypostases share one divine essence. If a common nature is only a name, the three Persons are three separate gods. Roscelin was accused of tritheism on exactly this ground.
2. **Christology**:
    - Christ assumed *human nature*, not merely one man's particular existence. "What is not assumed is not healed" requires that there be a real nature, common to all, that He healed.
3. **Adam and Christ**:
    - That all die in Adam and all are made alive in Christ (1 Corinthians 15:22) presupposes a real solidarity of nature.
4. **Morality and law**:
    - If there is no real human nature, there is no natural law and no stable account of what is good for man. Good becomes whatever God, or society, happens to command.
5. **Knowledge**:
    - Science studies kinds, not just individuals. If kinds are fictions, universal laws describe nothing real.

## 5. Consequences of Nominalism

- **Voluntarism**: with no natures to ground goodness, moral law rests on bare divine will. This sharpens the Euthyphro dilemma.
- **Fragmentation of the Church idea**: the Church becomes a collection of individuals, not a real body.
- **Legal models of salvation**: righteousness can be *imputed* by declaration when there is no shared nature to be healed. Late-medieval nominalism formed the background of the Reformers.
- **Scepticism**: Hume's doubts about causation and the self follow from allowing only particular impressions.

## 6. The Transcendental Point

- To argue at all, the nominalist uses universals: "all", "every", "argument", "true". He relies on logic that holds in every case.
- The claim "only particulars exist" is itself a universal claim.
- Universal, unchanging, immaterial realities need a ground that is itself universal, unchanging and immaterial. On the Orthodox view that ground is the Logos.

## Summary

- Realism says shared natures are real; nominalism says they are only names.
- The Christian answer locates universals first in God, then in things, then in our minds.
- The doctrines of the Trinity, the Incarnation and salvation all assume that natures are real.
