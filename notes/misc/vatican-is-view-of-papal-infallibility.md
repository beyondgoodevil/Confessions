**Vatican I's view of papal infallibility**, defined in **1870**, holds that the Pope is infallible only under specific conditions. The doctrine states that the Pope is free from error **when he speaks *ex cathedra*** (from the chair of St. Peter), meaning:

1. **When defining a doctrine** concerning **faith or morals**.
2. **To be held by the whole Church.**
3. **In his capacity** as the supreme pastor and teacher of all Christians.

## The Text (Pastor Aeternus, ch. 4)

The definition ends: such definitions of the Roman Pontiff are "**irreformable of themselves, and not from the consent of the Church**" (*ex sese, non autem ex consensu Ecclesiae*).

- That phrase was aimed at **Gallicanism** (the 1682 Gallican Articles: papal judgements bind only with the Church's consent).
- So infallibility isn't the pope speaking *for* a consensus he has checked; it's his own charism.

## How It Came About (Siecienski)

- Medieval roots: the pope can't be judged by anyone (*prima sedes a nemine iudicatur*); Peter's faith won't fail (Luke 22:32). But medieval canonists also said a pope who fell into heresy could be deposed.
- Opposed in the **Conciliarist** era (Constance, 1415: councils are above popes).
- 19th-century **ultramontanism** (de Maistre, *Du Pape*, 1819; Veuillot; Manning) pushed it as a defence against liberalism and revolution.
- **Vatican I** (1869–70): a "minority" of about 60 bishops (many from Germany, France, Austria, the US; Döllinger outside the council) thought it **inopportune** or historically false. Most left Rome before the final vote rather than vote no. Final vote **533–2**. The two "no" voters submitted at once.
- The council was suspended when Italian troops took Rome (1870). Döllinger and others refused it: the **Old Catholic** schism.

## Examples Usually Given

Catholic theologians generally agree only two clear *ex cathedra* definitions since 1870:
- **Immaculate Conception** (Pius IX, 1854: retroactively counted)
- **Assumption** (Pius XII, 1950)

There's no official list, which itself is an objection: if no one knows exactly which statements are infallible, what does the guarantee give?

## Historical Problem Cases

| Case | Problem |
|---|---|
| **Pope Liberius** (4th c.) | under pressure, signed a semi-Arian formula and condemned Athanasius |
| **Pope Vigilius** (553) | changed his position on the Three Chapters several times; the Fifth Council struck him from the diptychs |
| **Pope Honorius** (d. 638) | taught one will in Christ; condemned by name as a heretic by the **Sixth Ecumenical Council** (681), confirmed by Pope Leo II. Vatican I's defenders argued he wasn't speaking *ex cathedra* |

## Orthodox Objections

*Mine, from Siecienski's summary of the Orthodox case.*

- **Infallibility belongs to the Church**, not a single bishop: "the pillar and ground of the truth" (1 Tim 3:15). The Spirit leads the Church (John 16:13), not one see.
- **Councils**, received by the whole Church, are the voice of the Church. The Eastern Patriarchs' **Encyclical of 1848** (answering Pius IX): "the guardian of religion is the very body of the Church, that is, the people itself".
- **Luke 22:32** ("I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not") was a prayer for Peter personally before his denial, and the Fathers apply Peter's confession to all bishops.
- No first-millennium council or Father taught it. Honorius's condemnation is decisive against it historically.

## More from Siecienski (ch. 8–9)

- At **Florence** the Latins insisted every question "be defined in accordance with the dogmas of the Holy Roman Church". Patriarch Joseph's alleged will acknowledging the pope as "vicar of Christ" is disputed.
- **Peter Mogila**'s *Confession* (17th c.) has been called "the most Latin document ever adopted by an official council of the Orthodox Church".
- Orthodox apologists: **Honorius**'s anathema at Constantinople III disproves papal infallibility in the early Church: "not one father opened his mouth to oppose such audacity."
- **Vatican I**: Pius IX wanted spiritual authority against modern errors. The ultramontanes (Manning, Senestrey) "moved heaven and earth"; a French and German minority thought it "inopportune". Sixteen bishops who signed against the definition were forced to withdraw signatures (Strossmayer's account). The Melkite story that Pius kicked the Melkite patriarch Gregory in the head saying "*Testa dura*!"
- Bishop **Gasser**'s official explanation: infallibility is "personal" in that it belongs to the Roman Pontiff, "but not to the pope as a private person"; "separate, or rather distinct", resting on a special promise, without separating the pope "from his ordained union with the Church". Klaus Schatz: many crucial objections came "from the arsenal of history". **Küng**: the Church is "indefectible" rather than "infallible".
- **20th century**: Orthodox reaction reduced "primacy of honour" to "**honorary primacy**". **Bulgakov**, *The Vatican Dogma*. Paul VI and Athenagoras (1964): the "dialogue of love"; mutual lifting of the 1054 anathemas (1965); "**sister churches**". Paul VI knelt and kissed Metropolitan Meliton's foot.
- **Zizioulas**: Rome's present view of universal primacy "appears totally unacceptable to the Orthodox, and it should be, unless fundamentally qualified"; but a primacy within synodality is possible. The Moscow Patriarchate wanted a statement that Rome's jurisdiction didn't extend to the East in the first millennium.
- The Melkite proposal: "I believe everything Eastern Orthodoxy teaches" and "I am in communion with the Bishop of Rome as first among bishops, within the limits recognised by the Eastern Fathers of the first millennium."
