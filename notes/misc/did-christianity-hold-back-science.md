The popular story is that Christianity, in Lindberg's summary of it, "sent the scientific enterprise into a tailspin from which it did not recover for more than a thousand years" (*Beginnings*, p. 148). In 1896 Andrew Dickson White wrote that the establishment of Christianity "arrested the normal development of the physical sciences for over fifteen hundred years". Charles Freeman repeated the charge in 2003 (*Beginnings*, p. 358). This note tests the claim against the two historians who have done most to replace it, **David Lindberg** and **Edward Grant**, and against the church fathers themselves, chiefly Basil's homilies on the six days of creation. The question has three parts. What did the early church think pagan natural philosophy was *for*? What did the medieval church actually forbid? And did those prohibitions cost anything?

### 1. Two Myths and Six Precepts

Lindberg opens his chapter in the *Cambridge History of Science* by setting aside two extremes. One is the **warfare thesis** of Draper, White and Freeman, which grew out of the French Enlightenment. The other is its mirror image, associated with Stanley Jaki and Rodney Stark: that the church was science's "partner and patron, furnishing theological assumptions without which genuine science would have been impossible". Lindberg says the second is "equally the product of a religious agenda", and that "few scholars now take either of these positions seriously" (CHS 2, p. 268).

In their place he offers six methodological precepts (pp. 269–71):

1. "Science" and "theology" are abstractions. "Science and theology cannot interact, but scientists and theologians can."
2. The two were not kept in separate compartments. They were often the same person.
3. Science was never immune from its culture, and it is not immune now.
4. Neither side was monolithic, so "generalizations reducible to a phrase or a slogan will never be sufficient".
5. Episodes of conflict were local and contingent. They were driven by fears, rivalries and ambitions, not by a structural war.
6. In medieval terms the church had legitimate epistemic authority. Religion was "not a bothersome meddler but an inevitable and (in medieval terms) legitimate player in the game" (p. 271).

The sixth precept matters most. Asking whether the church "interfered" with science assumes a modern division of authority that nobody in the Middle Ages accepted.

Grant agrees about the myth but frames the question differently. Modern science, he notes, arose in seventeenth-century Western Europe "and nowhere else". Islam, China and Byzantium all had science, but none of them *institutionalised* it. The real question is therefore what in Latin Christendom made that institutionalisation possible (*Foundations*, p. 168).

### 2. Tertullian and the Fathers' Ambivalence

The hostile strand is real. Tertullian (d. c. 230) called philosophy the incubator of heresy, attacked "wretched Aristotle" for inventing dialectic, and concluded: "No curiosity is required of us after Christ Jesus, no investigation after the gospel" (*Prescription Against Heretics* 7, quoted in CHS 2, p. 271). Grant quotes his more famous line: "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?" (*Foundations*, p. 4). Tatian, Eusebius and Theodoret took pleasure in showing that Greek science contradicted itself. Theodoret likened it to writing on water (p. 3).

Lindberg's verdict is that this hostility was "the exception rather than the rule" (CHS 2, p. 272). Greek learning could not be repudiated, for four reasons:

- Many fathers had been philosophically educated before they converted.
- Doctrine could not be worked out without Greek logic.
- Answering pagan critics required philosophy.
- Nobody wanted to give up geometry, astronomy, medicine, agriculture and grammar and return "to a state of barbaric ignorance".

Christians refused the pagan cults, not Greco-Roman culture as a whole. Grant adds a structural point. Christianity spread slowly over about three and a half centuries, mostly without force, whereas Islam spread by conquest in under a hundred years. "It was the slow percolation of Christianity that enabled it to adjust to the pagan world around it" (*Foundations*, p. 2).

### 3. Augustine and the Handmaiden Formula

The settlement came from **Augustine**, and it was ambivalent from the start. In the *Literal Meaning of Genesis* (II.9) he notes that the "sacred writers" omitted discussion of the shape of the heavens because "such subjects are of no profit for those who seek beatitude, and, what is worse, they take up very precious time that ought to be given to what is spiritually beneficial" (CHS 2, pp. 272–73). Late in life, in the *Retractions*, he regretted having stressed the liberal arts (*Foundations*, p. 5).

Yet the same work says that sensory knowledge of creatures, compared with the ignorance of those who know nothing of creation, "deserves to be called day" (IV.23). Augustine also warns that "it is a disgraceful and dangerous thing for an infidel to hear a Christian, presumably giving the meaning of Holy Scripture, talking nonsense on these topics" (I.19). Bad science brings the faith into disrepute (CHS 2, p. 273).

*On Christian Doctrine* turns this into a policy:

- Whatever the pagans said that is true "should be taken from them as from unjust possessors and converted to our use" (II.40).
- A good Christian "should understand that wherever he may find truth, it is his Lord's" (II.18).
- The governing principle: "We should use this world and not enjoy it" (CHS 2, pp. 273–74).

Lindberg's summary: "The natural sciences must serve as the handmaidens of theology and religion" (p. 274). Grant traces the image back through Clement and Origen to Philo, and calls it "obviously a compromise between rejection of traditional pagan learning and its full acceptance" (*Foundations*, pp. 3–4).

Two things follow, and the rest of this note depends on them. First, the handmaiden is *employed*, not dismissed. Lindberg puts it this way: "for most of its history, the question has not been whether science will function as handmaiden, but which mistress it will serve" (*Beginnings*, p. 150). Second, the formula ranks the sciences: useful knowledge is welcome and curiosity for its own sake is suspect. The formula supplies both the license and the limit.

### 4. Basil's Hexaemeron

The genre in which the fathers did their natural philosophy was the **hexaemeron**, a commentary on the six days of Genesis 1. Philo, Basil, Ambrose (who borrowed heavily from Basil), Augustine and Philoponus all wrote one (*Foundations*, pp. 5–6). Read in full, Basil's nine homilies show both sides of the handmaiden formula at once.

**Scorn for the philosophers.** Basil opens by declining to refute the Greeks: "The wise men of the Greeks wrote many works about nature, but not one account among them remained unaltered and firmly established, for the later account always overthrew the preceding one. As a consequence, there is no need for us to refute their words; they avail mutually for their own undoing" (Hom. 1.2, p. 5). He dismisses the atomists' cosmos as "a spider's web". Of the astronomers he writes that "they who measure the distances of the stars", chart the stars of both poles and time the planetary orbits "have not found one means from all this either to understand that God is the Creator of everything and the just Judge" (Hom. 1.4, p. 8). The complaint is not that their astronomy is wrong. It is that it leads nowhere.

**Ready use of what the philosophers knew.** On why the earth does not fall, Basil first answers with Scripture: "In the hand of God are all the ends of the earth". Then, without hostility, he sets out the Aristotelian explanation (the earth holds the middle place, heavy things move to the centre, and "reason has shown that downward is the center") and invites the hearer to "transfer your admiration to the wisdom of God which has ordered them so". The hierarchy is plain in the closing line: "if this is not so, still let the simplicity of faith be stronger than the deductions of reason" (Hom. 1.9–10, pp. 16–17).

**A theology of nature that invites observation.** The homily on plants faces the problem of poisons head on. Hemlock grows beside wheat, and hellebore, mandrake and opium beside food. Basil's answer: "There is not one plant without worth, not one without use." Starlings eat hemlock and quails eat hellebore without harm, and doctors use mandrake for sleep and opium for pain (Hom. 5.4, p. 72). The homilies are full of animal behaviour taken largely from Aristotle's *History of Animals*: the tortoise that eats marjoram after a viper, ants splitting grain so it will not sprout. The assumption that every created thing has a purpose is anti-scientific in neither tendency nor effect.

**Indifference to cosmography.** The ninth homily states the principle outright. Cosmographers argue over whether the earth is a sphere, a cylinder, a disk or a winnowing fan, "each one overthrowing the ideas of the other". Basil refuses to think Genesis "of less value because the servant of God, Moses, gave no discussion concerning the shape". Moses "left unsaid, as useless for us, things in no way pertaining to us" (Hom. 9.1, pp. 135–36). This is not a flat earth. Basil names the sphere among the options, and his translator's note points to Aristotle's proof of it. He simply declines to let the question matter. He is also a literalist about the text: "When I hear 'grass,' I think of grass" (p. 135).

The *Address to Young Men on Reading Greek Literature* gives the matching rule for pagan books in general. Secular wisdom is the foliage that protects and adorns the fruit, which is truth. Christians should read pagan writers as bees visit flowers: "taking only so much of them as is suitable for their work", leaving the rest (chs. 3–4). Leonardo Bruni later translated the *Address* into Latin, and it became a charter for humanist education (*Foundations*, p. 4).

So Basil is neither an enemy nor a patron of inquiry. He takes Greek science where it helps, shrugs where it does not, and keeps it subordinate. Applied to astronomy, that indifference does real damage. Applied to plants and animals, it produces an enthusiastic naturalist.

### 5. A Dark Age, but Whose Fault?

Both historians concede the decline. Grant: "There is no denying, however, that a scientific dark age had descended upon Western Europe" (*Foundations*, p. 17). Isidore's geometry was poor and Bede's tides were local lore. The question is cause. Lindberg dates the decline of Roman learning from about AD 180, before Christianity dominated. Its causes were civil war, economic contraction, the loss of leisure, the split between East and West and the decay of Greek–Latin bilingualism. "Rome had managed to preserve and transmit the Greek classical tradition only in a thin and limited version" (*Beginnings*, ch. 7). Grant likewise calls Greek science "a fragile enterprise, able to advance and preserve itself just so long as the intellectual environment was favorable" (*Foundations*, p. 11). It had always depended on a few gifted people in a few centres.

Against that background Lindberg makes his best-known argument:

> If we compare the early church with a modern research university or the National Science Foundation, the church will prove to have failed abysmally as a supporter of science and natural philosophy. But such a comparison is obviously unfair. If, instead, we compare the support given to the study of nature by the early church with the support available from any other contemporary social institution, it will become apparent that the church was the major patron of scientific learning. (*Beginnings*, p. 150)

"Limited and selective patronage is a far cry from opposition" (p. 150). The Benedictine Rule required literacy and books. Cassiodorus' Vivarium copied texts. Isidore's *Etymologies* survives in over a thousand manuscripts, teaches a spherical earth and attacks astrology. Bede's computus became the standard method for dating Easter throughout Christendom. Lindberg's verdict on the monasteries: "Without them, Western Europe would not have had more science, but less" (*Beginnings*, p. 157). He also settles the flat-earth question. Cosmas Indicopleustes is "the only medieval European known to have defended a flat earth cosmology" (p. 161), and the claim that medieval scholars thought the earth flat "is pure legend", a product of Washington Irving and the nineteenth century (CHS 2, p. 285).

Lindberg does not hide the darker episodes. Hypatia, "a superb mathematician and philosopher", was "attacked and killed for her pagan beliefs by a mob of Christians" (*Beginnings*, p. 161). Justinian closed the Academy in 529 (*Foundations*, p. 188).

### 6. Aristotle Arrives: "Invaded Occupied Territory"

The real test came in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when the Greek and Arabic corpus was translated into Latin. Most of it was "theologically benign" and was welcomed without reservation: Euclid, Ptolemy, al-Khwarizmi, Ibn al-Haytham, Avicenna's *Canon*. Trouble arose only where "Aristotelian philosophy did not fill an intellectual vacuum, but invaded occupied territory" (*Beginnings*, p. 226). The points of conflict were these (pp. 228–31):

- the **eternity of the world**;
- a **determinism** whose Prime Mover was "a far cry from the Christian God, who knows when the sparrow falls";
- astrology versus free will;
- the **soul** as the form of the body, and Averroes' single intellect for all mankind;
- a naturalism that seemed to leave no room for miracles.

Behind all of these lay institutional rivalries: arts against theology, seculars against friars, Franciscans against Dominicans. "Viewed in the worst light, it appeared that Jerusalem was being asked to yield to the authority of Athens" (p. 231). Lindberg admits that "I do not believe that one could have predicted in advance that the negotiations would be successful" (CHS 2, p. 276).

### 7. What Was Actually Forbidden: The Paris Bans

The record of prohibitions is short and local:

- **1210.** The provincial synod of Sens forbade the reading of Aristotle's natural philosophy at Paris, publicly or in secret, under pain of excommunication. The decree followed charges of pantheism.
- **1215.** The ban was repeated for the University of Paris.
- **1231.** Gregory IX modified the ban. His reasoning was pure handmaiden: "Since the other sciences should serve the wisdom of holy Scripture, they are to be appropriated by the faithful insofar as they are known to conform to the good pleasure of the Giver" (*Beginnings*, p. 227). He appointed a three-man commission to purge the books of error.
- **1245.** Innocent IV extended the ban to Toulouse. Ironically, Toulouse had advertised in 1229 that it taught the books banned at Paris (*Foundations*, pp. 70–71).

The commission never reported. "No purged version of Aristotle has ever been discovered. The subsequent acceptance of Aristotle was based on a complete, uncensored version of his works" (*Beginnings*, p. 227). The books were probably read privately throughout. **Oxford never banned them.** By 1255 the Paris arts statutes *required* all of Aristotle's known works for the master's degree (*Foundations*, p. 71). The ban lasted about forty years in one city and was never enforced. In 1228 Gregory had also ordered theologians to keep natural philosophy out of theology. In Grant's words, "Gregory's ban did not prevail" (pp. 73–74).

The reasons it failed are part of the answer. The friars wanted Aristotle. **Albert the Great** wrote more than 8,000 pages of commentary, he said, because his Dominican brothers kept "begging us for the past several years" for a complete science of nature (*Foundations*, p. 174). Albert's rule on Noah's flood marks the boundary: "the philosopher's task is not to investigate the causes of God's will, but to inquire into the natural causes by which God's will produces its effect" (*Beginnings*, p. 241). **Aquinas** gave the reason that settled the matter: "what is divinely taught to us by faith cannot be contrary to what we are endowed with by nature. One or the other would have to be false, and since we have both of them from God, he would be the cause of our error, which is impossible" (p. 241). In Lindberg's image, Thomas offered the handmaiden "enlarged responsibilities and elevated status" (p. 242). In the short run conservatives saw him as "a dangerous radical" (p. 243).

**Roger Bacon** shows how elastic the formula was. For him theology is "mistress of the others… And it lays claim to their virtues and subordinates them to its nod and command". The conclusion he drew was expansion: "without mathematics created things cannot be known; it follows that mathematics is altogether necessary for sacred knowledge" (CHS 2, pp. 277–78). Lindberg notes that nobody has found a single theoretical claim in Bacon's roughly 90,000 words on optics that reflects theological influence (p. 285 n.).

### 8. The Condemnations of 1270 and 1277

The second crisis came from the **radical Aristotelians** of the arts faculty, Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia. Boethius declared that "it belongs to the philosopher to determine every question that can be disputed by reason" (*Beginnings*, p. 245), and held that the philosopher as philosopher must deny creation and resurrection. In 1270 Bishop Étienne Tempier condemned thirteen propositions. In 1272 the arts masters were made to swear that they would not treat theological questions, and that if they had to, they would resolve them in favour of the faith. That oath stayed in force into the fifteenth century (*Foundations*, pp. 71, 76). Pope John XXI asked Tempier to investigate. Within three weeks, in March 1277, Tempier issued 219 propositions "drawn up in haste, without apparent order", on the third anniversary of Aquinas's death and including some fifteen to twenty Thomist theses (p. 71; *Beginnings*, pp. 246–47).

The articles show what the theologians feared:

- **Eternity of the world:** 27 of the 219 articles, such as art. 9, "there was no first man, nor will there be a last". Yet "no one has yet been identified who held this heretical opinion without qualification" (*Foundations*, pp. 74–75).
- **Double truth.** Tempier's prologue condemns those who say that "things are true according to philosophy, but not according to the Catholic faith; as if there could be two contrary truths". But "no arts master has yet been discovered who believed literally in a doctrine of the double truth" (p. 77). Siger protected himself with "we say these things as the opinion of the Philosopher… although not asserting them as true". Boethius wrote that "whoever seeks to know these things by reason is a fool" (pp. 75–76).
- **The arrogance of philosophy:** art. 152, "That theological discussions are based on fables"; art. 154, "That the only wise men of the world are philosophers"; art. 40, "That there is no more excellent state than to devote oneself to philosophy"; art. 150, "That a man ought not to be satisfied to have certitude on any question on the basis of authority" (pp. 73, 200).
- **Limits on God's power:** art. 34, God could not make several worlds; art. 49, God could not move the heavens in a straight line because a vacuum would remain; arts. 139–41, God could not make an accident exist without a subject. The last also touched the Eucharist (pp. 78–79).

The principle behind the last group is that God can do anything that does not involve a logical contradiction (*Beginnings*, p. 247).

### 9. Birth Certificate of Modern Science?

**Pierre Duhem** read 1277 as an attack on entrenched Aristotelian physics and therefore as "the birth certificate of modern science". Lindberg calls this "a clever interpretation, and it is not entirely wrong" (*Beginnings*, p. 247). Once theologians insisted that God *could* make other worlds or a void, philosophers had to reason about them. Oresme argued that the cosmos is surrounded by infinite void space and warned critics that "to say the contrary is to maintain an article condemned in Paris" (p. 251). Grant traces the line further. Writers imagined God annihilating matter, and from that came the medieval thought experiments behind Locke's and Gassendi's void space, Hobbes's "feigning the world to be annihilated", and Clarke's defence of Newton's absolute space against Leibniz (*Foundations*, pp. 196–97).

Both historians nonetheless reject Duhem's verdict. Lindberg's points:

- In 1277 there was no Aristotelian orthodoxy to shatter.
- The condemnation was one of at least sixteen at Paris in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.
- It was partly revoked in 1325, after Aquinas's canonisation.
- Its real meaning: "the condemnations represent a victory not for modern science but for conservative thirteenth-century theology" (*Beginnings*, pp. 247–51).

Grant agrees that neither Duhem nor Koyré, who judged 1277 irrelevant, is right. The condemnation "made medieval natural philosophy more interesting than it would otherwise have been", but it "did not revolutionize Aristotelian natural philosophy nor cause it to be abandoned" (*Foundations*, p. 83). Lindberg also rejects the related thesis that stressing divine omnipotence produced empiricism: "the historical record does not appear to bear it out", since systematic experiment waited for the seventeenth century (*Beginnings*, p. 253).

The cost was real, but it was measured in caution, not in silence. Buridan, discussing the celestial movers, wrote "this I do not say assertively", deferring to "the theological masters". He also recorded that theologians had reproached him for mixing theology into physics (*Beginnings*, p. 250; *Foundations*, p. 81). From 1341 new Paris masters swore to teach Aristotle and Averroes "except in those cases that are contrary to the faith" (*Beginnings*, p. 251). The working device was **speaking naturally** (*loquendo naturaliter*). Albert of Saxony could assume the eternity of the world for the sake of argument and simply set aside the resurrection: "the natural philosopher is not much concerned with this argument" (*Foundations*, pp. 80–81). Any condemned proposition could be explored provided it was not asserted as true.

### 10. The Theologian-Natural Philosophers

Grant's central thesis is that the most important fact is the one the conflicts obscure. Aristotle "was enthusiastically welcomed and highly regarded by both arts masters and theologians" (*Foundations*, p. 70). The universities could teach a pagan natural philosophy only because theologians approved. A master of arts, which meant years of logic and natural philosophy, was normally required before studying theology. The result was a class of **theologian-natural philosophers**: Albert, Grosseteste, Pecham, Theodoric of Freiberg, Bradwardine, Oresme, Henry of Langenstein (pp. 84, 174–75). Of them Grant writes:

> Theologians had remarkable intellectual freedom and rarely permitted theology to hinder their inquiries into the physical world. If there was any temptation to produce a "Christian science," they successfully resisted it. (*Foundations*, p. 84)

Oresme cited the Bible about fifty times in his treatise on the configuration of qualities, but only as illustration, never as proof (p. 84). Theologians embraced natural philosophy so warmly that the church at times had to warn them against using it frivolously in theology (p. 175). On Grant's reading, the bans and 1277 "become relatively minor aberrations when viewed against the grand sweep of the history of Western Christianity" (p. 85). In the 1330s Ockham stated the principle of free inquiry: "Assertions… concerning natural philosophy, which do not pertain to theology, should not be solemnly condemned or forbidden to anyone, since in such matters everyone should be free to say freely whatever he pleases" (p. 201).

Lindberg agrees about the outcome. The church Christianised Aristotle and became "the most powerful and generous patron of serious philosophical and scientific effort the West had ever seen" (CHS 2, p. 280). In the universities natural science "found a secure institutional home for the first time in history" (p. 284). Benefices paid for the scholars. In the fourteenth century Scotus and Ockham pulled theology and philosophy further apart, and natural philosophers became "far less likely to allow God a causal or explanatory role in their theories" (p. 282).

### 11. The Comparative Test: Islam and Byzantium

Grant's strongest argument is comparative. Before 1500, Islam's mathematics, astronomy, optics and medicine outranked the West's. The West learned them from Arabic.

**Islam.** Natural philosophy remained a "foreign science". It was pursued by *falasifa* under royal patronage rather than in schools, and was often taught privately. Al-Biruni, Avicenna and Ibn al-Haytham did not hold teaching posts (*Foundations*, pp. 177–78). Most theologians rejected secondary causation, holding that God causes every event directly. Al-Ghazali's *Incoherence* declared the philosophers infidels on three points: eternity, God's knowledge of particulars and bodily resurrection. Ibn Khaldun advised that "the problems of physics are of no importance for us in our religious affairs or our livelihoods. Therefore, we must leave them alone" (pp. 179–81). Arithmetic and astronomy were valued because they divided inheritances and timed the prayers. Grant draws the irony: "in Islam, the handmaiden idea of the sciences took hold and remained. In the Latin West, by contrast, the handmaiden idea… faded away" (p. 179). Averroes had to write a treatise asking whether the Law *permits* the study of philosophy. Grant knows "of no analogous treatise in the late Latin Middle Ages" (p. 185).

**Byzantium.** This is the more pointed case, because Byzantium had the Greek texts in the original. Philoponus refuted Aristotle on eternity, projectile motion and the heavens (*Beginnings*, ch. 7), but after the sixth century little followed. Grant's reasons:

- The state was theocratic and had no separation of church and state.
- The Orthodox Church held to the handmaiden approach throughout and was hostile to study "for the sheer love of knowledge".
- John Italos was condemned in 1082 for loving Hellenic learning too much, and his errors were read aloud in every church on the first Sunday of Lent.
- Theodore Metochites summed up the scholarly mood: "The great men of the past have said everything so perfectly that they have left nothing for us to say."

Grant also judges that the Orthodox Church was "probably not the major factor" in Byzantium's meagre output. Constant war, the lack of institutions and a solitary, rhetorical scholarly culture mattered as much. The Byzantines' real achievement was to be "the world's librarians" (*Foundations*, pp. 186–91).

The lesson Grant draws is that the same handmaiden formula had different outcomes depending on the institutions around it. In the Latin West, the separation of church and state (Christ's "render unto Caesar", Gelasius's two powers, a church that "never attempted to establish a theocracy"), together with the universities' corporate freedom, let the handmaiden grow independent (*Foundations*, pp. 8–9, 172, 183).

### 12. Assessment

**The warfare thesis fails on the evidence.** Its key claims are false: that the church suppressed Greek science, that it taught a flat earth, that it caused the dark age, that 1277 was typical. The decline of Latin learning began before Christianity's ascendancy. Across Christendom the bans amounted to one city's unenforced ban on Aristotle for about forty years, while Oxford studied him freely. The flat earth is a nineteenth-century invention. The one great structural fact, the university with natural philosophy at the centre of its curriculum for four or five centuries, was created by churchmen and sanctioned by popes. In any period before the seventeenth century, comparing the church with other institutions rather than with the National Science Foundation makes it the patron, not the persecutor.

**The partner thesis also overreaches.** Lindberg is emphatic: "I am thus stating my vehement opposition to the opinion… that Christian religion or theology was the nursery out of which Western science emerged" (CHS 2, p. 282). The content of medieval science was imported from Greece and Islam. Christianity shaped how it was received but did not produce it. Grant's thesis is institutional, not doctrinal. His three preconditions are the translations, the universities and the theologian-natural philosophers, and the first owed nothing to Christian theology. Christianity's part in the rest is an accommodation, not an invention. The Jaki–Stark argument that creation doctrine alone made science thinkable cannot explain why Byzantium, with the same creed and the original texts, did so little.

**The handmaiden formula had a real cost, but a specific one.**

- *Basil and Augustine.* Their indifference to cosmography, which Moses left aside "as useless for us", ranked useful knowledge above curiosity. Combined with the collapse of schools, that indifference helps explain why early-medieval Latin science was thin. Augustine's late regret about the liberal arts was not harmless.
- *Thirteenth century.* Theology claimed the right to veto conclusions. The 1272 oath and 1277 forced arts masters into hypothetical language.
- *Fourteenth century.* Buridan and Oresme wrote with the condemned articles in view.

These are real constraints. Lindberg's answer is that patronage always comes with conditions. Is handmaiden status compatible with genuine science? "The answer to the latter question is an emphatic 'of course!' Patronage without 'strings' of any kind is a figment of the imagination." The question is how tight the strings were. "During the Middle Ages, the mistress, theology, was surprisingly liberal with her handmaidens". The medieval scholar's "freedom of thought and expression was roughly comparable to that of his successors in the seventeenth century" (CHS 2, pp. 284–85).

**Two things in the sources pull against the reassuring conclusion.** First, Grant's own evidence shows that the formula did not liberate by itself. In Islam and Byzantium the same subordination of science to religion persisted and stifled. What freed natural philosophy in the West was not the handmaiden formula but its gradual *erosion* in institutions with corporate autonomy, the "formulaic" invocation that Grant describes (*Foundations*, p. 7). Credit therefore goes less to Christian theology as such than to the Western church's particular arrangement with the state and the universities. Second, 1277 shows that theological anxiety could still impose itself, even if briefly and in one city. Its benign effects (the void, other worlds) were by-products. The theologians' aim was to rein philosophy in, and Lindberg rightly calls it a victory for conservative theology. That it stimulated speculation is a historical irony, not a vindication.

**The period this note does not cover.** Grant blames the modern myth partly on the seventeenth century: Galileo's Simplicio, Locke's "great mintmasters" of empty words, and above all the condemnation of Galileo in 1633, which made the church look like Aristotle's armed guard and was read back into the Middle Ages (*Foundations*, pp. 203–4). Whether Galileo's case shows a church holding back science is a separate question. It concerns the Counter-Reformation, biblical exegesis and Copernicanism, not the medieval reception of Aristotle. Lindberg notes that Galileo disputed the church's exegetical principles, not its authority to interpret Scripture (CHS 2, pp. 270–71 n.).

**On continuity.** Lindberg's final chapter is cautious about how much the Middle Ages gave to the Scientific Revolution. For him the revolution was chiefly metaphysical: the mechanical philosophy's lifeless atoms replaced Aristotle's purposeful nature. But he finds real continuities: Ptolemy in Copernicus, medieval visual theory in Kepler, the Merton mean-speed rule and impetus in Galileo, Oresme's graphs. Neither he nor Grant claims the church made those things. They claim it housed and paid the people who did (*Beginnings*, pp. 364–67).

### Conclusion

Christianity did not hold back science in the way the popular story claims. The early church inherited a Greek science already in decline. It kept what it could, chiefly through monasteries, and treated natural knowledge as a handmaiden: valued for its use to faith, suspect when pursued for its own sake. Basil's homilies show the formula in action. They scorn the philosophers' quarrels and are indifferent to the shape of the earth, yet borrow Aristotle's physics without embarrassment and insist that not one plant is without use. When Aristotle arrived in the thirteenth century, the church did try to restrict him: the Paris bans of 1210–31 and the condemnations of 1270 and 1277. Those measures were local, unenforced or short-lived, and they ended with Aristotle as the core of a university curriculum taught by theologians. The handmaiden had real strings attached, among them oaths, censures and the habit of "speaking naturally". Lindberg and Grant agree they were loose, and the comparison with Islam and Byzantium suggests why: what mattered was less the formula than the Latin West's universities and its separation of church from state, which let the handmaiden become independent.
