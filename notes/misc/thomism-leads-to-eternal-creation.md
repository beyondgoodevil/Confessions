## I. How Thomism Leads to Eternal Creation

Thomism teaches that God is Pure Act (actus purus)—meaning there is no potentiality in Him, and He is always in perfect, infinite actualization. According to Thomists:

- God's will and action are identical with His essence.
- There is no distinction between God being and God acting.
- God's will is immutable, eternal, and necessary.

From this, we logically derive:

1. If God’s will to create is eternal and unchangeable, then creation itself must also be eternal, since in God there is no "before" or "after" and no change from not-willing to willing.
2. There can be no temporal beginning in God's will, because His will is not conditioned or caused by anything, nor can it undergo new determinations.
3. Therefore, if God wills the world, He does so *necessarily and eternally*, making the act of creation coeternal with Him.

This contradicts:

- Genesis 1:1 — “In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.”
- The Orthodox view that creation had a beginning in time, while God Himself exists beyond and before all time.
- The teaching that God freely created the world, not out of necessity or natural outflow of His essence.

## II. Contradiction with the Fact that God “Rested”

The Scriptures teach clearly:

> “And God rested on the seventh day from all His works which He had made.” — Genesis 2:2 (LXX)

This *rest* is an indication of:

- Cessation of activity, at least regarding the creative acts.
- Divine freedom in choosing when to act and when not to act.
- A God who enters into time and relates to creation dynamically, not just through eternal, unchanging activity.

But in Thomism:

- God is *always acting*, always in perfect activity.
- There is no *real change* in God’s action—no pause, rest, or withdrawal.
- God's action toward creation is timelessly willed, and thus the idea of resting becomes metaphorical or anthropomorphic at best.

Therefore, Thomism denies that God can truly rest, because this would imply a change in God’s activity, and thus potentiality—which Thomism rejects.

## III. How This Negates God’s Free Will

Thomism affirms that God’s will is:

- Immutable — it cannot change.
- Identical with His essence — meaning He *necessarily* wills what He wills.
- Eternal and simple — meaning there is no "before" or "after" in God’s willing.

This leads to a serious problem:

1. If God wills the world eternally and immutably, then He cannot not will the world.
2. That means His will is not free in the way humans understand freedom—as a capacity to choose among alternatives.
3. Instead, God's will is necessary, not contingent.  
   – This resembles Neoplatonic emanation, where God necessarily overflows into creation.

Thus, God’s will is functionally identical with His nature, making it necessary that He creates, loves, and acts in precisely the way He does—without possibility of *not* doing so.

This denies true free will, because real freedom requires:

- The possibility to choose *not* to act.
- The ability to act at one time and not another.
- The distinction between essence and will, which Thomism collapses.

## IV. Orthodox Critique and Alternative

Eastern Orthodoxy, especially as articulated by St. Gregory Palamas, teaches:

1. Essence–Energies Distinction:  
   God’s essence is wholly transcendent and unknowable. His energies are His freely chosen manifestations—His actions, grace, will—through which He creates and interacts with the world.  
   – These are not identical with His essence.  
   – Therefore, God can act or not act in time without undergoing change in essence.
2. Uncreated Energies Are Real:  
   Creation is the result of God’s free energy, not of His essence.  
   – Creation is not necessary.  
   – God does not always act, and therefore can rest, or refrain from acting.
3. Freedom Preserved:  
   God remains absolutely free, not because His will is indistinguishable from His essence, but because His will and energy are not necessary extensions of His nature.
