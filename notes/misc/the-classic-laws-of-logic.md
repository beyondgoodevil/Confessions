The **classic laws of logic** are foundational principles that govern rational thought and reasoning. The three primary ones, often attributed to Aristotle, are:

1. **Law of Identity (A = A)**
    - A thing is itself.
    - Example: A tree is a tree, and 2 + 2 is 4.
2. **Law of Non-Contradiction (~(A ∧ ~A))**
    - Something cannot both be and not be in the same way at the same time.
    - Example: A car cannot be both moving and not moving at the same time in the same respect.
    - Aristotle's formulation (*Metaphysics* IV.3–4): "It is impossible for the same thing at the same time to belong and not to belong to the same thing in the same respect." The "most certain of all principles".
    - It **can't be proved** directly: any proof would assume it.
    - But it can be defended **by refutation** (*elenctically*): get the denier to **say something meaningful**. If he means "man" and not also "not-man", he's already accepted the law. If he refuses to say anything, he's "no better than a plant".
    - And no one lives as if it were false: "why does he walk to Megara rather than stay home thinking he's walking?" (*Met.* IV.4, 1008b).
3. **Law of the Excluded Middle (A ∨ ~A)**
    - A statement is either true or false; there is no middle ground.
    - Example: The light is either on or off—there is no in-between state (excluding dimmers or quantum cases, which deal with different categories).

A fourth law is sometimes included:

4. **Law of Sufficient Reason (Every true proposition has a reason why it is true)**
    - Often associated with Leibniz, this principle asserts that nothing is without explanation or cause.

These laws form the foundation of logical reasoning and are **presupposed** in any rational discourse.

They are not just rules of thought but laws of **being**: a thing can't be and not be. Logic follows reality (Kreeft, *Socratic Logic*).

- Kreeft states each law three ways: about **things** (nothing can both be and not be), about **terms** (S can't both be P and not be P) and about **propositions** (p can't be both true and false). The general form: "x is not non-x".
- The Greeks would call them *logoi*, not *nomoi*: facts we discover, not rules we make. "Thou shalt not kill" can be disobeyed; "the Law of Non-Contradiction... is always and necessarily obeyed by objective reality". We can break it only as a rule for our own thinking, by committing fallacies.
- Apparent exceptions are equivocations: "He's not himself today" means his actual self isn't his ideal self.
- Identity is the base; non-contradiction is its "negative corollary"; excluded middle adds that there's no third option. Together with sense experience they're Aristotle's answer to the skeptic who says syllogisms prove nothing. Kreeft thinks modern logicians, being mostly nominalists, undervalue the syllogism for this reason.
- **Sufficient reason** (Leibniz): everything has a reason both for **that** it is and for **what** it is; the principle of causality follows from it. "Though we obviously do not know the reason for everything, there is a reason for everything."

## Challenges from Non-Classical Logics

The main non-classical logics (Priest, *An Introduction to Non-Classical Logic*):

| Logic | Drops | Motivation |
|---|---|---|
| **Intuitionist** (Brouwer, Heyting) | **excluded middle** (and double negation) | in mathematics, a statement is true only if proved; for some statements we have neither proof nor disproof |
| **Many-valued** (Kleene, Łukasiewicz) | bivalence | future contingents ("there will be a sea battle tomorrow": Aristotle, *De Interpretatione* 9) may be neither true nor false yet; also sentences with names that refer to nothing |
| **Fuzzy** | sharp truth values | vagueness: "bald", "heap" (the sorites paradox) |
| **Paraconsistent** | **explosion** (from a contradiction, anything follows) | to reason with inconsistent information without everything following |
| **Dialetheism** (Priest) | **non-contradiction** itself: some contradictions are **true** | the Liar paradox ("this sentence is false"); paradoxes of set theory |
| **Quantum** (Birkhoff, von Neumann) | distributive law | quantum mechanics |

**Details from Priest**
- **Intuitionism** began with Brouwer (who thought arithmetic was the Kantian "pure form of temporal intuition"); Heyting axiomatised it in 1930; Kripke gave its possible-world semantics. Dummett later argued for it from the theory of **meaning**: if meaning is what we can show we understand, not Frege's truth conditions, then a sentence we can neither prove nor refute isn't guaranteed to be true or false.
- **Future contingents**: Aristotle's argument says that if "the first pope of the next century will be Chinese" is already true, it's necessary. Priest's reply: it trades on an ambiguity between "necessarily, if A then B" (true but the argument is invalid) and "if A, then necessarily B" (valid but no reason to accept it). So Aristotle's argument "does not... appear to work".
- In Kleene's 3-valued logic, *K3*, even the law of identity (if p then p) fails, since it has no logical truths at all. Łukasiewicz's *L3* changes the conditional to restore it.
- **Truth-value gluts**: the Liar ("this sentence is false") and Russell's set of all sets that aren't members of themselves both seem to be sound arguments for "A and not-A", each using excluded middle. In Priest's logic *LP*, "p or not-p" holds but contradictions don't explode (from "p and not-p" you can't infer just anything), at the price of modus ponens failing.
- Priest dismisses the reply that self-reference is meaningless: "this sentence has five words" is self-referential and fine.
- **Sorites**: Mary at five is a child, and anyone who's a child is still a child one second later; so she's a child at thirty-five. Fuzzy logic answers with degrees of truth, and modus ponens can fail when the premises are only nearly true.

**Replies**
- Most of these are formal systems for special purposes; they don't show the laws fail **of reality**.
- Priest's dialetheism is the only serious denial of non-contradiction. Critics: if "A and not-A" can be true, then Priest's own theory is consistent with its denial: Aristotle's point again.
- The excluded middle is better defended for **being** (every thing either is or is not F) than for **knowledge** (we may not know which).

## Logic and Orthodox Theology

- The Fathers used Aristotelian logic constantly (Schumann, ed., *Logic in Orthodox Christian Thinking*): Leontius of Byzantium, Maximus, John of Damascus (*Dialectica*, the first part of *The Fount of Knowledge*: Aristotle's categories and Porphyry's *Isagoge* as the tools for theology).
- But theology has **antinomies**: statements that seem to conflict (one and three; God and man; unknowable and known). Florensky called them antinomies; Lossky too. See [[The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church]].
- These are **not** contradictions in the strict sense: they're not "A and not-A in the same respect". God is one in **essence** and three in **hypostases**; Christ is one **person** in two **natures**. The distinctions of respect keep non-contradiction intact. See [[Jesus Being Fully Divine and Fully Human Is Not a Contradiction]].
- Apophatic theology isn't anti-logical: it denies that our concepts **capture** God, not that what we say about God can be contradictory. See [[Apophatic and Cataphatic Theology]].
- What's actually in Schumann's volume (eight essays):
    - **Schumann**'s preface argues there is a distinct "logical culture" of Orthodoxy, against the idea that Orthodox thought is "just mystic and illogic". He notes the Russian tradition of holy fools and Florensky's definition of antinomy: thesis and antithesis implying each other, so that "the truth is a self-inconsistent sentence".
    - **Swinburne** on Palamas: Barlaam held no syllogism can go from created things to God, since God isn't in the same genus. Swinburne reads Palamas's appeal to experience through his own **Principle of Credulity** (believe things are as they seem, absent counter-evidence) and **Principle of Testimony** (believe what others report, absent reason to doubt them).
    - **Paweł Rojek**, "The Logic of Palamism": Palamas's question was "what should the world be like since revelation and deification are possible?" The essence–energies distinction is a real distinction (*pragmatikē diakrisis*), not merely mental, and not a separation. Rojek tries to formalise it with kinds of identity and dependence, and argues the distinction isn't really an antinomy.
    - **Michael Craig Rhodes**, "On Contradiction in Orthodox Philosophy": defines contradiction from Aristotle (affirmation and denial of the same thing) and examines Florensky's "antinomy" (*p* and not-*p* both true, "above the plane of rationality"). "God is both one and three" isn't absurd the way "Pavel is his own father" is, because the subject is uncreated.
    - **Einar Duenger Bøhn**, "The Logic of the Incarnation": the problem isn't how Christ can be F and not-F, but whether being divine and being human are **compatible**. His solution: Christ is **fundamentally** divine and **essentially** human, two different kinds of truth that don't clash. He says it's neither kenoticism (the Son stops being divine) nor Nestorianism (two persons).
    - **Timothy Knepper** on Dionysius: ways of speaking of God by "removal" (*aphairesis*) and "hyper-" terms; God is "relatively effable".
    - **Bruce Foltz** on Florensky's *Pillar and Ground of the Truth*: "I=I" is "a cry of naked egotism"; true identity is A becoming itself through the other, like love. Florensky rejects "rational faith" with proofs as "a harsh, cruel stony growth in the heart".
    - **Schumann** on Stoic modal logic behind dogmatic thinking, and **Basil Lourié** on Dionysius's modal ontology.
- *Mine*: Florensky's "antinomy" talk is the risky end; Rojek and Bøhn are closer to the mainstream Fathers, who kept classical logic and limited its reach rather than replacing it.
