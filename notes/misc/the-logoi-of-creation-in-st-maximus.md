**St. Maximus the Confessor** (c. 580–662). Every created thing has a **logos** (pl. **logoi**): God's intention for it, what it is and what it's for. All the logoi are held in the one **Logos**, Christ. Main text is *Ambiguum 7* (which is hard going).

## 1. What the Logoi Are

- **Divine wills.** God's eternal intentions for each thing He'd make. Maximus takes from St. Dionysius the terms "predeterminations" and "divine wills".
    - The argument he credits to the disciples of Pantaenus (Clement's teacher): pagans asked how God knows things, by intellection or by sensation? Neither, they said. God made everything by His will and knows His own will, so "He knows beings as His own wills" (*Amb.* 7.24).
    - That's how Scripture can say "I know thee by name" to Moses and "I know you not" to others: each person's choice to move with or against God's logos for them prepares them to hear one or the other.
- **Uncreated.** They belong to God. Creatures are made *according to* them.
- **Many and one.** As many logoi as creatures, but one in the Logos: "the one Logos is many logoi, and the many logoi are one Logos."
    - Anyone who looks carefully at the "infinite natural differences" of things will know "the one Logos as many logoi, indivisibly distinguished", and the many as one, since all are related to Him "without being confused with Him" (*Amb.* 7.15).
    - **Procession and return**: by His "creative and sustaining procession" the One is many; by the "revertive... and providential return" the many are one, as radii are gathered in the centre of a circle (*Amb.* 7.20). (This holds once we set aside the highest negative theology, where the Logos isn't participated by anything at all.)
    - God "contained within Himself the pre-existing logoi" from eternity, but things were made "at the appropriate moment in time". God is "eternally an active creator", but creatures exist first potentially, then actually, because "it is not possible for the infinite and the finite to exist simultaneously on the same level of being" (*Amb.* 7.19).
- **Not the essence.** They're on the side of God's will and action toward creation, not what He is in Himself. So creation is free. He didn't have to.

Compare Plato's forms: see [[The Forms and the Demiurge]]. Same job (patterns of things) but willed, personal, and inside the Logos.

## 2. Scripture Behind It

- **John 1:1-3**: "All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made."
- **Col 1:16-17**: "All things were created by him, and for him... and by him all things consist."
- **Eph 1:10**: God will "gather together in one all things in Christ."
- **Ps 104:24**: "In wisdom hast thou made them all."
- **Acts 17:28**: "In him we live, and move, and have our being."

## 3. Logos and Tropos

Two things to tell apart in any creature:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **logos of nature** (*logos physeos*) | what it is | fixed by God, can't be corrupted |
| **mode of existence** (*tropos hyparxeos*) | how it actually lives that nature out | can go with the logos or against it |

- Sin doesn't change human nature. It's a wrong *mode*, living against your logos.
- Salvation doesn't swap human nature for something else. It heals and lifts the mode.
- Same distinction does work in Christology: in Christ the logos of human nature is untouched, the mode is new (virgin birth, walking on water).

This is probably the most useful single tool in Maximus. Keeps turning up.

## 4. Being, Well-Being, Eternal Well-Being

1. **Being**: given at creation.
2. **Well-being**: by freely choosing to live according to one's logos.
3. **Eternal well-being**: given by grace in the age to come.

First and third are gifts. The middle one needs our cooperation. That's where synergy sits. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]].

Maximus maps Acts 17:28 onto the three (*Amb.* 7.22): we are "in" God by not falsifying the logos of **being**; we "move" in Him by the logos of **well-being**, moved by the virtues; we "live" in Him by the logos of **eternal being**. So we're "portions of God" three ways: as existing, as good, and (by grace) as gods.

- **Virtue is participation in God**: Christ "is made unto us wisdom, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption" (1 Cor 1:30), so He is the essence of every virtue, and "anyone who through fixed habit participates in virtue, unquestionably participates in God" (*Amb.* 7.21). The image is given by nature; the **likeness** is added by choice through the virtues.
- **The exchange**: the same power "divinizes man through his love for God, and humanizes God through His love for man". "The Logos of God... wills always and in all things to accomplish the mystery of His embodiment" (*Amb.* 7.22).

**What the end looks like** (*Amb.* 7.10–12, 26–28):
- The chain: a mind moving rightly comes to **know**; knowing, it **loves**; loving, it suffers **ecstasy** toward the beloved and speeds up, until it is "wholly present in the whole beloved", like **air** filled with light or **iron** in the fire.
- This isn't the destruction of free will but "a voluntary surrender of the will": "not as I will, but as thou wilt"; "it is no longer I who live, but Christ". In the end there will be "only one energy, that of God and of those worthy of God, or rather of God alone".
- As the stars vanish when the sun is up, so in that state "nothing will appear apart from God".
- God will be to the soul "what the soul is to the body", so the soul receives immutability and the body immortality. "Man will remain wholly man in soul and body, owing to his nature, but will become wholly God in soul and body owing to the grace."
- It's called **pleasure** (the fulfilment of natural striving), **passion** (an ecstatic power lifting the passive to act) and **joy**, which "neither remembers former sorrows, nor fears... future satiety".

## 5. Against Origenism

- Origenists: souls pre-existed in unity with God, got bored ("satiety"), fell, were put in bodies.
    - They read Gregory Nazianzen's phrase that we are "a portion of God" as souls having pre-existed as parts of God. *Ambiguum 7* is written against them.
- Their order: rest → movement → becoming.
- Maximus flips it: **becoming → movement → rest**. Creatures come to be, move toward God, rest in Him at the end.
    - Rest at the end isn't the end of movement but its fulfilment: the "**ever-moving rest**" (*Amb.* 7).
- What pre-exists in God is the *logos* of each creature, not the creature.
    - So we are "a portion of God" because each of us has a **logos in God**, pre-existing as God's will, not as a pre-existent soul (*Amb.* 7).
- So movement isn't a fall. It's the natural road to the goal.
    - "If a man moves rightly according to his logos, he comes to be in God, in whom the logos of his being pre-exists as its beginning and cause" (*Amb.* 7).
- And his killer point: if souls could get bored of God once, why not again? Then nothing is ever secure.
    - The arguments of *Amb.* 7 in order:
        1. **Nothing created is at rest yet.** God alone is unmoved; everything made from nothing moves toward its cause, and only "the appearance of the ultimate object of desire" can stop it. So no created thing ever was at rest to fall from.
        2. **If they fell once they'd fall forever.** "Anyone who through experience is able even once to spurn something will find no reason to cease from doing so for all eternity."
        3. **Evil as teacher?** If the fall taught them to stay put, then the Beautiful is loved only because of its opposite, and evil becomes "even more beneficial than nature itself", the mother of love. Absurd.
        4. **Becoming comes before motion.** Nothing can move before it exists. No creature is its own end (quoting an "outsider", Aristotle: "the end is that for the sake of which all things exist; it, however, is for the sake of nothing"), perfect in itself, or impassible.
        5. **Scripture**: "Ye are not as yet come to the rest" (Deut 12:9); "I shall be satisfied, when I awake, with thy likeness" (Ps 17:15); "Not as though I had already attained" (Phil 3:12); "These all... received not the promise" (Heb 11:39); "Come unto me... and I will give you rest".
        6. **Satiety** comes either from trivial objects or from repugnant ones. God is neither: He is "infinitely attractive" and increases the appetite of those who enjoy Him.
        7. **Soul and body come to be together**, "the constitutive parts of a single form", separable only in thought. Even after death the soul is "the soul of a particular human being" and the body "the body of a particular man". The soul's survival after death doesn't prove it existed before: the principle of **origin** (when, where, in relation to what) differs from the principle of **being** (what, how).
    - Gregory's sentence isn't about why we were created but about the **misery** that came after the transgression: God made earthly goods unstable so that, being "toyed with and mocked by visible things", we'd look to what is stable (*Amb.* 7.30–35).
    - Why Adam died (*Amb.* 7.32): Adam chose "to be a pile of dust rather than God by grace". God fixed death to the very faculty we misused, so that through pain "we might learn that we have fallen in love with what is not real". *Amb.* 10.28 adds: Adam trusted his senses instead of God and fed on the forbidden fruit instead of the Word, "the bread that came down from heaven", and so "set in motion the whole cycle of bodily nourishment", where eating, meant to support life, feeds death.

## 6. Natural Contemplation

- The logoi are why creation is intelligible. To really know a thing = to see its logos.
- ***Theoria physike***: the stage where the purified mind sees the logoi in things and is led through them to the Logos. (Middle stage of three: practice → natural contemplation → theology.)
    - Five modes of contemplation of creation: **being, motion, difference, mixture, position** (*Amb.* 10.19, the longest *Ambiguum*, on Gregory's sermon about passing through "matter and this fleshly cloud"). The first three lead to knowledge of God as **creator, provider and judge**; the last two lead to virtue and likeness to God.
    - *Amb.* 10.36: whatever moves had a beginning, since every motion has a cause; only the "Prime Mover" is unmoved. So the world isn't beginningless.
    - The saints (Abraham, Moses, Elijah, Melchizedek) as types of the stages of ascent (*Amb.* 10).
- Creation is a kind of scripture and Scripture a kind of cosmos. Both read by looking through the letter to the Logos.
    - In the **Transfiguration**, Christ's garments (white as light) are the **words of Scripture** and the **visible creation**, both made transparent to the Logos (*Amb.* 10).
        - The disciples "crossed over from the flesh to the spirit" before leaving this life. The light of His face was a symbol of His divinity "which transcends intellect, sensation, being, and knowledge" (*Amb.* 10.17).
        - So "the two laws, the natural and the written, are of equal value and equal dignity" (*Amb.* 10.18).
        - The **natural law** is like a book: physical bodies are its letters and syllables; more universal principles are its words, through which the Word is "rendered legible", but only that He is, not what He is.
        - The **written law** is "like another world", with its own heaven, earth and what lies between: theological, natural and ethical philosophy.
        - In both, the visible words or forms are "garments" and the inner meanings or logoi are the "fleshes" of the Word: "He is concealed in His manifestation... and He is manifested through concealment".
        - Two errors: stop at creation and you become a "murderer of the Word", worshipping creation like the pagans; stop at the letter and you get stuck in "bodily matters". Don't be like Potiphar's wife, who "grasped only the garments of Joseph". Or like Mary Magdalene, who took the risen Lord for the gardener.
    - The saints as stages of ascent in *Amb.* 10: Moses crossing the sea and climbing Sinai, the unleavened dough bound in garments (reason kept free from sensible things), Joshua and Jericho, Elijah at Horeb (earthquake, fire and wind as discernment, zeal and faith), Elisha, Hannah and Samuel. **Melchizedek**, "without father, without mother... having neither beginning of days, nor end of life" (Heb 7:3), shows someone who by grace has set aside natural characteristics and become "by grace what the Giver of grace is by nature".

## 7. Why It Matters

- Creation is good and means something. Every creature expresses a divine intention.
- Avoids both pantheism and deism. God is present in everything through the logoi without *being* everything.
- **Theosis** = becoming fully what your logos intends, by sharing in God.
    - Man was made to unite the five divisions of being: uncreated/created, intelligible/sensible, heaven/earth, paradise/inhabited world, male/female (*Amb.* 41).
    - Adam failed; **Christ** unites them all, starting from the last (born of a virgin) and ending with uniting creation to God (the Ascension). See [[Maximus the Confessor as a European Philosopher]] sec. 6.
- **Knowledge.** There are stable universal meanings because things are made according to logoi in the Logos. This is the Orthodox answer to [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]].
- Later theology (Palamas) ties the logoi to the uncreated energies.

Not clear to me yet: are the logoi simply *identical* with the energies, or related to them? People seem to say both.

An answer from the *European Philosopher* volume (Tollefsen and others):
- The logoi are God's **wills** (*thelēmata*) and **intentions** for creatures (sec. 1), so they are on the **energies** side of the essence–energies distinction. They aren't simply identical with "the energies" in general, but they're the energies considered as God's creative and providential **intentions**: the energies **as directed to each thing**.
- Tollefsen's phrase: the logoi are "God's thoughts and wills about creation", and creatures participate in God by being made according to them and moving toward them.
