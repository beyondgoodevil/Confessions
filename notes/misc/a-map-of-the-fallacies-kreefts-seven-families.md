Peter Kreeft's *Socratic Logic* lists 49 **material fallacies** in seven families of seven, the most important first in each family. He chose the large number on purpose: "we list 49, because the more we know, the more we can avoid. Like sins, fallacies are easier to avoid if they are labeled" (p. 68). This note sets out the whole map. For each family there is a table giving each fallacy, what goes wrong in it, one of Kreeft's examples, and links to this notebook's notes on single fallacies where they exist. Kreeft's formal fallacies follow. After that come a key to where the existing fallacy notes fit, and three rival maps given equal weight: Aristotle's original thirteen as Sister Miriam Joseph's *The Trivium* presents them, the three groups in Stephen Layman's *The Power of Logic*, and Anthony Weston's deliberately unclassified list in *A Rulebook for Arguments*. Two shorter sections cover the cases where a "fallacy" turns out to be a good argument, and the short story Kreeft prints in place of exercises.

### 1. What a Fallacy Is and Why Sort Them

Kreeft divides fallacies by where the mistake lies. **Formal fallacies** are "mistakes in reasoning", errors in the third act of the mind (reasoning), as in "Some men are mortal, and some mortals are fish, therefore some men are fish". **Material fallacies** are "mistakes in understanding the meaning or use of terms", errors in the first act of the mind (understanding). They are "not mistakes in the logical form but mistakes in the content or matter or meaning" (p. 68). He covers material fallacies first and at length because they do more damage. Most textbooks "specialize in what they do best: the clear, black-or-white formal fallacies", but a practical text should be ordered "not by the topic's clarity but by its practicality" (p. 68). He also notes a difference in counting. "There is an exact number of formal fallacies, but no exact number of material fallacies", because they overlap, the list can always be extended, and they can be classified in different ways (p. 68).

The three other books define the term in three different ways:

- **The Trivium**: "A fallacy is a violation of logical principle disguised under an appearance of validity; it is an error in process. Falsity is an error in fact." To find a fallacy "is to discover the reason why the mind was deceived into regarding error as truth" (p. 187).
- **Layman**: what makes an error a fallacy is its *psychological* pull. Nobody is fooled by "Two plus 2 equals 4. Therefore, Santa Claus exists", but "other errors in reasoning tend to be psychologically persuasive; these are called fallacies" (p. 121). An informal fallacy is one that does "not involve the explicit use of an invalid form", so exposing it "requires an examination of the argument's content" (p. 122).
- **Weston**: naming a fallacy adds nothing new: "to call something a fallacy is usually just another way of saying that it violates one of the rules for good arguments" (p. 87).

All three also doubt that any classification can be complete:

- **The Trivium**: because one argument can be fallacious in several ways, "a classification of fallacies is neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive" (p. 187).
- **Layman**: "logicians do not agree on the best way to classify them", although sorting them still helps us "see some commonalities among them" (p. 122).
- **Kreeft**: his fallacy chapter has no exercises because "'Name the fallacy' exercises often resemble the game 'Pin the Tail on the Donkey'" (p. 113).
- **Hamblin**, whose criticism lies behind all of this, wrote that the last thing anyone should attempt is "a new classification of the old material, yet another tree" (*Fallacies*, p. 191). I come back to his point in section 13.

### 2. Family One: Fallacies of Language

These are "double vision" fallacies: one set of words is seen as two different things (p. 80). Kreeft's rule for undoing them: "To unmake an ambiguity, make a distinction" (p. 72). He insists that "no term is equivocal itself; a term is only used equivocally", and that "Concepts are not equivocal, only terms" (p. 71).

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1A | **Equivocation** | One term used in two senses within an argument; "the simplest and most common" material fallacy (p. 71) | "Nothing is more expensive than diamonds. But paper is more expensive than nothing" (p. 73) |
| 1B | **Amphiboly** | Ambiguous syntax, not an ambiguous word (p. 74) | Berkeley: "Is it not a great contradiction to think a thing exists when you do not think it?" |
| 1C | **Accent** | Meaning shifted by stress, tone or innuendo (p. 75) | Antony's "And Brutus is an honorable man" |
| 1D | **Slanting** | A "question-begging epithet": a word that describes and evaluates at once (pp. 76–7) | "I am firm, you are stubborn, he is pigheaded" (Russell) |
| 1E | **Slogans** | "There is no fallacy in a slogan as such, but in its use as a substitute for argument" (p. 78) | Rival bumper stickers on abortion |
| 1F | **Hyperbole** | Exaggeration, including "absurd extension" of an opponent's claim (p. 78) | "Oh, so you're against pornography. You must be against freedom of speech." |
| 1G | **Straw man** | Refuting a weak version of the opponent's view (p. 79) | Johnson kicking the stone to "refute" Berkeley |

Kreeft separates absurd extension (1F) from a legitimate *reductio ad absurdum*. A reductio shows that a premise really leads to something everyone knows is false, and it contains "no formal or material fallacy" (p. 79). Against the straw man he cites a medieval debating rule: state the opponent's idea in your own words, "to his satisfaction, before you go on to refute it" (p. 79).

See also [[Word-Concept Fallacy: Biblical Examples]], a close relative of 1A. It confuses a word with the concept the word expresses, the confusion Kreeft's distinction between term and concept is meant to prevent.

### 3. Family Two: Fallacies of Diversion

These seven "turn our attention away from the facts, from reality, to something else". In place of an *argumentum ad rem*, an argument to the point, they argue "ad" something else: the person, authority, threat, pity, shame, popularity or ignorance (p. 80).

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2A | **Ad hominem**, including poisoning the well, *tu quoque* and the genetic fallacy | Attacking the person instead of the issue (pp. 80–81) | The convicted thief who tells the judge, "If I'm a thief, you're a bigger one" |
| 2B | **Ad verecundiam** | "The illegitimate appeal to authority, or the appeal to illegitimate authority" (p. 82) | "There must be something to astrology: my mother swears by it." |
| 2C | **Ad baculum** | Appeal to force or fear in place of reason (p. 83) | "Before you answer, remember who pays your salary." |
| 2D | **Ad misericordiam** | Pity used as "a substitute for argument" (p. 84) | The student who wants an A for having studied hard |
| 2E | **Ad ignominiam** | Appeal to shame (p. 84) | Augustine stealing pears because "I was ashamed to be ashamed" |
| 2F | **Ad populum** | Believing or doing something "only because it is popular" (p. 85), including snob appeal and the Big Lie | "Forty million Frenchmen can't be wrong." |
| 2G | **Ad ignorantiam** | True because not shown false: "Nothing logically follows from nothing" (p. 86) | "He can't prove he earned that money, so he must have stolen it." |

Kreeft calls the **genetic fallacy** "the most common form of ad hominem today". It confuses two senses of "because": a psychological cause of a belief and a logical reason for it (pp. 80–81). If Einstein had found relativity only to give Hitler the bomb, "that would not prove that E does not equal MC2". When each side of a gender argument says the other believes what it does "just because" of its sex, "the two suspicions cancel each other out" (p. 81). Two of the diversions are in his view not mistakes in themselves. "Appeal to authority is not in itself fallacious", since most of what we know comes to us second-hand (p. 82), and pity "is usually a good thing" (p. 84). He also notes that the opposite of the appeal to force, the appeal to desire ("It's true because I want it to be true"), has no technical name but is "perhaps the commonest fallacy of all" (p. 83).

See also [[The Ad Hominem Fallacy]], [[Tu Quoque Fallacy]] and [[The Consensus Fallacy]] (2F).

### 4. Family Three: Fallacies of Oversimplification

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3A | **Dicto simpliciter** | Applying a general rule to a special case without the needed qualification (p. 86) | "Man is a rational animal. Therefore even an idiot can pass a logic course." |
| 3B | **Special case** | The reverse: from a special case to a general rule (p. 87) | "There are a lot of idiots who can't pass a logic course. Therefore man is not rational." |
| 3C | **Composition** | From part to whole (p. 87) | Texas has the most millionaires, so it is the richest state |
| 3D | **Division** | From whole to part (p. 88) | "Irishmen are scattered all over the world. Pat is an Irishman. Therefore Pat is scattered all over the world." |
| 3E | **Black-and-white** | No middle ground between extremes (p. 89) | "Do you hate me?" "No." "How wonderful! You love me!" |
| 3F | **Quoting out of context** | Ignoring the literary or real context (p. 90) | "Judas went and hanged himself" plus "Go and do thou likewise" |
| 3G | **Stereotyping** | No room for the exception (p. 91) | "You're tall; you must play basketball." |

Under 3B Kreeft explains the proverb "the exception proves the rule". It does not mean that an exception proves a rule true. It means that "the exception or special case presupposes the rule. If there is no rule, there can be no exceptions" (p. 87). Under 3F he gives the standard Protestant–Catholic example: setting Paul's "justified by faith" against James's "not by faith alone" ignores the two problems they were answering, legalism for Paul and antinomianism for James (p. 90). Under 3G he warns that "one of the most misused stereotypes in modern discourse is that of the stereotype", since every generalization now gets called one (p. 91).

See also [[Appeal to Generality]], a claim too sweeping to test, which is related to 3A and 3G.

### 5. Family Four: Fallacies of Argumentation

Kreeft calls these "fallacies of logical strategy", like bad military strategy. They are close to formal fallacies but still material. His test: "a computer could infallibly detect formal fallacies but not material fallacies" (p. 92).

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4A | **Non sequitur** | The conclusion simply does not follow; "the master fallacy of this group" (p. 92) | "I hate courthouses. Therefore you will lose your case in court today." |
| 4B | **Ignoratio elenchi** | Reasons that prove a different conclusion; "missing the point" (p. 94) | Chamberlain: peace is better than war, so appease Hitler |
| 4C | **Begging the question** | "Smuggling the conclusion back into the premises, usually under different words" (p. 94) | Molière's doctor: opium makes you sleep because of its "dormative virtue" |
| 4D | **Complex question** | A question that cannot be answered without begging another (p. 95) | "Who made God?" assumes God was made |
| 4E | **Arguing in a circle** | Using the conclusion to prove the premise that proved it (p. 95) | Chesterton: peasants are credulous because they see ghosts, and their ghosts are dismissed because peasants are credulous |
| 4F | **Contradictory premises** | Usually "using reason to attack reason" (p. 97) | "There are absolutely no absolutes." |
| 4G | **False assumption** | Like begging the question but "more covert"; the basis of many jokes (p. 99) | "That ain't my dog." |

Kreeft's distinction in 4A is useful. "The sky is blue. The sea is blue. Therefore the sky is the sea" is a formal fallacy, because the form is invalid. "Grass is green. I feel depressed today. Therefore the grass is to blame" is a material non sequitur, because nothing in the content connects premises and conclusion (pp. 92–3). Two of his examples are open to dispute. He treats Descartes' *cogito* as begging the question, since "I think" already assumes the "I" (p. 94), and he cites Descartes' proof of God from clear and distinct ideas as a circle (p. 96).

See also [[A Circular Argument]] and [[The Fallacy of Circular Reasoning]] (4C, 4E), and [[The Appeal to Irrelevance Fallacy]], the red herring, which falls under 4B.

### 6. Family Five: Inductive Fallacies

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5A | **Hasty generalization** | Too few or unrepresentative examples; "a matter of degree and a common-sense 'judgment call'" (p. 100) | "All the swans we've ever seen were white" |
| 5B | **Post hoc** | "After this, therefore because of this" (p. 100) | The lemon peel that keeps the alligators away: "See? It works." |
| 5C | **Hypothesis contrary to fact** | The "if only" fallacy: "We cannot know what isn't, only what is" (p. 101) | The chain from Italian clocks to "the only reason we are not Nazis" |
| 5D | **False analogy** | "Analogies do not prove anything", and this fallacy assumes they do (p. 102) | Mill's "visible / desirable" |
| 5E | **Argument from silence** | Silence about *x* taken to show disbelief in *x*, or that there is no *x* (p. 103) | A writer never mentions her husband, so she was unmarried |
| 5F | **Selective evidence** | Citing only the supporting data; "the most basic of all mistakes for a scientist" (p. 103) | Pessimists' and optimists' rival lists |
| 5G | **Slanting the question** | Poll wording that fixes the answer (p. 104) | The two ways of asking about abortion |

Under **post hoc**, Kreeft lists the alternatives to the first event being the cause: coincidence, a third cause of both, or the second event causing the first (p. 101). Under **false analogy**, he separates using an analogy that is not a real resemblance from using a real analogy falsely, by assuming that things alike in one way are alike in another. He also includes a deliberate false analogy from Scripture. Jesus' "It is not fair to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs" was meant to test the Canaanite woman, and "he was pleased when she detected and refuted his fallacy by extending his analogy" (Matt. 15:21–28; pp. 102–3). Under **argument from silence**, he notes that the law already recognizes the fallacy: Thomas More's "silence betokeneth nothing", and the Fifth Amendment (p. 103).

See also [[Hasty Generalization Fallacy]] (5A).

### 7. Family Six: Procedural Fallacies

Kreeft says these "violate basic logical justice. The arguments may be formally valid, but they are used, or treated, wrongly". Textbooks seldom discuss them, and they "do not have technical titles" (p. 104). Hamblin's dialectical approach (section 13) would put this family at the centre.

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 6A | **"Refuting" an argument by refuting its conclusion** | "All offense and no defense": setting up a counter-argument while leaving the original standing (pp. 104–5) |
| 6B | **Assuming that refuting an argument refutes its conclusion** | "All defense and no offense": "There will always be some bad arguments for true conclusions" (p. 105) |
| 6C | **Ignoring the argument** | Waiting your turn and then talking past it; Marx in the *Manifesto*; "When your point is weak, holler like hell" (p. 106) |
| 6D | **Substituting explanations for proofs** | A proof "slammed the door shut"; an explanation only opens it (pp. 106–7) |
| 6E | **Answering another argument than the one given** | Slaying "the wrong enemy" (p. 107) |
| 6F | **Shifting the burden of proof** | Changing midway who must prove what (p. 108) |
| 6G | **Winning the argument but losing the arguer, or vice versa** | "Good logic is an instrument of persuasion; it is only one ingredient in a larger interpersonal situation" (p. 108) |

Under 6A Kreeft discusses Kant's antinomies, where reason seems to prove both sides of a question. His reply: "two contradictory conclusions cannot both be true, therefore they cannot both be proved by perfectly good arguments", so at least one side must contain a mistake, usually an ambiguous term (p. 105). Under 6F he separates protocol from logic. In science an idea is "guilty until proved innocent". In conversation it is "innocent until proved guilty": if your aunt says the diner has the best apple pie, you do not say "Prove it" (p. 108). The burden becomes a matter of logic only when it is shifted silently partway through a debate, and who carries it should be "agreed on before argument proceeds" (p. 108). Under 6G his example is from *A Man for All Seasons*. Norfolk asks More to sign "for friendship's sake?", and More replies by asking whether, when More goes to Hell for disobeying his conscience, Norfolk will come with him "for friendship's sake" (p. 109).

See also [[Moving the Goalpost]] (6F, changing the standard of proof after it has been met) and [[Ad Hoc Claim]] (6D, an explanation invented to rescue a position). [[Double Standard Fallacy]] and [[Double Standard Fallacy 2]] are not on Kreeft's list. They come closest to 6F and to the medieval rule behind 1G: the same standard must apply to both sides.

### 8. Family Seven: Metaphysical Fallacies

Most textbooks leave these out. Kreeft includes them because "metaphysics is at the root of the other divisions of philosophy" (p. 109).

| | Fallacy | What goes wrong | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7A | **Reductionism, "nothing buttery"** | Reducing form to matter; "Whenever we hear the phrase 'nothing but,' a bell of suspicion should ring" (p. 109) | "In our world a star is only a big ball of gas." "Even in your world, that is not what a star is, only what it is made of." (Lewis) |
| 7B | **Accident** | Treating the accidental as essential, or the essential as accidental (p. 110) | Racism, which treats race as if it were species |
| 7C | **Quantity for quality** | Measuring what cannot be measured, or mystifying numbers (p. 111) | IQ tests as a measure of wisdom |
| 7D | **Misplaced concreteness** | Treating an abstraction as a concrete thing (Whitehead's phrase) (p. 111) | "Telescopic philanthropy": loving Humanity, not people |
| 7E | **Confusing logical, physical and psychological causes** | The confusion behind the genetic fallacy (p. 112, referring to p. 81) | See 2A |
| 7F | **Existential fallacy** | Confusing essence and existence (p. 112) | Treating existence as a perfection in one version of the ontological argument |
| 7G | **The natural for the common** | Mistaking what is usual for what belongs to a nature (p. 113) | Wanting property is natural; wanting more than we can use is only common |

Kreeft's examples here reflect his Thomist commitments, and he admits some are contested. In a footnote he says that his two examples of misplaced concreteness, Plato's Forms and humanism's worship of "humanity", are "controversial, and some would defend these two examples as not fallacious at all" (p. 111 n.). He also observes that each era overrates what it is good at. Ancient thought tended to give numbers qualities, and modern thought tends to reduce qualities to numbers (p. 111).

### 9. The Formal Fallacies

Kreeft takes up formal fallacies later in the book, as "fallacies of logical form", meaning faults in "the relationship between, or arrangement of, terms and propositions" (p. 194). They are few and exact:

| Rule broken | Fallacy | Kreeft's example |
|---|---|---|
| A syllogism has three and only three terms | **Four terms**, including **ambiguous middle** (p. 243) | "All power tends to corrupt" (Acton) + "Knowledge is power" (Bacon) → "knowledge tends to corrupt" |
| The middle term must be distributed at least once | **Undistributed middle** (p. 243) | |
| No term undistributed in a premise may be distributed in the conclusion | **Illicit major / illicit minor** (p. 243) | |
| No syllogism with two negative premises | **Two negative premises** (p. 243) | |
| A universal affirmative cannot be converted simply | **Illicit conversion** (pp. 168–9) | "All great models use [this lipstick]", implying that all who use it are great models |
| *If p then q* cannot be run backwards | **Affirming the consequent**, **denying the antecedent** (pp. 292–3) | "If the wind blows, the paper falls" |
| *p or q* does not exclude both | **Affirming a disjunct** (pp. 301–2) | "Either he is a villain or a fool, and he is a villain, therefore he is not a fool" |

Kreeft points out that the two hypothetical fallacies are the same mistake: "they begin with p ⊃ q and then proceed as if it were q ⊃ p" (p. 293). **The Trivium** makes the same connection in Aristotle's terms. Its "fallacy of consequent" is a material fallacy that underlies all of these formal ones: the false assumption that "an A proposition is convertible simply". It is "one of the most frequent causes of error in reasoning" (pp. 200–201). Formal errors and errors of content are therefore not two separate kinds. **Layman** shows the reverse case: an equivocation can disguise an invalid form ("A; if B, then C; so, C") as modus ponens (p. 122).

### 11. Three Rival Maps

**Aristotle's map, in The Trivium.** Sister Miriam Joseph keeps the oldest arrangement (pp. 188–206). Six fallacies are *in dictione* (dependent on language), "occasioned by a hidden assumption not conveyed in the language": equivocation, amphiboly, composition, division, accent and verbal form. Seven are *extra dictionem* (outside language), each with "a hidden false assumption not warranted by the language": accident, secundum quid, consequent, ignoratio elenchi, false cause, begging the question and complex question. Two features differ from Kreeft. First, she treats all the language fallacies as "special instances of the fallacy of four terms", so they are formal and material at the same time (p. 188). Second, she places all the arguments "ad" under ignoratio elenchi. *Ignoratio elenchi* means "ignorance of the nature of refutation", and an argument that evades the issue is the opposite of an *argumentum ad rem*. She divides them by rhetoric's modes of persuasion. Ad hominem is an "unsound *ethos*", an appeal through the speaker's character, while ad populum, misericordiam, baculum and ignorantiam are unsound *pathos*, appeals to the audience's feelings (pp. 202–4). Kreeft took his name for the opposite of these fallacies, "ad rem", from the same tradition. Her account of accent is wider than his. She files quoting out of context under it, with the example of the Psalm in which the Bible does say "There is no God", but as the words of "the fool" (p. 191).

**Layman's map.** *The Power of Logic* uses three groups, defined by *how* each argument fails:

1. **Irrelevant premises** (pp. 122–31): ad hominem (abusive, circumstantial and *tu quoque*), straw man, ad baculum, ad populum, ad misericordiam and ad ignorantiam. These are premises that are "logically irrelevant" but "for psychological reasons ... may seem relevant".
2. **Ambiguity** (pp. 137–42): equivocation, amphiboly, composition and division.
3. **Unwarranted assumptions** (pp. 145–55): begging the question, false dilemma, appeal to unreliable authority, false cause (with slippery slope as a variety) and complex question. Each relies on an assumption that "stands in need of support".

Layman's grouping puts some fallacies where Kreeft does not. Ad verecundiam moves from diversion to unwarranted assumption: the authority is not irrelevant, its reliability has simply been assumed (p. 150). Composition and division move from oversimplification to ambiguity, because they get "persuasive force from a confusion of concepts", for instance between grizzly bears as a group and as individuals (pp. 140–42). His examples are also philosophically pointed:

- He reads "God is love; therefore love is God" as an equivocation on "is", which can mean "has the attribute of" or "is identical with" (p. 137).
- He reads the argument from foreknowledge to necessity in Augustine's *On Free Choice of the Will* as an amphiboly over the scope of "necessarily" (p. 139). See [[Free Will and Divine Foreknowledge]].
- He notes that the inference from "each part of the universe is dependent" to "the universe is dependent" is a disputed case of composition: "This controversy has yet to be settled" (p. 141).

**Weston's list.** *A Rulebook for Arguments* refuses to classify at all. Its appendix lists 23 fallacies alphabetically, from *ad hominem* to *straw person*, and sends the reader back to the rule each one breaks (pp. 87–94). It adds some names Kreeft lacks: **mere redescription**, which is broadly a form of begging the question but "more helpful to recognize ... as a separate fallacy"; **overlooking alternatives**; **persuasive definition**; and **red herring** (pp. 91–4). It also treats **non sequitur** as a catch-all, "a very general term for a bad argument. Try to figure out specifically what is supposed to be wrong with it" (p. 92). Weston's practical advice is the reverse of Kreeft's attitude to names: you learn more by asking which rule an argument breaks than by finding a label for it.

### 12. When the "Fallacy" Is a Good Argument

All four books (Kreeft, the *Trivium*, Layman and Weston) agree that several fallacies on the list are corruptions of good arguments, and they draw the line in much the same places:

- **Authority.** Kreeft lists five ways an appeal to authority goes wrong: the authority is irrelevant, unreliable, unnecessary, treated dogmatically, or trusted uncritically (p. 82). The *Trivium* says it is "perfectly legitimate to supplement reasoning with authority (*argumentum ad auctoritatem*), but it is fallacious to substitute authority for reasoning in matters capable of being understood by reason" (p. 204). Layman and Weston agree, adding that "Expertise in one area doesn't necessarily 'rub off' on another" (Layman, p. 150) and that authorities may be disqualified if they are uninformed or partial (Weston, p. 87).
- **The person.** Criticizing a person's character "is certainly a relevant consideration", and the fallacy is in substituting the attack for the argument (Kreeft, p. 80). The *Trivium* agrees: a witness known to have lied in court may properly be doubted (p. 203).
- **Pity.** An appeal to pity is not fallacious when the facts that arouse pity are "also logically relevant to the conclusion", as in an appeal to relieve famine (Layman, p. 129).
- **Ignorance and burden of proof.** The presumption of innocence "is not a principle of logic". It is a legal safeguard meant to avoid punishing the innocent (Layman, p. 131). For Kreeft, who bears the burden of proof "is not a matter of logic but of personal protocol" until someone shifts it partway through a debate (p. 108).

Hamblin had made the same point against the textbooks: "Argument from authority has been mentioned in lists of valid argument-forms as often as in lists of Fallacies" (*Fallacies*, p. 43).

### 13. Hamblin's Warning

Hamblin's *Fallacies* (see [[Hamblin's Fallacies: The Standard Treatment and Formal Dialectic]]) raises a question about every map in this note. On his account the list is mostly inherited. Aristotle's thirteen survive in modern textbooks, and "the genre was invented by Locke", whose four arguments "ad" were meant as a survey of ways of winning assent, not a list of faults (pp. 41, 160–61). The names multiplied in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Kreeft's 49, Layman's 15 and Weston's 23 are three recent arrangements of the same inherited material.

Hamblin's own proposal explains why the arrangements disagree. Many fallacies are not invalid inferences but faults in a dialogue, and different dialogues have different rules. Kreeft's sixth family is the closest any of these books comes to that view. His procedural fallacies concern who must answer what, when, and how a refutation has to be carried out, and he notes that the arguments in them "may be formally valid" (p. 104). Read through Hamblin, those seven are what the others have in common: each is a fault that can only be seen in a dialogue between people.

### 14. "Love Is a Fallacy"

In place of exercises, Kreeft reprints Max Shulman's comic story "Love Is a Fallacy", which "will stick with you, as a 'big picture,' longer than many little flea-like exercises" (p. 113). A vain eighteen-year-old law student trades his father's old raccoon coat to his faddish roommate in exchange for the roommate's girl, Polly. He then spends five evenings teaching her logic so that she will make a suitable wife for a future lawyer. His lessons follow Kreeft's list: dicto simpliciter, hasty generalization, post hoc, contradictory premises, ad misericordiam, false analogy, hypothesis contrary to fact and poisoning the well. When he finally proposes, she names the fallacy in each move he makes, and turns him down for the roommate, whose raccoon coat is her reason.

The story's irony fits this note. Polly learns to label fallacies perfectly, but the labels win her no wisdom and leave her choice exactly where fashion put it. The narrator commits Kreeft's sixth-family fault 6G: he wins every argument and loses the person. His plan was itself the coldest of calculations. The story makes in fiction the point Hamblin argues and Weston gives as advice. A name for a fallacy is a move in an argument between people, and knowing the names is not the same as reasoning well.

### Conclusion

Kreeft's map is the fullest of the four: 49 material fallacies in seven families of seven, plus the formal fallacies of the syllogism and the conditional. Its families describe *how* a mind goes wrong. It sees double (language), looks away (diversion), flattens what it sees (oversimplification), takes a bad route to the conclusion (argumentation), generalizes too fast (induction), cheats in the exchange (procedure), or mistakes what a thing is (metaphysics). The rival maps show how much depends on the chosen principle. Aristotle, in the *Trivium*, sorts by whether the trick lies in the language. Layman sorts by the way the premises fail. Weston declines to sort and points back to the rules. All four, and Hamblin behind them, agree on two things. Many "fallacies" are good arguments misused. And learning the names, as Polly does in Shulman's story, does not by itself make anyone reason better.
