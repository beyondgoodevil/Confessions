## What is Skepticism?

Skepticism is an epistemological position that doubts or questions the possibility of knowledge. Skeptics argue that humans cannot achieve certainty about the external world, other minds, or even the validity of our perceptions and reasoning processes. In its extreme form, skepticism can claim that we know nothing for sure, or that knowledge is fundamentally inaccessible to us.

There are different types of skepticism, such as:

1. **Global Skepticism**: The belief that it is impossible to know anything at all, including knowledge of the external world or our own minds.
2. **Local Skepticism**: Doubts about certain kinds of knowledge, such as skepticism about the existence of the external world, the reliability of sense perception, or the possibility of metaphysical knowledge.

McCain: skeptics usually don't say our beliefs are **false**, only that our reasons aren't **good enough** to count as knowledge. And most epistemologists aren't skeptics; they study skepticism because some serious thinkers are, and because the scenarios show what knowledge actually requires.

**BonJour's four ways skepticisms differ** (ch. 12):
1. **Target**: which condition of knowledge is attacked (belief, truth, justification, the anti-Gettier condition). The only interesting one is **justification**: do we have good reasons to think our beliefs are true?
2. **Scope**: everything, or one area. The most threatening kind grants the Cartesian foundation (our own conscious states and self-evident truths) and denies we can get from there to the external world.
3. **Strength**: "not *conclusively* justified" is trivially true; "not justified at all" is too strong to defend. The real challenge is in between: are our beliefs **strongly** justified, likely enough to be knowledge?
4. **Character**: a positive thesis argued from premises, or a purely negative challenge. The negative skeptic "practices... the intellectual equivalent of guerrilla warfare, burning and pillaging, but not attempting to build anything". A global negative skepticism can't be refuted without begging the question, but that doesn't make it correct; it depends on who has the **burden of proof**.

## Reductio Against Skepticism

1. **Self-Defeating Argument**: If skepticism is correct and we cannot know anything, then the skeptic’s statement itself—that "we cannot know anything"—would also be unknowable. This leads to a **self-defeating** position: if we cannot know anything, then we cannot even know that we can't know anything, which undermines the very foundation of the skeptical claim.
2. **Impossibility of Knowledge**: Skepticism, especially in its extreme forms, leads to the conclusion that knowledge is impossible. If we cannot know anything with certainty, then all beliefs, including skepticism itself, are subject to doubt. This paradoxical conclusion suggests that adopting skepticism as a philosophy undermines the possibility of any coherent knowledge, making it a **logical contradiction**.

Critics ancient and modern have pressed this charge. The Pyrrhonist dodge is the best answer to it: they claim not to **believe** skepticism, only to find themselves suspending judgement (see Ancient Skepticism below).

## Ancient Skepticism

| | **Pyrrhonian** (Pyrrho, Sextus Empiricus) | **Academic** (Arcesilaus, Carneades: Plato's Academy) |
|---|---|---|
| claim | suspend judgement (*epochē*) on everything, including whether knowledge is possible | nothing can be known (with certainty); some things are more **probable** |
| goal | **tranquillity** (*ataraxia*): stop worrying about what's true | avoid rash assent |
| method | set equal arguments against each other (*isostheneia*) | criticise the Stoic criterion of truth |
| reply to "you assert skepticism" | Pyrrhonists don't assert anything; they report how things seem | Academics do seem to assert one thing |

## Skeptical Arguments

**Agrippa's trilemma**: Any belief needs a reason. That reason needs a reason. So either:
1. **infinite regress**: reasons go on forever
2. **circularity**: eventually a reason depends on the belief itself
3. **dogmatic stopping point**: an unjustified starting point

All three seem unsatisfactory. This is the root of **foundationalism** (option 3, with self-evident foundations), **coherentism** (a kind of 2) and **infinitism** (1). See [[Criterion Problem]].

**Descartes's dreaming argument**: I can't tell for sure I'm not dreaming; so I can't know I'm sitting here. **Evil demon**: even maths could be a deception. See [[Descartes: The Cogito and Mind-Body Dualism]].

**Brain in a vat** (Putnam; BonJour ch. 7, 12):
1. If I know I have hands, I know I'm not a handless brain in a vat being fed experiences.
2. I can't know I'm not a brain in a vat (my experiences would be identical).
3. So I don't know I have hands.

**Hume's inductive skepticism**: no non-circular justification for expecting the future to resemble the past. See [[Hume on Causation and Induction]].

- Premise 1 of the brain-in-a-vat argument rests on **epistemic closure**: if I know P, and know that P entails Q, I know Q. Hard to deny: what's the point of knowledge if you can't reason from it? Premise 2 rests on the fact that my evidence would be exactly the same in the vat.

**More scenarios** (McCain, nos. 11–17):
- **Dream vs demon**. Unlike demons, we really have had vivid dreams, even dreams within dreams. And dreaming is a different threat: I might be dreaming I hold a book while actually holding one. So skeptical scenarios don't need our beliefs to be **false** to undermine knowledge (Sosa).
- **Russell's five-minute-old universe**: the world popped into existence five minutes ago complete with fake memories and fossils. That gives skepticism about the **past**, and also about **memory**: you can't check memory without using memory, like a car salesman who says "just ask me whether I'm trustworthy".
- **The problem of the criterion**: Descartes's apple-sorting. To know *which* apples are good you need a test; to know the test works you need some good apples. Same for "what do we know?" and "how do we tell?", for ethics, and for truth in general. Chisholm's options: **particularism** (start from cases), **methodism** (start from a criterion), or **skepticism**. A newer option starts from intuitions on both and works toward **reflective equilibrium**.
- **The debasing demon** (Schaffer, 2010): doesn't fake your evidence but makes your beliefs not **based** on it, so you believe the right things for the wrong reasons. Replies: it only threatens knowing that you know (the "KK principle", widely rejected); or we have plenty of evidence our beliefs are properly based; or it's actually impossible.
- **Lottery skepticism**: you don't know your ticket will lose, yet you "know" you can't afford a Lamborghini, which entails it will lose. The same goes for knowing where your car is (it might have been towed) or what you'll be doing in an hour (you might die suddenly). Replies: knowledge needs an explanatory link between the fact and the belief; purely statistical evidence doesn't license outright belief; deny closure; contextualism.
- **"You only believe that because..."**: you'd be Muslim if born in Saudi Arabia, an externalist if you'd studied at Rutgers. Replies:
    - sensitivity and safety aren't required for knowledge (Bogardus)
    - **permissivism**: the same evidence can rationally support different attitudes
    - only worry if you have independent reason to think you're wrong (Vavova)
    - some beliefs are "epistemically self-promoting": the belief itself gives reason to trust how it was formed, a reply also used for religious belief (Moon)

## Answers

Besides the self-refutation charge (the reductio above):

| Answer | Who | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Moorean** | G. E. Moore | "here is one hand." I'm more certain I have hands than of any premise in the skeptic's argument. So reject premise 2 of the brain-in-a-vat argument |
| **Denial of closure** | Dretske, Nozick | you can know you have hands without knowing you're not a BIV. You only need to rule out **relevant alternatives**: in the closest worlds where I have no hands, I have stumps or hooks, not a vat |
| **Hinge propositions** | Wittgenstein, Wright | some things (I'm not deceived by a demon) we're simply **entitled** to, given their role in all our thinking |
| **Matrix as metaphysics** | Chalmers | if you found you were in the Matrix, your beliefs about tables wouldn't be false; you'd learn that tables are, at bottom, computational patterns |
| **Externalism** | reliabilists, Plantinga | knowledge doesn't require being able to **prove** you know; it requires that your belief actually be reliably formed. If I'm not a brain in a vat, I know I have hands, even if I can't prove it |
| **Contextualism** | DeRose, Lewis | "know" has higher standards in the philosophy room than in ordinary life. Both "I know" and "I don't know" can be true in different contexts |
| **Semantic externalism** | Putnam | a real brain in a vat couldn't even think "I am a brain in a vat", since its word "vat" refers to vat-images. So the hypothesis is self-refuting |
| **Common sense** | Thomas Reid | the skeptic trusts reason to doubt the senses; but both come from the same maker. If you trust one, trust the other |
| **Inference to the best explanation** | BonJour | the real-world hypothesis explains the order of our experience better than the demon or the vat |

**BonJour's verdicts** (ch. 12):
- **Particularism** (Moore, Chisholm) gives common sense too absolute a weight and so rules out skepticism by fiat. But common sense does create "a fairly strong rational presumption" against skepticism, because there's no other starting point big enough for philosophy to get a grip. It's still only a presumption: it could be defeated if epistemologists kept failing to say *what* the justification is.
- **Denying closure** fails: if my knowing "I have hands" is compatible with my being a handless BIV, "what exactly do I know when I know that I have hands?"
- **Contextualism**: it doesn't really preserve closure (I can't even think "I'm not a BIV" without raising the standards). And it makes the skeptic and ordinary people talk past each other, but their disagreement doesn't feel merely verbal.
- **Pragmatism** ("justified if it works"): that a belief leads to success is itself a claim about the external world, so it begs the question.
- **Externalism** gives only a hypothetical answer: *if* the right conditions hold, we know, but we're given no reason to think they do. **Quine**'s naturalism "changes the subject".
- **Rorty**'s "epistemological behaviorism" (justified = accepted by your community) avoids skepticism only by abandoning truth, and can't call itself true.
- His conclusion: "a successful defense of something close to the Cartesian approach is really the only significant alternative to skepticism".

**BonJour's explanatory argument** (ch. 7):
- Following Peirce's "abduction": a hypothesis can be justified as the **best explanation** of the data, causal claims included, as atomic theory is justified by cloud-chamber tracks and fogged film.
- Our experience has a specific order. There are repeatable, reversible, intersecting "**sensory routes**", exactly what you'd program into a computer game simulating objects in space. And it has the gaps you'd expect if we were moving through a world of objects (eyes closed, asleep, poor light).
- The rival hypotheses (Berkeley's God, the demon, the vat computer) can only explain the *specific* features of experience by **emulating** what a material world would produce. So they need two claims: that a material world would produce this experience, *and* that God or the computer can successfully mimic it. Realism needs only the first. A conjunction is less probable than either part.
- His analogy: my truck is gone and only my wife has the key. "She drove it" beats "an intruder kidnapped her and made her drive it", since the second contains the first plus more.
- He admits open questions: whether some rival can avoid parasitism on the material-world hypothesis, and how much probability the argument really gives.
