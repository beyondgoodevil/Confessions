> "Many other signs truly did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book: But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God." (John 20:30–31)

> "And if Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain." (1 Cor 15:14)

Hume says he has found an argument against miracle stories that will be, "with the wise and learned, an everlasting check to all kinds of superstitious delusion". For two centuries this was the standard reason educated people gave for not taking the Gospel miracles seriously. Two books in the library take it apart: Houston's *Reported Miracles* (1994), a careful philosophical critique, and Earman's *Hume's Abject Failure* (2000), by a philosopher of science who is not a believer.

## 1. Background

- **Locke** (*Essay* IV.16) had said we weigh testimony by "common observation in like cases" and by "particular testimonies". Then, without argument, he exempted miracles from the usual rules for weighing evidence. Houston calls this "special pleading". Hume's essay can be read as Locke turned round: apply the ordinary rules and miracles lose. Locke's "king of Siam", who disbelieved in ice, becomes Hume's "Indian prince".
- **Tillotson** had argued against transubstantiation that the apostles' senses could not be more certain than ours, which tell us the bread is bread. Hume opens by praising this "decisive argument" and claims to have found one "of a like nature" against all miracles.
- Earman stresses that there was nothing new in it. The 18th-century British debate (Woolston, Sherlock's *Tryal of the Witnesses*, Annet, Middleton) had already made most of these moves. As Wootton put it, "it is harder to identify what is original in Hume's essay than it is to find sources for… his argument."

## 2. The Argument

**Part I: the "proof"**
- "A wise man… proportions his belief to the evidence." Where experience has been uniform we have a **proof**; where it has varied, a **probability**.
- We trust testimony only because we have found witnesses usually reliable. There is no a priori link between testimony and truth.
- A **miracle** is "a violation of the laws of nature", or more exactly "a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent".
- Laws of nature rest on "a firm and unalterable experience". So "the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined."
- **The Maxim**: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish; and even in that case there is a mutual destruction of arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of force, which remains, after deducting the inferior."

**Part II: "we have been a great deal too liberal"**
1. No miracle has ever had enough witnesses "of such unquestioned good-sense, education, and learning" in a public enough place.
2. People love wonder. "The passion of surprise and wonder… gives a sensible tendency towards the belief of those events."
3. Miracle stories "abound among ignorant and barbarous nations".
4. **Contrary miracles**: miracles back rival religions, so they cancel each other, like "the Caesarean and Pompeian factions" each claiming the victory.

**The test cases**
- Vespasian curing a blind man with spittle (Tacitus). The Saragossa leg restored with holy oil (Cardinal de Retz). The many cures at the tomb of the Jansenist Abbé Pâris, attested by crowds and never disproved. Of these last: "what have we to oppose to such a cloud of witnesses, but the **absolute impossibility** or miraculous nature of the events, which they relate? And this surely, in the eyes of all reasonable people, will alone be regarded as a sufficient refutation."
- He grants that eight days of universal darkness in 1600, testified everywhere, could be believed, since it serves no religion. But a well-attested resurrection of Queen Elizabeth could not: "the knavery and folly of men are such common phenomena".
- So: "no human testimony can have such force as to prove a miracle, and make it a just foundation for any such system of religion."

**The parting shot**
- "The Christian Religion not only was at first attended with miracles, but even at this day cannot be believed by any reasonable person without one… whoever is moved by Faith to assent to it, is conscious of a continued miracle in his own person, which subverts all the principles of his understanding."

## 3. The Old Reply: Question-Begging (Paley, Lewis)

- **Paley**: Hume "suppresses all those circumstances of power, and disposition of the Deity". On Hume's terms miracles are "alike incredible to him who is previously assured of the constant agency of a Divine Being, and to him who believes that no such being exists".
- **C. S. Lewis**, *Miracles*: "we know the experience against them to be uniform only if we know that all the reports of them are false."
- **Houston's verdict**: as a reply to Hume, both miss.
  - Paley assumes theism, but Hume is addressing an apologist arguing to an unbeliever, who cannot assume God.
  - Lewis reads "uniform experience" as *all* experience including the disputed case, which would make miracles impossible by definition. Hume's actual argument is an **inductive inference** about a disputed case from all the *other* cases. That is not circular in itself.

## 4. Houston's Real Objections

**(a) The evidence for the law is not obviously relevant**
- Hume puts on the scale against "Jesus walked on the water" all the evidence for Archimedes' principle and the density of human bodies. That evidence counts only if we have **already excluded** that a god may have acted on this occasion. If there is any reason to think God might have, "a body of evidence, however vast, in favour of Archimedes' Principle… cannot without further argument be taken as relevant".
- So Hume "reaches his conclusion… only by assuming that no god has acted miraculously". For those who already assume that, Part I is unnecessary. "For those who do not make it the argument has no force."

**The Humean comeback, and the answer**
- *If God can interrupt nature, induction collapses, and the apologist needs induction to weigh testimony.* Houston: this holds only if the alternative to perfect regularity is a capricious or deceiving spirit. There is a **third possibility**. The same God who upholds the order of nature acts rarely, **for a recognisable purpose**: to endorse a prophet, or to signal the meaning of events. Such signs need order as their background. Only an atheist picture leaves "perfect regularity or chaos" as the only options. (Here he uses E. J. Craig's point that a hidden world-picture can quietly supply premises.)
- *Then what non-question-begging reason can the apologist give that God may have acted?* He doesn't need to assume God first. Houston's analogy is a man seen at 2 a.m. near a warehouse fire, who looks like Gordon Allan, a shareholder in the failing family firm. You don't assume arson to take "arson" seriously as a **promising hypothesis** that makes sense of many facts at once. "A god may have acted" can be promising in the same way, if it explains the testimony and its circumstances better than "credulity and delusion" does.

**(b) An improbable event does not make an improbable report**
- Hume slides from "the event is unusual" to "the report is therefore probably false". Whether reports of the extraordinary are less reliable is itself an **empirical** question he never investigates.
- Some witnesses are *more* careful precisely when what they report is strange. His example is the archetypal Edinburgh solicitor who won't admit it is a fine day in June. Careless errors may be commoner in reports of the mundane.

## 5. Earman: "An Abject Failure"

Earman is not a Christian apologist, and he says so. His verdict: "Of Miracles" is "a confection of rhetoric and *schein Geld*" (sham money).
- **Unoriginal**: the essay is mostly warmed-over material from the British debate. What is distinctive "does not stand up to scrutiny".
- **The straight rule**: Hume's "proof" assumes that uniform experience gives a law probability **1**. Then a miracle has probability **0**, and "for any further evidence… either Pr(M) = 0 or Pr(M) is not defined". This is "very simple. And very crude." It works as well against new physics as against resurrections. Particle physicists have never seen a proton decay, but they don't give that probability 1. If they did, building detectors to look for decay would be irrational.
- **Richard Price**, Hume's contemporary, answered with the Bayes–Laplace rule: a die that lands the same way a million times gives "only a probability, not a certainty" that it will do so again.
- **Multiple independent witnesses**: with even modest assumptions of independence and reliability, Bayes' theorem shows that enough independent witnesses can make an improbable event highly probable. Their combined reliability can "converge to certainty". Hume's "subtraction" of one probability from another is crude arithmetic that Campbell (*Dissertation on Miracles*, 1762) already attacked.
- **The Jansenist case shows the game**: faced with "clouds of witnesses", Hume falls back on "absolute impossibility". That is the straight rule again, and it makes his testimony arguments idle.
- **His conclusion for theists**: there is "considerable comfort" in the *manner* of Hume's failure. Rational belief in natural religion can proceed inductively, "as it does in science and everyday life on the basis of observation and eyewitness testimony". Whether it ends in belief depends on one's prior probabilities. "In this respect quarks are no better off than Gods."

## 6. Augustine and Aquinas: What a Miracle Is

- **Augustine**: "a portent… happens not contrary to nature, but contrary to what is known of nature" (*City of God* XXI.8). God implanted "causal reasons" (*rationes causales*) in creation, and a miracle may be nature's own latent powers brought out suddenly by God. The daily order (seasons, seeds growing) is no less wonderful; miracles arouse a wonder habit has dulled.
- **Aquinas**: God cannot act against the order that depends on Him as first cause, "for, if He did so, He would act against His foreknowledge, or His will, or His goodness". But He can act outside the order of **secondary causes**, "for He is not subject to the order of secondary causes; but, on the contrary, this order is subject to Him" (ST I q.105 a.6). A miracle is what is done by God "outside the order of the whole created nature", and so causes wonder (a.7).
- Neither speaks of "violation". The very definition Hume attacks is a Deist and mechanist one.

## 8. Assessment

*Mine.*

**The essay's real premise is a picture of nature**
- Hume's definition, "a violation of the laws of nature by a particular volition of the Deity", already assumes the world of Malebranche, the Deists and Newton's mechanism. Nature is a closed machine of laws, and God is an outside agent who sometimes breaks in. Remove the Deist picture and the argument loses its grip. In Augustine and Aquinas the same God who gives the order gives the sign. A miracle no more "violates" nature than a king violates his own law by pardoning. See [[Malebranche: Occasionalism and Seeing All Things in God]] and [[Hume on Causation and Induction]].
- There is a bigger irony. Hume's own scepticism about causation, that we never perceive necessary connection, destroys the iron "laws" he needs here. If induction is only custom, "uniform experience" can't yield the "proof" he claims. Houston defends him charitably on this, but Earman's straight rule shows the tension is real: Hume is a sceptic in Section IV and dogmatic in Section X.

**Houston's "promising hypothesis" fits the Resurrection exactly**
- The case for the Resurrection was never "establish an isolated violation, then infer God". It is the Gordon Allan structure. There is the empty tomb, the appearances to individuals and groups, the conversion of James and of Paul (a hostile witness), and the birth of a movement of Jews worshipping a crucified man on the first day of the week. All of this is best explained together by "God raised him", in the context of Israel's hope. That is how the minimal-facts argument works, and Houston shows it is not circular. See [[The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus]].
- Hume's "ignorant and barbarous nations" and "remote antiquity" don't fit first-century Judaea under Roman administration, with letters written within twenty years (1 Cor 15:3–8, "the greater part remain unto this present"). Earman's point about independent witnesses applies directly. Paul names the witnesses who are still alive so they can be checked.

**Signs, not stunts**
- Scripture's words for miracles are *semeia* (signs), *dynameis* (powers) and *terata* (wonders), and the Gospels prefer "signs". Christ refuses to perform for Herod or on demand: "There shall no sign be given… but the sign of the prophet Jonas" (Matt 12:39). This is Houston's "third possibility" in the Gospel's own terms. A miracle is intelligible because it means something, as the healing of the blind shows Him to be "the light of the world" (John 9:5). See [[Revelation and Miracles]].
- The Orthodox go further. A miracle is not God interrupting a self-running machine but the **uncreated energies** shining through creation, which is already full of the *logoi*. The Transfiguration is the model: nature is not violated but shown as it truly is. That is why the Church expects miracles to continue, in the saints, relics and icons, not as proofs pushed at unbelievers but as signs of the age to come breaking in. See [[The Transfiguration of Jesus on Mount Tabor]], [[God's Uncreated Energies]] and [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].

**Hume's parting jab is half true**
- "Whoever is moved by Faith… is conscious of a continued miracle in his own person." The Fathers would agree that faith is a gift, a work of grace. "Flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee" (Matt 16:17). But grace does not "subvert all the principles of [the] understanding". It heals the *nous* so that it sees what is there. Faith and reason are not rivals here. The disciples' faith rested on what they "have seen with our eyes… and our hands have handled" (1 John 1:1). See [[Fideism and Evidentialism]] and [[Nous]].

**What I take from it**
- Weigh testimony honestly. Hume is right that credulity, love of wonder and pious fraud are real. The Church herself investigates claimed miracles cautiously, and the Fathers warn against seeking signs. But a rule that says *no* testimony could ever count is not caution. As the Jansenist case shows, it is a decision made in advance.
- "Blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29). Even this was spoken to a man who had seen, and touched.
