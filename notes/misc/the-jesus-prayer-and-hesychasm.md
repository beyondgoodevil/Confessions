**Hesychasm**: from *hesychia*, stillness. The Orthodox tradition of inner prayer and watchfulness, aiming at constant remembrance of God. The **Jesus Prayer** is at the centre of it.

## 1. The Prayer

> **"Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner."**

Shorter forms:

- "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me."
- "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me."
- "Lord, have mercy" (*Kyrie eleison*)
- Ware: any short prayer containing the name **Jesus** counts as "the Jesus Prayer".

Two halves:

- "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God" = confession of who He is. "No man can say that Jesus is the Lord, but by the Holy Ghost" (1 Cor 12:3).
- "have mercy on me, a sinner" = repentance.
- Ware calls these the two "moments" of Christian devotion: **doxology** (looking up to God's glory) and **compunction** (returning to oneself as a sinner who's been forgiven).

So it's the Gospel in one line: who Christ is, what I am, what I need.

And it includes the **Trinity**: to the Son, as "Son of God" (so the Father), in the Spirit (1 Cor 12:3, above). Ware: the Spirit, "while remaining anonymous, is the embracing environment" of the invocation.

**The four strands** (Ware, *The Jesus Prayer*). Ten words in English, seven in Greek, but four older traditions meet in them:

1. **The cry for mercy**: *Kyrie eleison*, in the liturgy from at least the 4th c. Not gloomy: the Fathers linked *eleos* (mercy) with *elaion* (olive oil). "Probably bad etymology, but it is good theology": mercy is God's love poured out to heal. Hesychius: calling on Jesus "with a keen yearning that is full of sweetness and joy" fills "the air of our heart" with peace.
2. **The discipline of repetition**: the Desert Fathers' *monologia*. Many phrases were used: Ps 50(51):1; Ps 69(70):1; Abba Apollo's "As man, I have sinned; as God, do You forgive"; Macarius's "I beseech You, O Lord". Repetition brings the mind "from fragmentation to unity", but needs discretion.
3. **The quest for stillness** (*hesychia*): Evagrius: "do not shape within yourself any image of the Deity... prayer is a putting-away of thoughts." So it's "non-iconic" prayer, unlike Ignatian meditation where you picture a Gospel scene.
4. **The veneration of the Holy Name**: the most decisive strand. Mal 1:11; "hallowed be thy name"; Acts 4:12. Hermas: "the name of the Son of God is great and boundless, and it upholds the whole world." Origen: a name "brings before us the distinctive character of what is named". To call someone by name makes them "dynamically present": the Name has "sacramental force".

## 2. Where It Comes From in Scripture

- **Luke 18:13**, the publican: "God be merciful to me a sinner."
- **Luke 18:38**, the blind man: "Jesus, thou Son of David, have mercy on me."
- **Matt 15:22**, the Canaanite woman: "Have mercy on me, O Lord, thou Son of David."
- **1 Thess 5:17**: "Pray without ceasing."
    - Ware: that means "an implicit state rather than a series of explicit acts". In Scripture "always" can mean "often" (Heb 9:6, the priests going "always" into the tabernacle at set hours), but unceasing prayer means more than that (*The Art of Prayer* I).
- **Phil 2:10**: "At the name of Jesus every knee should bow."
- **Acts 4:12**: "none other name under heaven... whereby we must be saved."
- **Matt 6:6**: "Enter into thy closet... and pray to thy Father which is in secret." The Fathers take the closet to be the heart.
    - The material closet holds only the man; the inner closet holds "God and all the Kingdom of Heaven" (Luke 17:21) (*The Art of Prayer* I).

## 3. History

**Desert Fathers, 4th–5th c.**

- Egyptian monks used short repeated prayers to keep the mind on God while plaiting mats and baskets.
- Evagrius, St. Macarius: guarding thoughts, prayer of the heart.

**The Name, 5th–7th c.**

- St. Diadochos of Photiki: constant remembrance of "Lord Jesus".
    - Ware: Diadochos is the key figure, tying three strands together (repetition, stillness, the Name): give the restless intellect "nothing but the prayer *Lord Jesus*".
- St. John Climacus, *Ladder*: let the remembrance of Jesus be joined to your breath.
- St. Hesychios of Sinai: watchfulness (*nepsis*) + the Jesus Prayer.
- Love of the Name in the West too: Bernard of Clairvaux; Richard Rolle: "a joyous song, and heavenly music in mine ear".

**Athos and the 14th c. controversy**

- Athonite monks prayed it with bodily stillness and spoke of seeing the **uncreated light**, the light of Tabor.
    - Palamas (*Triads*): the light of Tabor is the same light the saints see: uncreated, the glory of God, "the beauty of the age to come". See [[God's Uncreated Energies]].
- **Barlaam of Calabria** attacked: God is known only indirectly, any light seen must be created. (He also mocked them as "navel-gazers".)
    - Meyendorff: Barlaam saw the monks as unqualified fanatics, and was most shocked by the claim that the **body**, not only the mind, could share in the experience of God.
- **St. Gregory Palamas**, *Triads*: God is unknowable in **essence**, truly known and shared in through His **uncreated energies**.
    - The *Triads in Defence of the Holy Hesychasts* (c. 1338–41) are three sets of three treatises against Barlaam.
    - **I**: against the claim that **secular learning** is needed for knowing God; the nous and the body in prayer (defence of the psychosomatic method).
    - **II**: the same themes, more developed; prayer and the body; the light.
    - **III**: the **uncreated light** and the **essence–energies** distinction; deification as real participation.
- Councils of Constantinople **1341, 1347, 1351** sided with Palamas.

**Philokalia, 1782**

- St. Nikodemos of the Holy Mountain + St. Makarios of Corinth collect the main texts.
- St. Paisius Velichkovsky takes it into Slavonic → Optina elders, St. Seraphim of Sarov.
    - 19th-century Russian teaching on the prayer, especially **St. Theophan the Recluse** and **St. Ignatius Brianchaninov**, is collected by Igumen Chariton in *The Art of Prayer*. Chariton copied it into a notebook from his reading; the repetitions are on purpose, "to impress them deeply on the mind".
    - Theophan was a bishop (Tambov, Vladimir) who retired as a recluse, "disposed to solitude, gentle and silent". Ware: "We cannot understand Russian Orthodoxy unless we understand the celebrated Recluse."
    - Theophan's view: "Russian authors are easier than Greek".

**Way of a Pilgrim, 19th c.**

- Anonymous Russian story of a wanderer learning the prayer. This is how most laypeople (and most Westerners, via Salinger) heard of it.

## 4. Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *hesychia* | stillness, inner quiet |
| *nepsis* | watchfulness, sobriety, guarding the heart |
| *nous* | spiritual intellect, "eye of the soul" (not discursive reason) |
| *logismoi* | intrusive thoughts / suggestions |
| prayer of the heart | the nous "descends into the heart" and prays there |
| *umilenie* | "warm tenderness of heart", a Russian word that resists translation |
| *theoria* | vision / contemplation of God |
| *theosis* | deification, sharing God's life by grace |
| *prelest* | spiritual delusion |

## 5. Three Stages

From St. Theophan the Recluse and others:

1. **Lips.** Said aloud or under the breath, with effort to pay attention. (Theophan's first degree is "bodily prayer": reading, standing, prostrations.)
2. **Mind.** Said inwardly, attention stays in the words: "mind-in-the-head".
3. **Heart.** Becomes continuous, "self-acting": "mind-in-the-heart". This is a gift, not something a method produces.
    - It comes as a gift after long labour (*The Art of Prayer*). "The first kind of prayer requires effort, the second works by itself."
    - **"Stand before God with the mind in the heart"**: Theophan's definition of prayer. Not the words but the standing. "The essence of prayer is the spiritual lifting of the heart towards God."
    - **Warmth of heart**: a sign of prayer becoming prayer of the heart. Not emotional excitement but a quiet, steady warmth. Don't confuse it with physical or imaginative heat.
    - The Fathers distinguish **three warmths**: physical (from concentrating on the heart region), lustful (from the enemy), and spiritual, "sober and pure" (*The Art of Prayer* IV).
    - Prayer of the heart isn't "affective prayer" in the Western sense but prayer of the whole person, body, soul and spirit (Ware). Macarius: "the heart is Christ's palace". The "hidden man of the heart" (1 Pet 3:4) is the God-like spirit breathed into Adam, still there after the Fall (Theophan).
    - When the mind's dwelling in the heart becomes natural and constant, "the Fathers call it union of mind and heart" (the *startsi* of Valamo).
- Spiritual life as a whole is moving from communion with God "in thought and intention" to "real, living, and conscious communion" (*The Art of Prayer* V). Gregory of Sinai: God is gained "either by activity and work, or by the art of invoking the Name of Jesus", the second being quicker.

## 6. Practice

- A **rule**: set number or set time, counted on a prayer rope (*komboskini* / *chotki*).
    - Ware: the rope is "not to act as a measurement but to assist us to concentrate".
- Through the day: at work, walking, waiting.
    - Ware's **free** use (through the day, first thought on waking and last before sleep) and **fixed** use (a set time of quiet). You can have the first without the second. People who said it before an operation found themselves saying it as they came round.
- Attention over quantity. "Enclose the mind in the words."
    - **Attention** is everything (Theophan): "the principal thing is to stand with the mind in the heart before God, and to go on standing before Him unceasingly day and night."
    - "The essential part is not in the words, but in faith, contrition, and self-surrender to the Lord."
- **Silence**: Kierkegaard, "Create silence!" Prayer is first listening (the *orans* in the catacombs). The prayer "leads us through words into silence", not into a "holy-dozy" half-sleep (John Main, in Ware).
- Small practical points from Ware: leave a short pause after each prayer; sit upright on a chair with a back; aloud at first; it can be said in a group, one after another.
- Posture and breathing appear in some texts. Secondary, and the Fathers warn against using them without a guide.
    - The breathing form: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God" breathing in, "have mercy on me, a sinner" breathing out (Ware).
    - Palamas (*Triads*) defends it for **beginners**, whose minds keep scattering: it's "not out of place" to recall the mind by control of breathing, and to fix the eyes on the breast or navel rather than letting them "roam hither and thither". Dionysius's two movements of the mind: "along a straight line" (out to things) and "circular" (returning into itself), the second being the way it "transcends itself and is united to God".
    - Where is the mind? Some say the brain, "as in a kind of acropolis"; Palamas says the heart, "as in an instrument" (Matt 15:19).
    - Ware's introduction: the bodily techniques "are in no sense an essential part of the Jesus Prayer". The Valamo *startsi* now advise against them because of the danger.
    - **Humility** above methods: "prayer is God's gift; ask for it and it will be given" (*The Art of Prayer*).
- A **"soul friend"** (*geronta*, *starets*): you climb a mountain for the first time with someone who's been up before (Ware).

## 7. Warnings

- **Not a mantra.** It's addressed to a Person, with faith and repentance. The power is in who is called on, not the repetition.
    - Ware: never "simply a rhythmic mantra", but a personal invocation of the one born in Bethlehem who died and rose.
- **Inside the Church's life.** Goes with confession, Communion, fasting, the commandments. Doesn't replace them.
    - Gregory of Sinai called prayer "Baptism made manifest": moving from grace present secretly to grace at work "with full assurance and conscious awareness". The Xanthopouloi put the Prayer between Baptism and frequent Communion (Ware).
- **Spiritual father** for the deeper practice, because of *prelest*.
- **Don't look for experiences.** No seeking lights or sensations, and distrust them if they come.
    - What comes from grace "we must simply await"; if coldness returns right after a sweet feeling, it was your own effort, not grace. "Do not measure yourself." "What comes from God is wholly peaceful, calm and sweet" (*The Art of Prayer* IV, VI).
    - The first attack once you begin is **self-satisfaction** (*The Art of Prayer* VI).
- **No images.** Don't imagine Christ or anything else while praying; that leads to delusion (*prelest*). Prayer is beyond imagination (*The Art of Prayer*).
    - Ware's softer advice: don't fight images head-on. "What we are seeking is not so much a mind stripped of images as a heart full of love." Let thoughts recede; "in the foreground, put Jesus."
- **Dryness** comes either as punishment or "as an education": to teach us to expect nothing from our own powers (Theophan). Demons can't enter a soul "unless they first deprive him of all holy thoughts, and make him empty" (Palamas, in *The Art of Prayer*). Barsanuphius: the passions are afflictions, so "flee for refuge to the Name".
- **Not "petrifaction"**: becoming insensible isn't holiness. Tears are a gift: "God has touched the eyes of your heart" (Palamas, *Triads*).

## 8. Why It Matters for Theology

- It depends on the essence–energies distinction. If Barlaam is right, the monks are at best seeing something created, and theosis is a metaphor.
    - The light that may be seen in prayer is "not a physical and created light, but spiritual and uncreated" (Ware). Ware notes Aquinas also held there's no total comprehension of the divine essence, even in heaven.
- Shows that knowing God in Orthodoxy is communion, not only correct propositions.
    - Palamas (*Triads*): philosophy is useful but not necessary for knowledge of God; the prophets and apostles were fishermen.
- The body is involved. It too is to be sanctified and raised.
    - The **body** shares in prayer and in grace: the transfigured face of Moses, the relics of saints (Palamas, *Triads*). Paul calls the body "this death" because of sinful desire, not because the body is bad; the "passionate part" of the soul is to be offered alive, not killed.
- **Is it selfish?** Seraphim: "Acquire inner peace, and thousands around you will find salvation." Hammarskjöld: "Understand through the stillness; act out of the stillness." "The meditator becomes a mediator" (Ware). It can become intercession ("have mercy on us", or adding names); Lev Gillet used the Name to see others in "their most profound and divine reality".
- Ware won't claim it's the only way: "It has helped many; it has helped me; perhaps it will also help you."

Thought: the Palamite controversy looks like an argument about monks' prayer technique, but it's really about whether God can be *participated in* at all. That's why three councils dealt with it.
