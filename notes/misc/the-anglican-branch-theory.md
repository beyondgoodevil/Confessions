The **Anglican Branch Theory** is a view that proposes the **Anglican Communion** (comprising the Church of England and its affiliated churches) as one of the three "branches" of the **Christian Church**, alongside **Roman Catholicism** and **Eastern Orthodoxy**. This theory asserts that all three are legitimate expressions of the true Church, each representing a different tradition or "branch" within the larger **universal Church** (the **Body of Christ**). The theory emerged in the 19th and 20th centuries, particularly as Anglicanism sought to assert its identity in relation to Roman Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy.

Key points of the theory include:

1. **Three branches**: The theory posits that the Christian Church consists of three "branches" — the **Roman Catholic Church**, the **Eastern Orthodox Church**, and the **Anglican Church**. These branches are considered distinct but equally valid expressions of the **one true Church**.
2. **Shared roots**: It holds that all three branches share a common apostolic foundation, claiming that the Anglican Church, like the Catholic and Orthodox Churches, can trace its origins back to the early Christian Church, particularly to the apostles and the early councils.
3. **Ecclesial validity**: Supporters of the theory argue that the Anglican Church is just as valid as the Catholic and Orthodox churches and that it possesses the fullness of the Christian faith, including apostolic succession, valid sacraments, and historical continuity with the ancient Church.
4. **Ecumenical perspective**: The Branch Theory was often advanced to promote ecumenical dialogue and cooperation between the Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican traditions, suggesting that despite their differences, they all share a common heritage in Christ.
