> "Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God. He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love." (1 John 4:7–8)

> "Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death." (Song 8:6)

In 1930 the Swedish Lutheran bishop Anders Nygren argued that Christianity brought into the world a love so new that the Greeks had no word for it. Christian **agape** and Platonic **eros** were "two fundamental motifs" pulling in opposite directions, and the history of Christian thought was the story of their confused mixing: Augustine's *caritas* was a synthesis, and Luther finally pulled them apart again. The question matters because the Orthodox Fathers did the very thing Nygren calls the great confusion. Dionysius and Maximus call God **Eros**, and Gregory of Nyssa reads the Song of Songs as the soul's erotic ascent. So the debate is really about whether the Greek East got the Gospel wrong.

## 1. Plato: Diotima's Speech

Socrates says he learned "erotics" from a priestess, Diotima of Mantineia.

**Eros is a daimon, not a god** (201d–203a)
- Agathon had praised Eros as the most beautiful and youngest god. Diotima refutes Socrates the same way Socrates refuted Agathon: eros *desires* the beautiful and good, and you only desire what you lack, so eros is neither beautiful nor good.
- But that does not make him ugly. There is a middle, as right opinion lies between knowledge and ignorance.
- The gods are happy because they *have* the good things, so Eros cannot be a god. He is a "great daemon", between mortal and immortal. Daemons ferry prayers up and commands down; "god does not mingle with a human being" except through them.

**The myth of his birth** (203b–204c)
- At the feast for Aphrodite's birth, **Poros** (Resource, son of Metis, Cunning) gets drunk on nectar and falls asleep in Zeus' garden. **Penia** (Poverty), begging at the door, lies with him and conceives Eros.
- So Eros has his mother's nature: tough, squalid, shoeless, homeless, sleeping in doorways. From his father he gets cunning: a hunter, "always weaving devices", "philosophizing through all his life", a "magician, druggist, sophist".
- He dies and comes alive again on the same day; what he gains always flows out.
- This makes eros the **philosopher**. The gods don't philosophise, since they are wise. The ignorant don't either, because they think they lack nothing. Socrates' mistake was to picture Eros as the *beloved* (beautiful and blessed) rather than the *lover*.

**What eros wants** (204d–206a)
- The lover of good things wants them **to be his**, and then he will be happy. "Eros is of the good's being one's own always."
- "Eros" in the ordinary sense is one species named after the whole, as "poetry" (*poiēsis*, making) is used only for verse. Really every desire for good and happiness is eros: money-making, gymnastics, philosophy.
- Against Aristophanes' story of the halves: we don't love what is our own *unless it is good*; people will cut off their own hand if it is diseased.

**"Bringing to birth in beauty"** (206b–209e)
- The work of eros is "bringing to birth in beauty, both in body and in soul". Everyone is pregnant and longs to give birth. Beauty is the midwife; ugliness makes the pregnant shrink and hold it in.
- So eros is not simply "of the beautiful" but "of engendering and bringing to birth in the beautiful". Why? Because generation is the mortal's only share in **immortality**. Even an individual stays "the same" only by constant replacement: hair, blood, opinions, even knowledge (studying replaces what forgetting removes).
- Animals fight to the death for their young. Men risk everything for fame: Alcestis, Achilles, Codrus.
- Those pregnant in body turn to women and have children. Those pregnant in soul bring forth **prudence and virtue**, above all the ordering of cities (moderation and justice). Their children are poems and laws, Homer's and Hesiod's, Lycurgus' and Solon's. Such lovers have "a firmer friendship" than parents, because their offspring are "more beautiful and more immortal".

**The ladder** (210a–212a)
- One body → all beautiful bodies (realising the beauty is one, and slackening his passion for the one as "petty") → beauty of souls → practices and laws → sciences → the "open sea of the beautiful" → "suddenly" the **beautiful itself**.
- It is always, neither coming to be nor passing away; not beautiful in one way and ugly in another; not a face or a speech or "in something else", but "alone by itself and with itself, always being of a single form". Other beautiful things share in it while it is not affected at all.
- Lower beautiful things are used "as steps".
- Only here does a man bring forth not "phantom images of virtue" but **true virtue**, and become "dear to god" and, if any man can, **immortal**.

**Alcibiades** then bursts in drunk and praises Socrates, who is ugly outside like a Silenus statue with gods inside, and who refused his seduction. Many readers (Bloom, Nussbaum) take this as Plato's own counterweight: here is love for *one* irreplaceable person, which the ladder has no room for. See [[Nussbaum's Fragility of Goodness: Luck, Tragedy and the Vulnerable Good]].

## 2. Nygren: Two Motifs

Nygren's *Agape and Eros* lays out each motif in contrasting marks.

**Agape** (from the Synoptics and Paul)
1. **Spontaneous and "unmotivated."** We look in vain for anything in man that explains God's love. Jesus eats with sinners and says He came not for the righteous. "Motivated" love is human; unmotivated love is divine.
2. **"Indifferent to value."** Not that God loves the bad *rather than* the good, but that value is not the reason. Even God's love for the righteous is unmotivated.
3. **Creative.** Agape "does not recognize value, but creates it". The man God loves has no value in himself; God's love gives it.
4. **The initiator of fellowship with God.** There is no way *from* man *to* God; only God's way down to man.

**Eros** (from Plato, Aristotle and Plotinus)
1. **Acquisitive.** Plato puts it between having and not having; without a sense of need, eros would never arise. Plato "is fundamentally unaware of any other form of love than acquisitive love". Because it is drawn by value, it can never be unmotivated.
2. **Man's way to the divine.** It is the soul climbing back to its heavenly home. Heavenly eros is set against the vulgar, sensual eros that binds the soul to things below.
3. **Egocentric.** Even at its height it seeks the lover's own good, his own happiness and immortality ("the good to be one's own always").
4. Hence Plato's gods **do not love**. Aristotle's God moves the world "as the beloved", by being desired, not by desiring.

Nygren's tabulation ends in the image of two directions: eros is an upward movement; agape comes down. On this view, when Christians later use eros of God (Dionysius) or define charity as desire for God (Augustine), they are importing a pagan way of salvation by ascent. Luther "smashes" the synthesis.

## 3. Vlastos: Plato Cannot Love a Person

Vlastos accepts part of Nygren and corrects part.
- Platonic eros is **not** as "egocentric" as Nygren says. It is "only too patently **Ideocentric** and creative". The lover wants to *beget* virtue in the beloved, and finally he loves the Form, not himself.
- The real defect is different. We love people in Plato only for their virtue and beauty, so we love the *image of the Form* in them. Since everyone is a mixture with "streaks of the ugly, the mean, the commonplace, the ridiculous", **"the individual, in the uniqueness and integrity of his or her individuality, will never be the object of our love."** Vlastos calls this "the cardinal flaw in Plato's theory".
- Plato "is scarcely aware of kindness, tenderness, compassion, concern for the freedom, respect for the integrity of the beloved". He has "missed that dimension of love in which tolerance, trust, forgiveness, tenderness, respect have validity".
- Vlastos links this to the *Republic*: a city where love for the polis as a whole overrides any respect for each citizen's liberty.

## 4. Dionysius: God Is Eros

*Divine Names* IV is the Christian text Nygren most dislikes, and the most important for the East.

**The word** (IV.11–12)
- Some think the scriptural writers prefer "love" (*agapē*) to "yearning" (*erōs*), and that *erōs* is unworthy of God. Dionysius answers that we should look to the meaning rather than the sounds, and that Scripture uses both of the same reality.
- He cites Wisdom ("I was a lover of her beauty", Wis 8:2) and **Ignatius**: "He for whom I yearn has been crucified" (*Romans* 7.2). The "theologians" treat "yearning" and "love" as having **"one and the same meaning"**. They add "real" to "yearning" for divine things only because of how carnal people misuse the word.
- The fault is not in the word but in "divided" and "partial" eros, "an empty image" or "lapse" from real eros. Yannaras builds on this phrase (§6 below).

**Ecstasy** (IV.13)
- "This divine yearning brings **ecstasy** so that the lover belongs not to self but to the beloved."
- Superiors show it in providence for those below, equals in mutual bonds, inferiors in turning to what is higher.
- Paul is the example: "I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me" (Gal 2:20). He was "beside himself" for God (2 Cor 5:13).
- And then the bold step: **God Himself** is carried outside Himself. "The very cause of the universe… is, as it were, **beguiled by goodness**, by love, and by yearning, and is enticed away from his transcendent dwelling place", while still remaining within Himself. Creation and providence are God's ecstasy.

**The circle** (IV.14–17)
- God is both Yearning and the Yearned-for: eros moves out from the Good, through beings, and back to the Good. It goes round "like an eternal circle, the good from the good and in the good and to the good", "always proceeding and remaining and being restored" (IV.14, as Yannaras quotes it).
- So there is **no** clean split between a love that descends and a love that ascends. Descent and return are one movement, and it starts in God.

The Reformers' distrust of Dionysius began here. Froehlich's introduction notes that the young Luther used Dionysian words ("ecstasy", "divine darkness") but was "uneasy about the Platonic content from the start". He read the *via negativa* as the experience of God's absence and *Anfechtung*. By 1520 he had rejected the corpus. Nygren's book is in effect that rejection worked out as a system.

## 5. Gregory of Nyssa: Passion Turned to Impassibility

Gregory's *Homilies on the Song of Songs* (c. 391) are the fullest patristic use of eros for the soul and God.
- **Homily 1.** Solomon takes the most intense pleasure there is, erotic passion, and makes it a figure, so that we may learn to love the divine beauty as much as the body is drawn to what is akin to it, and "turn passion into impassibility, so that when every bodily disposition has been quelled, our mind within us may boil with love, but only in the Spirit".
- Only those who have "put on" Christ and are "transfigured for impassibility" should hear the Song. Wisdom herself bids us "be in love", for "this passion, when directed toward things incorporeal, is blameless and impassible".
- He notes that neither small children nor the very old feel eros. The Song is for the ripe soul.
- **Homily 13.** The bride is "wounded by the incorporeal and fiery arrow of love", "for **agapē when intensified is called erōs**". This is the patristic answer to Nygren in one line: eros is not another love but agape at full heat.
- The ascent never ends. The bride finds the Beloved and at once seeks Him again (*epektasis*, Phil 3:13). Since the Good is infinite, desire is never filled and so is never bored. Plato's ladder had a top; Gregory's does not.
- Impassibility (*apatheia*) here is not coldness. It means freedom from the disordered passions, so that all desire can go to God.

## 6. Maximus and Yannaras: The World's Eros and "True Eros"

**Maximus** (scholia on Dionysius, cited by Yannaras §43)
- "The first cause of heavenly eros is God… For if this eros is love… and it is written that 'God is love', it is evident that the eros or love that unifies all things is God."
- From God it passes to the angels (no faction among them), then to the saints of the Church ("that they may be one"), then to all humanity ("the law of affectionate attraction"), to animals ("psychic" eros: birds flying in flocks, cattle in herds, fish in shoals), and finally to inanimate things ("natural" eros), "drawn erotically to the creator because they are sustained by him".
- Elsewhere he gives eros a root in human nature: "Without the power of desire there is no longing, and so no love… and without the incensive power… there can be no peace" (cited Yannaras §52). The passions are not cut out but turned. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].

**Yannaras** (*Person and Eros*, 1976)
- **§42.** The fullness of eros is the mutual indwelling (perichoresis) of the Trinity: "the place of God is the non-dimensional personal-loving relation". Love is a **mode of existence**, not a feeling: "he that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God" (1 John 4:16). God's eros "goes forth" ecstatically to create and returns through creatures' response.
- **§43.** Man is the **place where the world's eros becomes personal**. He gathers creation's attraction and offers it back to God, as priest of creation.
- **§52.** Eros is "the existential impetus towards transcending the boundaries of individuality", towards overcoming time and decay. But there are two ecstasies:
  - **Divided eros**: the "erotic ecstasy of individuality". It is nature's reproductive drive and a pursuit of the individual's own pleasure. It meets the other only "as object and insurmountable limit of individual pleasure", and so confirms the splitting of nature into individuals. Its secret is "the tragic awareness of the void".
  - **True eros**: personal, universal reference. It presupposes the natural drive and is "mingled" with it, but breaks its closure. Its sign is **presence**: the other "rising up from the oblivion of non-relation".
  - The hour of a lover's meeting may last as long by the clock as waiting for a late train, but the two are not the same *duration*.

## 7. Lewis: Gift-love, Need-love and the Demon

Lewis set out (ch. I) to praise **Gift-love**, which is like God, and belittle **Need-love**, "the son of Poverty", as Plato said. He found he couldn't.
- Our love for God "must always be very largely, and must often be entirely, a Need-love". A creature that came before God saying "I love you disinterestedly" would be "bold and silly".
- So he distinguished **nearness by likeness** (being like God in some respect: Gift-loves, power, creativity) from **nearness of approach** (actually moving toward union with God). They don't always coincide. His image is a cliff above the village: very near, but you have to walk five miles round.
- He takes from Denis de Rougemont that love "ceases to be a demon only when he ceases to be a god". The natural loves claim divinity at their *best*, not their worst, because only then is the claim plausible. "We may mistake Like for Same."

**Eros** (ch. V)
- He distinguishes Eros, "being in love", from **Venus**, its sexual element.
- Without Eros, sexual desire wants "the thing in itself"; Eros wants **the Beloved**. The lustful man does not really "want a woman"; he wants a pleasure for which she is the apparatus. Eros wants "one particular woman", not the pleasure she can give. This is the very thing Vlastos found missing in Plato.
- Eros turns a Need-pleasure into the most Appreciative of pleasures. It "obliterate[s] the distinction between giving and receiving".
- He says the danger of Eros is not chiefly carnal (St Paul fears the cares of marriage, not the bed). We should not take Venus too seriously; the body is "Brother Ass", and lovers laugh.
- The grandeur of Eros: "Better to be miserable with her than happy without her." That is its likeness to God, and also its danger. Eros "honoured without reservation and obeyed unconditionally, becomes a demon". It makes its own religion, and "it is for love's sake" excuses abandoning parents, children and friends.
- Eros vows what it cannot keep ("I will be ever true"). "We must do the works of Eros when Eros is not present." Without humility, charity and grace, Eros dies or else lives on as "hate-in-love". Lewis points to *Anna Karenina*.

**Charity** (ch. VI)
- "Love is enough." "It isn't." The natural loves "cannot even remain themselves… without God's help". "When God arrives (and only then) the half-gods can remain."
- Against Augustine's lesson from his friend's death (*Conf.* IV), Lewis argues we should not refuse to love creatures in order to avoid grief. "To love at all is to be vulnerable… The only place outside Heaven where you can be perfectly safe from all the dangers and perturbations of love is Hell."
- Inordinate love is not loving a person too much, but loving God too little.
- God gives three supernatural gifts: a share in His own **Gift-love** (which can love "lepers, criminals, enemies"), a **Need-love of Himself** ("jolly beggars"), and the hard grace of *receiving* charity from others.
- The natural loves are not replaced but **taken up**, "as God becomes Man… by taking of the Manhood into God". Only loves into which Love Himself has entered will rise with Him.

## 8. Comparison

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| does God love? | no; the gods lack nothing | yes, only as agape | yes, as Eros, ecstatically | yes; the Bridegroom seeks the bride | yes, as Gift-love |

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**Nygren saw a real difference but drew the wrong line**
- He is right that the Gospel's love begins with God and not with us: "Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us" (1 John 4:10). And he is right that Plato's gods do not love, and that Diotima's eros is born of poverty. The Fathers never said otherwise.
- But he made the difference a difference of **two loves**, when it is really a difference of **two Gods**. Plato's eros is acquisitive because his god is a static object at the top of a ladder. When the God at the top turns out to be a Trinity of Persons whose life *is* love, the same eros is shown to be self-giving at its source. This is Dionysius' whole point. The Fathers did not baptise Plato's eros unchanged. They kept the word and changed what stands at its source.
- His "agape" is also too thin to be the love of Scripture. A love "indifferent to value" that "creates" all value comes close to saying the creature is nothing. But God "saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Gen 1:31). God loves what He has made *because He made it good*, and He loves the sinner because the image is still there under the ruin. Lewis' warning against a God who loves us for our "native luminosity" is true. The answer, though, is that the luminosity is a gift, not that there is none. See [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- And Scripture itself uses the language of desire for God everywhere: "As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God" (Ps 42:1); "My soul thirsteth for God" (Ps 42:2); the whole Song of Songs. A theology that has to treat the Psalter's longing as suspect has gone wrong somewhere.

**Ignatius' line is the key**
- "He for whom I yearn has been crucified." The object of Christian eros is not the timeless Form "unmixed with human flesh" but a Man nailed to wood. That changes eros from inside. It is no longer the climb away from bodies but the desire for the One who came down into a body and gave it away. Gregory's "agape intensified is eros" and Ignatius' cry say the same thing: the Cross does not abolish desire; it gives desire its true object.
- So the old picture of "eros goes up, agape comes down" fails exactly at the Incarnation. God's descent *is* His eros (Dionysius: "beguiled by goodness"), and our ascent is only a response to it. The circle replaces the two arrows.

**Vlastos' criticism holds against Plato, not against the Fathers**
- Plato's lover does use the beloved "as steps", and the passage where he "slackens" his love for the one body as "petty" is chilling. Alcibiades' speech shows Plato knew it.
- But the Christian answer is not to drop eros. It is the **person**. Yannaras' "true eros" is love for the other in their unrepeatable otherness: the opposite of using them as a rung. Lewis' observation that Eros wants "the Beloved herself" is what Vlastos found missing, and Christianity explains *why* it is possible: each person is made by name and loved by name by God, so to love a person is not to pass through them to something else. "I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine" (Isa 43:1). See [[Being as Communion]].

**Lewis' warning is the necessary ascetic note**
- Yannaras sometimes writes as if eros were self-justifying. Lewis and the *Ladder* correct this: unchastened eros becomes a demon and makes its own religion. The Fathers' *apatheia* is not hostility to eros but its healing. The passions are disordered loves, and the cure is to turn them, not kill them. Gregory's "passion into impassibility" and Maximus' desire and *thumos* turned toward God are the same programme. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]] and [[The Ladder of Divine Ascent]].
- On Augustine and Nebridius, Lewis is partly right: Christ wept at Lazarus' tomb, and a love that refuses to risk grief is not Christian. But Augustine's real point in the *Confessions* is not "don't love" but "love them in God", and that is Lewis' own conclusion in his last chapter.
- Lewis is also right that the question of whether we love God "more" is not about the intensity of feeling but about which claim the will obeys. That is the Gospel's "hate father and mother" (Luke 14:26) read soberly.

**Mimetic desire and true eros**
- Girard's mimetic desire is Yannaras' "divided eros" seen from the outside: desire that never meets the other as a person but only as a model or rival, and so ends in the void. The cure in both is the same: a desire whose model is Christ, who desires nothing at another's expense. See [[Mimetic Desire: Girard, Oughourlian and Lacan]].

**What I take from it**
- Agape and eros are one love seen from two sides: as **gift** (it gives itself without counting) and as **longing** (it cannot rest without the beloved). In God both are perfect, with no lack. In us both are real, and the longing is not a fault but the shape of a creature made for God. "Thou hast made us for thyself, and our heart is restless until it rests in thee" (*Conf.* I.1).
- Marriage, friendship and the love of beauty are not rival loves to be shed but rungs that need not be kicked away, because the top of this ladder is a Person who took a body. See [[Friendship and Love in Ancient and Medieval Thought]] and [[Beauty as a Way to God]].
