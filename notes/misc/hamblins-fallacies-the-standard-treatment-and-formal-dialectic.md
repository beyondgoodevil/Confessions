Charles Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970) is the book most often credited with starting the modern study of fallacies. Hamblin was an Australian logician. His starting complaint was that the chapter on fallacies in every logic textbook was a museum piece: a list inherited from Aristotle, stocked with jokes and stale examples, and not connected to any theory. The book has three parts. The first criticizes that "Standard Treatment". The second is a history of the subject, the only full one in English, running from Plato's *Euthydemus* to J. S. Mill, with a chapter on India. The third offers his own proposal: fallacies cannot be understood until logic is extended into **formal dialectic**, the study of rule-governed dialogues in which participants make statements, ask questions and take on **commitments**. This note follows the book in order and sets beside it three readings I studied. **Plecnik and Hoaglund**, in the preface to the Vale Press reprint, explain what the book set in motion. **Woods and Irvine** question Hamblin's reading of Aristotle. **Ganeri** uses Hamblin's commitment store to read early Indian debate.

### 1. The Book and Its Moment

Plecnik and Hoaglund place *Fallacies* in a period when logic was moving back toward ordinary argument. In 1958 Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* broke arguments into data, warrants and backing, and Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca published *The New Rhetoric*. The journal *Philosophy and Rhetoric* followed in 1968 (p. 1). For the period before Hamblin they give a striking figure: "not many more than half a dozen articles on informal fallacies from 1950 to 1970", where now there are that many "or more each year" (p. 1).

They list the work that followed:

- Kahane's *Logic and Contemporary Rhetoric* (1971), which used real-world examples.
- Thomas's *Practical Reasoning in Natural Language* (1973), with its convergent and linked arguments.
- Scriven's *Reasoning* (1976).
- Woods and Walton's *Argument: The Logic of the Fallacies* (1982).
- The informal logic movement itself: the Windsor conference of 1978, Johnson and Blair, the journal *Informal Logic*, and AILACT (pp. 2–3).

On their account Hamblin supplied that movement with its central problem. A fallacy is a fallacious argument, so we need to know what an argument is. His answer, that an argument must be judged inside a dialogue, gave it a method (pp. 3–4). I come back to their summary of his legacy in section 13.

### 2. The Standard Treatment

The opening line sets the tone: "There is hardly a subject that dies harder or has changed so little over the years" (p. 9). Aristotle's thirteen fallacies from *On Sophistical Refutations* are still in the textbooks. Writers on fallacies include "at least one Pope, two saints, archbishops in profusion" (p. 9). Yet "there has never yet been a book on fallacies" (p. 10). His central charge:

> "We have no theory of fallacy at all, in the sense in which we have theories of correct reasoning or inference." (p. 11)

We are, he says, "in the position of the medieval logicians before the twelfth century: we have lost the doctrine of fallacy, and need to rediscover it" (p. 11). The textbook chapter is "as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined — incredibly tradition-bound, yet lacking in logic and in historical sense alike, and almost without connection to anything else in modern Logic at all" (p. 12). His medieval specimen: "Everything that runs has feet; the river runs: therefore, the river has feet" (p. 12).

The **standard definition** is the target of the whole book: "A fallacious argument, as almost every account from Aristotle onwards tells you, is one that seems to be valid but is not so" (p. 12). The rest of the book takes it apart. "Seems" cannot be left as a matter of psychology: to justify calling something a fallacy, "the seeming-valid must have a quasi-logical analysis" (p. 253). And many textbook fallacies turn out not to be invalid (pp. 42–4) or not to be arguments at all (p. 39).

Woods and Irvine quote the same passages at the close of their chapter. They add that Hamblin could have put Aristotle "at the very top of the list of those whose views disappoint him so. In fact, this is not what Hamblin does" (p. 96). His anger is aimed at the later tradition, not at its founder.

### 3. Fallacy by Fallacy: What the Textbooks Get Wrong

Hamblin then goes through the textbook list. The textbooks he examined were Cohen and Nagel, Black, Oesterle, Schipper and Schuh, Copi and Salmon (p. 13 n.). The pattern he finds is that the examples deceive nobody, the categories overlap, and the "fallacies" often turn out to be reasonable.

- **Equivocation and amphiboly.** The examples are puns: "Some dogs have fuzzy ears; my dog has fuzzy ears; therefore my dog is some dog!" (p. 14), or Copi's "SAVE SOAP AND WASTE PAPER" (p. 16). Such examples are not fallacies, since nobody is deceived. "Puns and anecdotes do not take the place of logical analysis", yet "I have found no example that is any better" (p. 18).
- **Figure of speech.** The best modern example is Mill's argument in *Utilitarianism*: "visible" means "can be seen", so "desirable" must mean "can be desired". Hamblin's dry comment is that it was given to Mill "to make the greatest of modern contributions to this Fallacy by perpetrating a serious example of it himself" (p. 26). Woods and Irvine quote the passage and are unconvinced: "here, too, we seem not to have an especially convincing example of a fallacy discernible in speech" (pp. 86–7). On their reading even the best case is a confusion of meaning, not a deception in debate.
- **Secundum quid.** Textbooks copy their examples from one another. One example about raw meat began in a twelfth-century Munich *Dialectica* and has been misfiled under Accident since De Morgan: "Writers of textbooks take their examples one from another" (pp. 29–30).
- **Begging the question.** Cohen and Nagel allow that a circle is acceptable if it is large enough. Hamblin's reply: "Apparently a fallacy is not objectionable so long as it is big enough" (p. 35).
- **The consequent.** Affirming the consequent is the one fallacy with a clear logical analysis, and so the textbooks move it into the chapter on the syllogism: "As soon as a Fallacy has some relation to the rest of Logic it is removed from its place in the chapter on Fallacies!" (p. 36). And why name this invalid form and not, say, "the Fallacy of Distributing Quantifiers without regard to Negation Signs?" (p. 37).
- **Many questions.** "Have you stopped beating your wife?" is not an argument at all: "a man who asks a misleading question can hardly be said to have argued … Where are his premisses and what is his conclusion?" (p. 39). Its proper home is dialectic (p. 40).
- **The arguments "ad".** Hamblin lists ad hominem, verecundiam, misericordiam, ignorantiam, populum, baculum and the rest, and adds: "We feel like adding: ad nauseam — but even this has been suggested before" (p. 41). He points out that the genre was invented by Locke and that most of the names date from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (p. 41). Many of these arguments are not obviously fallacious. Attacking a witness's character "may reasonably be considered to have shaken" his testimony (Joseph, quoted p. 42). "Argument from authority has been mentioned in lists of valid argument-forms as often as in lists of Fallacies" (p. 43). Ad ignorantiam is accepted in court, which leads to: "it must be a strange form of argument that is now valid, now invalid, according as presumptions change with context" (pp. 43–4).

He ends the survey with Aldrich (1691) on the number of fallacies: "The number could also be increased; but since it satisfied Aristotle it has satisfied all later logicians" (p. 49).

### 4. Back to Aristotle: Fallacies as Moves in a Debate

Chapter 2 returns to *On Sophistical Refutations*. A refutation (*elenchus*) is "reasoning involving the contradictory of the given conclusion" (p. 50). A sophist is "one who makes money from an apparent but unreal wisdom" (165a19). Part of Aristotle's purpose is openly practical. He lists the sophist's five aims: refutation, falsehood, paradox, solecism and babbling. He also lists the tricks of length, speed and anger: "Elementary rules for producing anger are to make a show of the wish to play foul, and to be altogether shameless" (174a16; pp. 51–2).

Hamblin's main historical claim is that the work was written young, in Plato's Academy, "before Formal Logic had been invented", and is "itself a Logic-in-the-making" (p. 52). It belongs to a culture of formal debate whose rules Hamblin reconstructs (pp. 61–2):

- One questioner faces one answerer, who defends a thesis.
- The questions must be answerable yes or no.
- The answerer gives his own opinion.
- A contradiction forces him to give way, and the two cannot "agree to differ".
- The audience judges.

Plato's *Euthydemus* shows the sophistic version: "Then you beat your father" (298). Crito says he "would rather be refuted by such arguments than use them" (p. 58).

Aristotle's thirteen fallacies fall into two groups (pp. 62–3). Six depend on language: ambiguity, amphiboly, combination, division, accent and form of expression. Seven are outside language: accident, secundum quid, misconception of refutation, assuming the original point, consequent, non-cause as cause, and making two questions into one. Hamblin argues that several only make sense as faults in a debate. "Non-cause as cause" is not post hoc reasoning. It is a fault in reductio: the questioner derives a falsehood from the thesis plus an extra concession, and the answerer replies that the falsehood comes from the concession, not the thesis (p. 78). The name "False Cause" is "no older than Isaac Watts … 1725" (p. 80). Begging the question and many questions depend on a questioner: "a question cannot be 'begged' without someone to do the begging" (pp. 73–4).

According to Hamblin, Aristotle then turned away from this. In the *Prior Analytics* begging the question becomes "a species of failure to demonstrate" (p. 67), and the fallacies of language drop out (pp. 68–9). Dialectic came to look like a technique that was "unessential to the pursuit of truth", and at times "a hindrance" (p. 60): "Dialectic is standing in the way of his understanding of Logic" (p. 65). The moral Hamblin draws is: "we need to give up our tendency to see them as purely logical and see them instead as moves in the presentation of a contentious argument by one person to another" (pp. 65–6).

**Woods and Irvine** contest this reading at two points. First, they find "this tension between dialectic and logic somewhat misconceived" (p. 33). Hamblin is "certainly right" that the dialectical works mark the beginning of logical theory. But Aristotle would never have called dialectic unessential to truth. It is "an indispensable instrument of negative knowledge, of the discovery of what is not the truth", and a kind of induction that can lead inquirers to see first principles (p. 33 n. 12). Second, the thirteen are not *inherently* dialectical. A sophistical refutation is one that only *appears* to be a refutation, and that can happen "even when its premisses appear to be, and are, expressions of reputable opinion" (p. 57). They separate two senses of "dialectical". In the generic sense it means "an interchange of speech acts under conditions of challenge or test", the sense Hamblin and Hintikka use. In Aristotle's narrower sense it means arguing from reputable opinions (pp. 57–8). They also find it "rather strange" that Hamblin praises Aristotle for treating fallacies as dialectical while blaming him for wavering (pp. 96–7).

Woods and Irvine also answer the complaint that Aristotle's examples are silly. If a fallacy is an argument whose badness is not apparent, then a convincing example would be one the reader did not recognize as a fallacy, "and so in one clear sense of the term would also be a bad example" (p. 97). Hamblin's complaint about textbook examples (section 3) runs into the same difficulty.

### 5. The Tradition: Sextus, Boethius and the Obligation Game

Chapter 3 covers the long middle of the history. For the period from Aristotle to the eleventh century, the literature on fallacies "would hardly fill a small notebook" (p. 89).

- **The Stoics.** They classified bad arguments by four kinds of inconclusiveness: inconsistency, redundancy, bad form and deficiency. They treated these as "involuntary lapses of reasoning rather than as tricks of a sophist" (p. 92). Hamblin calls this "a catalogue rather than a doctrine" (p. 93).
- **Sextus Empiricus.** He made the most radical criticism of the whole idea of fallacy, and Hamblin later builds on it. The fallacies the dialectician can expose are useless to expose. The useful ones are caught by experts in each art, not by the dialectician (p. 95). His example is a medical argument in which only the doctor can see that "abatement" is used in two senses. When we face a plausible argument with an absurd conclusion, "we shall not assent to the absurdity just because of the argument but avoid the argument because of the absurdity", as one avoids a road that leads to a chasm (p. 96). Hamblin notes that "no one has ever taken up Sextus's criticisms of the concept of fallacy" (p. 97).
- **Boethius and Abelard.** Boethius derived six kinds of merely apparent contradiction from Aristotle's definition of refutation (pp. 104–6). Abelard searched Aristotle's "little book" for one of them, "Univocation", and found nothing (p. 109).
- **The thirteenth century.** William of Sherwood and Peter of Spain wrote the handbooks. Peter's *Summulae* was used into the nineteenth century (p. 115). William held that the acceptability of an inference "is inseparable from its producing belief regarding a doubtful matter" (p. 122). Hamblin treats this as an early epistemic criterion.
- **The obligation game** (pp. 125–9). This classroom disputation is Hamblin's main medieval precedent for his own proposal. The opponent proposes a *positum*, usually false (for example "Socrates is black"). The respondent must concede what follows from what he has granted, deny what is incompatible with it, and otherwise answer truly. The order of questions matters. The game ends with "Cedat tempus". On Hamblin's account it was an attempt to "reintroduce Dialectic into Logic" (p. 126).

### 6. Where the Arguments "Ad" Came From

Chapter 4 traces the modern list. Its centrepiece is the passage that started it, Locke's *Essay* IV.17.19–22 (pp. 159–60). Locke describes four kinds of argument "that men, in their reasonings with others, do ordinarily make use of to prevail on their assent":

1. **Ad verecundiam**: citing the authority of eminent men.
2. **Ad ignorantiam**: requiring the adversary "to admit what they allege as a proof, or to assign a better".
3. **Ad hominem**: pressing a man "with consequences drawn from his own principles or concessions".
4. **Ad judicium**: "proofs drawn from any of the foundations of knowledge or probability". Only this one "brings true instruction with it".

Hamblin's point is that this was "not intended as a classificatory treatment of fallacies", and that Locke "does not clearly condemn any of the argument-types" (pp. 160–61). In Locke's sense, ad hominem is the Socratic method of holding someone to their own admissions. Plecnik and Hoaglund single this out as one of the book's lasting corrections (p. 1). The phrase goes back to Aristotle's remark that some solutions are aimed "against the man, not against his argument", which became the Latin *non ad orationem, sed ad hominem* (SR 178b17; pp. 161–2). It was Isaac Watts (1725) who added new "ad" arguments and, Hamblin says, subtly altered the others "in perversion of Locke's original intentions" (pp. 163–4).

Two nineteenth-century writers are treated sympathetically:

- **Bentham's** *Book of Fallacies* lists political evasions: the "Hobgoblin Argument, or No Innovation", the "Wisdom of our ancestors: or Chinese argument", the procrastinator's argument, and question-begging terms such as *innovation* against *improvement*. Bentham hoped members of Parliament would meet a fallacy with voices "crying aloud 'Stale! Stale! Fallacy of Authority!'" (pp. 165–8, 284). Hamblin defends his motive: "Bentham is no Machiavelli: he always teaches in order to unteach" (p. 168).
- **Whately** came closest to Hamblin's own view. His theory of **presumption** and **burden of proof** says a presumption is "a pre-occupation of the ground" that stands until a sufficient reason is brought against it (pp. 170–72). An argument ad hominem shows not "that 'such and such is the fact,' but that 'this man is bound to admit it'". Whately notes in a footnote that such arguments often have "the effect of shifting the burden of proof, not unjustly". On this view, "The fallaciousness depends upon the deceit, or attempt to deceive" (pp. 173–4). Hamblin calls the idea in the footnote "an excellent and interesting one" (p. 175).

### 7. The Indian Control

In chapter 5 Hamblin uses the **Nyaya** tradition as a "control", an independent history to compare with the Greek one (p. 10). The *Nyaya-sutra* gives a five-member argument:

- Thesis: "The hill is fiery".
- Reason: "Because it has smoke".
- Example: "Whatever is smoky is fiery, like a kitchen".
- Application.
- Conclusion (p. 178).

There is also a ten-member version set out as a dialogue, which shows that the schema was "aimed at representing the presentation of an argument to others; that is, at Rhetoric or Dialectic, not pure Logic" (p. 179).

The Nyaya debate manuals include five fallacious reasons, three kinds of controversy (discussion, disputation and wrangling), 24 **futile rejoinders** (*jati*) and 22 **clinchers** (*nigrahasthana*), the situations in which a debater is defeated (pp. 180–86). Hamblin sees a "startling similarity" to Aristotle. Either there was contact, or "formal debate is an important or necessary ingredient of any intellectual culture at a certain stage of its development, and is the driving force behind the development of Logic" (p. 186). Later Navya-Nyaya dropped the debate material, so: "The development of Logic out of a theory of debate, and its ultimate repudiation of its origins, thus seems ultimately to have taken the same course in India as in the West" (p. 189).

**Ganeri's** chapter supports Hamblin's picture and fills it in. The *Nyaya-sutra* distinguishes three kinds of debate (NS 1.2.1–3; p. 322):

- **Good debate** (*vada*): proof and refutation based on proper evidence.
- **Tricky debate** (*jalpa*): uses quibbles (*chala*), false rejoinders (*jati*) and clinchers.
- **Refutation-only debate** (*vitanda*): the opponent's thesis is attacked but no counter-thesis is defended.

Ganeri's comparison follows Hamblin's: "if dialectic is a rough kin of *vada*, and sophistic of *jalpa*, then the Socratic elenchus could be regarded as a species of *vitanda*" (p. 322). He also confirms the turn Hamblin describes. The *Nyaya-sutra* "begins a transformation", away from "the place of argumentation within dialectic and debate" and toward "the more formal properties of sound inference" (p. 321). Ganeri dates the story earlier, to the question-and-answer contests of the Upanishads and the *Questions of King Milinda* (pp. 309–10). In those dialogues the loser is censured "based on reasons he himself will acknowledge" (p. 310). Hamblin's later theory is built on the same idea: a refutation counts only if it is drawn from the opponent's own commitments.

### 8. Formal Fallacies and the Limits of Form

Chapter 6 asks whether formal logic can do the job instead. Hamblin rules out one response at the start: "Least of all should one aim at producing a new classification of the old material, yet another tree" (p. 191). He lists seven assumptions logic has made since Aristotle. One is that arguments are "impersonal and context-free" (p. 192). He then tests formal treatments one by one.

- **What formal tools can handle.** The consequent, non-cause and the masked man can be formalized. The masked man becomes a problem of substitution in opaque contexts, in Frege's and Quine's sense (pp. 206–8).
- **Secundum quid** cannot be fixed by adding qualifying words. English already has "wholly" and "mainly", yet "there is no end to the further qualifications a statement might carry … the Fallacy of Secundum Quid is an ever-present and unavoidable possibility" (pp. 212–13).
- **Begging the question** is not mere equivalence of premiss and conclusion: "There is nothing wrong with equivalence of premiss and conclusion in a proof-process, provided the premiss is acceptable for reasons independent of the conclusion" (p. 215).
- **Many questions** can be partly analysed with Belnap's and Åqvist's logic of questions. But the fault lies only in questions whose presupposition is false, and at that point formal analysis has to borrow dialectical ideas (pp. 215–18).

The verdict on a general formal account of the traditional material "seems to be a simple 'No'" (p. 205).

### 9. What an Argument Is

Chapter 7 begins: "A fallacy is a fallacious argument. Someone who merely makes false statements, however absurd, is innocent of fallacy unless the statements constitute or express an argument" (p. 224). So before we can convict anyone of a fallacy we need to know what an argument is, and when one has been put forward. In Hamblin's phrase, a speaker cannot be convicted "until he can have an argument pinned on him" (pp. 224–5).

Hamblin sets out three sets of criteria for a good argument:

| Criteria | What they require |
|---|---|
| **Alethic** (1)–(4) | Premisses true; conclusion implied; following reasonably immediately; unstated premisses omissible (pp. 234–5) |
| **Epistemic** (E1)–(E5) | Premisses *known* to be true; the step clear; and (E5) the conclusion would be in doubt without the argument (pp. 236–8) |
| **Dialectical** (D1)–(D5) | "The premisses must be accepted"; the passage "of an accepted kind"; and (D5) "in the absence of the argument, it would not be accepted" (p. 245) |

The alethic criteria are not enough, because true premisses that nobody knows persuade no one. The epistemic criteria are too strong, because everyday argument works from what is accepted, not from what is known. That leads to the dialectical criteria, relative to a particular hearer: "We are, in fact, talking about the class of arguments that Aristotle called dialectical, and that Locke called ad hominem" (p. 241).

This answers an old objection. Mill held that "in every syllogism, considered as an argument to prove the conclusion, there is a petitio principii" (p. 226). Sextus had said the same (p. 95). Hamblin replies that criterion (D5) makes a question-begging argument a bad argument by definition, since its conclusion is no less accepted than its premisses. So "the truth is not that all good arguments are question-begging, but that none are" (p. 247). He adds that it is "totally misleading" to treat formal proof as the model of rational argument, since formal proof does not rule out circularity (pp. 248–9). And no theory can guarantee that the logically sound argument wins in practice: that would require that "Right must be Might" (p. 251).

### 10. Formal Dialectic: Commitment Stores and System H

Chapter 8 contains the book's constructive proposal. A dialectical system is "a regulated dialogue or family of dialogues" (p. 255). "Logic can be conceived as a set of dialectical conventions" (p. 256). Each participant has a **commitment store**, a running record of what they have committed themselves to. It "need not correspond with his real beliefs" (p. 257):

> "We do not believe everything we say; but our saying it commits us whether we believe it or not." (p. 264)

Hamblin first formalizes the medieval obligation game (pp. 260–63). He then builds **System H**, a "Why-Because system with questions" (pp. 265–8). Two players, White and Black, take turns. The permitted moves are "Statement S", "No commitment S", "Question S, T …?", "Why S?" and "Resolve S".

- **Syntactic rules** (S1–S5) set the legal replies. For example, "Why S?" must be answered by withdrawing S or by supplying grounds T together with T ⊃ S.
- **Commitment rules** (C1–C5) say how each move adds to or removes from each player's store.

Fallacies are handled by adding rules, not by listing faults:

- **Many questions** is banned by a rule that a question "may occur only when S∨T∨…∨X is already a commitment of both speaker and hearer" (p. 269).
- **Begging the question** is banned by requiring that "Why S?" be asked only when S is the hearer's commitment and not the speaker's. Any argument that terminates under the resulting rules cannot be circular "when seen as a whole" (pp. 271–2).
- **Burden of proof** is replaced by the idea of **initiative**: "He who asserts must prove" (pp. 274–5).
- The three senses of "why" (asking for proof, asking for justification, asking for a cause) explain how non-cause as cause arose (pp. 273–4).

Hamblin does not overclaim. It is "not even certain that it is possible to legislate for good sense by purely dialectical means", but the system shows "how much can be achieved with comparatively meagre resources" (p. 275). He ends the chapter by modelling the Greek game, in which the questioner keeps no commitment store (Thrasymachus complains that Socrates will "refuse to answer himself"; pp. 276–7), and an inductive game that could be played with cards (pp. 280–82).

**Ganeri** applies the commitment store to the Buddhist *Kathavatthu*. In its "way forward", the proponent first gets the opponent to commit openly to two propositions. He then shows that those commitments entail a third, and finally that this third clashes with the rest. Ganeri notes that Walton later distinguished open from "veiled" commitments, which a participant may not even know they hold, because in Hamblin's model all commitments are "on public display" (pp. 318–19). The goal of such a move is "not to disprove the thesis, but to force a retraction of commitment". It is therefore judged by how well it does that, "and not simply or only or even in terms of its deductive or inductive soundness" (p. 319). This is Hamblin's dialectical criterion applied to a text he did not discuss.

### 11. Equivocation as a Point of Order

The last chapter is the most philosophical. Hamblin asks who enforces the rules of a dialogue. In ordinary talk there is no chairman: "The control of each dialogue is in the hands of the participants themselves" (p. 283). He distinguishes **points of order**, which are remarks about how the dialogue is being conducted ("That doesn't follow", "That is irrelevant"), from **topic points**. He argues that the most common use of logical terms, including the names of fallacies, is to make points of order. A good classification of fallacies should help people raise them, as Bentham wanted (p. 284).

He then tests equivocation against several theories of meaning:

- **Ask the speaker.** This fails when thinking is dominated by a model. A staff officer who treats battalions like chessmen cannot tell "located" (found) from "located" (placed) (pp. 287–9).
- **Patterns of use.** Quine and Grice and Strawson offer accounts of what a group will revise or find incomprehensible (pp. 289–91). This creates a puzzle. Take a man who never separates legal from moral obligation and says "Ignorance of the law is no excuse" with moral disapproval. His usage is consistent, so in his own usage the word is not equivocal. If an argument deceives deeply enough, it gives its users a coherent pattern of use, "and thereby destroy[s] the supposition that the words are, for him, equivocal" (pp. 292–3).
- **A presumption.** Meaning-constancy should be presumed "in the absence of evidence to the contrary", like the presumption of innocence (pp. 294–5).

Hamblin's conclusion is that subtle equivocation "should sometimes be regarded less as a fallacy than as an idiom in disuse" (p. 296). Here he takes up Sextus again: the two slaves both called Manes, and the road to the chasm (pp. 296–8). Sometimes we find out that an inference is invalid only after we know its conclusion is false (p. 298). Logic is "at least partly a normative enterprise" (p. 299). A charge of equivocation may be one of several ways out of a threatened contradiction. Where a new model has given a word a new use, calling it equivocal may be "at best academic and at worst obstructionist" (p. 300).

His thesis: "equivocation is a procedural, non-topical concept … 'That is equivocal' does not describe a locution, but objects to it" (pp. 300–301). A speaker cannot coherently say "P, therefore Q, and that's equivocal", just as he cannot say "S and that's false" (pp. 301–2). And the logician cannot escape by claiming to be a neutral onlooker. That is "an intellectual fancy that is both comforting and delusive" (p. 303). The book ends:

> "No one will understand equivocation who thinks that words permanently retain their meanings; or, particularly, who thinks that it is his task to make them do so. The road to an understanding of equivocation, then, is the understanding of charges of equivocation." (p. 303)

### 12. Woods and Irvine: The Ambiguity of "Dialectical"

Woods and Irvine's chapter is mainly an account of Aristotle's early logic. Their engagement with Hamblin is a running objection, and it is worth stating on its own. On their reading, Aristotle has a logical core, the broad notion of a syllogism, embedded in debate. A real refutation must meet extra conditions on which premisses are allowed, since the refuter has to draw them from the answerer's concessions. These conditions are not themselves syllogistic (p. 46). So "an argument might be a perfectly good syllogism and yet be a perfectly bad refutation" (p. 46). This lets them accept most of what Hamblin says about the debate setting while denying that it is in tension with logic.

They make three specific points against Hamblin:

1. **Not every fallacy is a dialogue fault.** Hamblin and Hintikka are right that begging the question and many questions depend on a questioner (p. 46 n. 25, citing Hamblin pp. 73–4). But a refutation can merely appear valid even when its premisses really are reputable. So the thirteen as a whole are types of bad reasoning that occur *in* dialogue. They are not defined *by* it (p. 57).
2. **Two senses of "dialectical".** Hamblin's generic sense, an interchange under challenge, is not Aristotle's technical sense of arguing from reputable opinion (pp. 57–8). Running the two together makes Aristotle seem to waver.
3. **Begging the question has several forms in Aristotle.** In *On Sophistical Refutations* it violates the definition of a syllogism. In the *Posterior Analytics* it is a failure of demonstration. The *Topics* lists five ways of begging, one of them, in Hamblin's phrase, begging "a conjunctive conclusion piecemeal" (p. 91, citing Hamblin p. 74). The different forms answer to different settings, so no single dialectical account covers them all.

### 13. What the Book Started

**Plecnik and Hoaglund** end their preface by saying what Hamblin left to his successors. "As formal fallacies are a breach of certain deduction rules, so the informal fallacies are a breach of dialectical criteria that govern a particular form of dialogue" (p. 4). That gives three types of fallacy: **deductive**, **inductive** and **dialectical** (p. 4). It also explains why textbook authors disagree about fallacies: each works with an unstated dialectical system (p. 4). Mill's charge that all deduction begs the question arises when the dialectical context is missing. Many questions makes sense only in a dialogue (pp. 4–5).

The three readings agree that Hamblin was right on two points: the textbook treatment was empty, and the debate setting matters. They disagree about how far to go:

- **Plecnik and Hoaglund** take the dialectical turn as the right foundation for informal logic.
- **Woods and Irvine** keep a logical core that does not depend on dialogue, and read Aristotle as already holding the two together.
- **Ganeri** shows that commitment, retraction and burden of proof are the right tools for an entire non-Western tradition. That supports Hamblin's claim that logic grew out of debate in more than one culture.

### Conclusion

Hamblin's argument comes down to this. The traditional definition of a fallacy, "an argument that seems valid but is not", fails. The examples deceive nobody. The list overlaps with itself. And many items on it, such as ad hominem in Locke's sense, appeals to authority and arguments from ignorance, are often good arguments. The cause is that logic since Aristotle has treated arguments as impersonal and free of context. Fallacies, though, are faults in what one person does to persuade another. His remedy has three parts. Judge arguments by dialectical criteria: what is accepted by the hearer. Model dialogue formally with commitment stores and rules. And treat names like "equivocation" as points of order raised within a dialogue, not descriptions of sentences. Woods and Irvine show that his history makes Aristotle more of a dialectician than he was. Ganeri shows that his model fits the Indian debate tradition, which was his own "control" case. Plecnik and Hoaglund show how much of later informal logic grew from his question, "what is an argument?"
