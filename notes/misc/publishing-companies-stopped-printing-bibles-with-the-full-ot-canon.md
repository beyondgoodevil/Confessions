Publishing companies began omitting the “Apocrypha” (more accurately called the Deuterocanonical books) from most editions of the King James Version (KJV) in the mid-19th century, around the 1820s–1880s, primarily due to Protestant pressure—especially from groups like the British and Foreign Bible Society.

### Full Historical Overview

### 1. 1611 KJV Included the Apocrypha

The original 1611 King James Bible included the full Apocrypha, placed between the Old and New Testaments. This was standard practice at the time and reflected the practice of earlier English Bibles like the Great Bible (1539) and the Geneva Bible (1560). The Church of England accepted these books for reading in church, though not for doctrine (Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles).

Books included:

- Tobit
- Judith
- Wisdom of Solomon
- Ecclesiasticus (Sirach)
- Baruch
- 1 & 2 Maccabees
- Additions to Esther and Daniel (Prayer of Azariah, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon)

### 2. Usage Until the 18th Century

Throughout the 17th and 18th centuries, the Apocrypha continued to appear in KJV editions. It was printed as a distinct section, and even Protestant authorities—especially in Anglican and Lutheran traditions—allowed its use for “instruction in life and manners.”

### 3. Shift Begins – Early 19th Century

A turning point came in the early 1800s, when radical Protestant groups—notably Reformed, Baptists, and Puritans—began to object to the inclusion of the Apocrypha. They viewed it as unbiblical, citing its supposed "Romanist" origins.

The British and Foreign Bible Society (BFBS), a major publisher and distributor of the Bible, officially resolved in 1826 to no longer fund or distribute Bibles that included the Apocrypha. This decision had major consequences, as the BFBS had tremendous influence over English Bible publishing.

### 4. Spread to the U.S.

American Protestant publishers, following the lead of British societies and reflecting strong Puritanical and Evangelical sentiment, increasingly printed KJVs without the Apocrypha throughout the 19th century. By the 1880s, the omission of the Apocrypha became the norm in American KJV Bibles.

### 5. 20th Century to Today

Today, most KJV editions do not include the Apocrypha, especially those produced by Evangelical and Baptist publishers. However, academic presses (like Cambridge University Press or Oxford University Press) and some Anglican or Lutheran editions still offer it.

### Final Note (Orthodox Perspective)

For Eastern Orthodox Christians, the term “Apocrypha” is problematic and misleading. These are Deuterocanonical books, part of the Septuagint, and fully recognized by the Orthodox Church as Scripture. The removal of these books from the KJV is seen not merely as a publishing trend, but as a doctrinal error stemming from Protestant innovations that broke with both the ancient Church and the biblical tradition inherited from the Apostles.
