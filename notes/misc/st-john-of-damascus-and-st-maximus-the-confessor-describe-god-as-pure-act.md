---

Both St. John of Damascus and St. Maximus the Confessor describe God as "pure act," though not in the same philosophical sense as Thomas Aquinas. For them, this expression emphasizes God’s absolute actuality, immutability, and infinite activity. Here’s why they use this concept:

1. God is Perfect and Lacks Potential: Both saints affirm that God is entirely actual with no unfulfilled potential. Unlike created beings, which are a mix of potentiality (what they can become) and actuality (what they are), God is fully complete and unchanging. This reflects the Eastern Christian understanding of God's immutability.
2. Infinite Energy and Activity: In their writings, particularly within the Eastern Christian framework, God’s essence is understood as beyond comprehension, while His energies (activities) are how He relates to and sustains creation. Calling God "pure act" underscores His constant, unceasing activity without change or division.
3. Scriptural and Theological Basis: For instance, St. John of Damascus in *The Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* emphasizes that God is not composed of parts or subject to change. Similarly, St. Maximus elaborates on God’s self-existent and eternal nature, often discussing how creation participates in God’s energies without altering His essence.
4. Refutation of Anthropomorphism: Calling God "pure act" avoids viewing Him as a being bound by time or space, who might "grow" or "change." This aligns with the patristic goal of preserving God’s transcendence and infinite perfection.

While they borrow and adapt this terminology, the meaning differs from Aristotelian or Thomistic notions, focusing more on God’s dynamic relationship with creation rather than solely philosophical abstraction.
