The *Iliad* begins with politics before it begins with battle. A priest offers ransom for his daughter; "all the rest of the Achaians cried out in favour" of accepting it, but Agamemnon refuses (1.22–25). A plague follows, and on the tenth day Achilles, prompted by Hera, "called the people to assembly" (1.54). The quarrel that drives the whole poem happens in front of the army, in a meeting whose rules are unclear: who may speak, who decides, and what happens when the king is wrong. The poem returns to these questions repeatedly — in the disastrous testing of the army and Thersites' outburst in Book 2, Diomedes' challenge in Book 9, the Trojan debates between Hector and Polydamas, and the reconciliation in Book 19.

Older scholarship, following Moses Finley's *The World of Odysseus*, tended to treat this as pre-political: a king who could ignore any opinion, a people who could only shout, and speeches that were emotional appeals rather than reasoning. This note sets out five later readings that take the *Iliad*'s politics seriously in different ways. The broader history of Greek political thought from Homer to the sophists is covered in [[Politics Before Philosophy: Greek Political Thought from Homer to the Sophists]], and Ahrensdorf's reading of Homer as a philosopher in [[Homer as Political Philosopher: Ahrensdorf on Homer, Plato, Machiavelli and Nietzsche]].

### 1. Van Wees: A System of Government

Hans van Wees, a historian, argues that the poems describe a coherent, if simple, system. A community is dominated by princely families (*basileis*), each with a sceptre; the men who actually exercise power are the elders (*gerontes*); and one "most princely" man is the ruler (*anax*). He thinks the evidence favours "the traditional view that Homeric monarchs occupy a formal, public, hereditary position", against the then-popular view of the king as an informal "big man" (pp. 31–32).

The elders settle disputes, receive foreign guests, decide strategy and divide plunder. Most decisions are taken in their own meetings, fed at public expense: the princes drink "things of the community" (pp. 32–33). When there is a public matter, heralds call the men to an assembly, sit them on the ground and tell them to "listen to the princes". Only princes are meant to speak, holding the sceptre in turn. "The people may shout in agreement or silently disapprove, but they have no vote or any say in the matter", unless the princes are divided and the people "vote with their feet" by following one side out (p. 33).

But van Wees stresses what this implies. Decisions are spoken of as acts of the whole people: "the Trojans" grant land, Achilles tells Agamemnon "you [plural] take away what you have given" (1.299), and the gods punish whole peoples for crooked verdicts. Plunder is gathered into a central space and divided into prizes for the best men and fair portions for all. The system "recognises a distinction between public and private affairs" and "endows the community with a sort of passive sovereignty in public matters" (pp. 35–36). Against the argument that assemblies were rare because Ithaca held none for twenty years, he points out that in the *Iliad* both armies meet almost daily: assemblies are called "whenever a decision needs to be made" (pp. 33–34).

### 2. Hammer: Plebiscitary Politics

Dean Hammer, a political theorist, finds three models in earlier scholarship and finds each incomplete (pp. 145–52). The *royal* model sees a king ruling quiescent subjects by divine right. The *class* model — Ian Morris, Walter Donlan in one mood, William Thalmann — sees the epic as an "ideological device" of an aristocracy, in which the people are "ignored" almost "to the point of total exclusion". The *integrationist* model sees a chief or big man bound to his followers by reciprocal gifts. Hammer objects that the class readings are "curiously undialectical", and that the people, though they "do not vote and do not make binding decisions", are not "compliant, inert, absent, or silenced" either (p. 150).

His alternative, drawn from Max Weber, is "plebiscitary politics": a system in which "the decisions of leaders derive at least part of their legitimacy from the acclaim, or perceived acclaim, of the people" (pp. 146–47). Decisions are "enacted" in a public space. The Achaeans shout their approval for Chryses and later for Diomedes; Homer gives their shout the vocabulary of the war-cry, lending the people's voice heroic force (pp. 154–55). But "in plebiscitary politics … acclaim is up for grabs", which makes the field volatile (p. 155). Agamemnon in Book 2 tries to shore up his authority by stirring the army and is nearly abandoned by it. Hector repeatedly uses the crowd to override Polydamas, and in Book 18 the poet himself condemns the result:

> So spoke Hektor, and the Trojans thundered to hear him;
> fools, since Pallas Athene had taken away the wits from them.
> They gave their applause to Hektor in his counsel of evil,
> but none to Poulydamas, who had spoken good sense before them.
> (18.310–13)

Hammer argues that this produces a "political heroism": the assembly is the place "where men win glory" (1.490), and Phoenix was sent to make Achilles "a speaker of words and one accomplished in action" (9.443). It requires "soundness" and "good sense" in leaders and concern for the common good, as Nestor tells Agamemnon: "when many assemble together follow him who advises / the best counsel" (9.74–75) (pp. 157–59).

On *themis*, customary right, Hammer argues that the quarrel is not simply a breach of norms but exposes the "untenability of themis as a prerogative of the king" (p. 132). Achilles calls Agamemnon "King who feed on your people" (1.231) and insists that booty once distributed belongs to a public act; Thersites echoes him: the women in Agamemnon's huts are those "whom we Achaians / give to you first of all" (2.227–28). Diomedes, who stood silent before Agamemnon's insults in Book 4, claims in Book 9 that speaking against the king is "my right, lord, in this assembly" (9.33). Hammer sees the beginnings of collegiality among the elite and suggests that this plebiscitary space was "a laboratory for democracy" from which both tyranny and later democratic forms emerged (pp. 132–33, 161–62).

On Thersites himself Hammer is brief: he is punished "not because he lies (he actually speaks accurately) but because his lower status does not allow him to speak in this way against the king", and his deformed body is the poem's way of marking that status (pp. 60–61).

### 3. Barker: The Iliad Founds Dissent

Elton Barker, in *Entering the Agon*, reads the four Achaean assemblies (Books 1, 2, 9 and 19) as a sequence rather than as evidence for a fixed institution. His thesis is that the *Iliad* "brings dissent within the boundaries of the assembly over the course of its narrative" and in doing so involves its audience in "founding a political community" (pp. 38–39).

**Book 1.** The assembly is called for the community's benefit, and Achilles promises the seer Calchas protection to speak against the king. Calchas' fear — "a king is stronger, when he is angry with a lesser man" — sets "power against speaking freely, obedience against persuasion" (p. 42). Agamemnon's obsessive repetition that Achilles wants to be "above all… over all… to all" shows him losing control, and he is the only speaker in Homer to be interrupted (p. 43). When Achilles reaches for his sword, Athena intervenes. For Barker this "marks a limit to dissent": it does not say Achilles is wrong, but "dissent cannot, and does not, equal violence" (p. 50). The assembly fails because dissent is contained only by a personal bond between hero and goddess, "and not through the public institution — yet" (p. 51).

**Book 2 and Thersites.** The second assembly is chaotic from the start: Agamemnon acts on a false dream, tests the army with a speech meant to be disbelieved, and nearly loses it (pp. 53–55). Then Thersites speaks. The narrator's introduction is "the most detailed and evaluative of its kind": he knew "many words, but disorderly", spoke "with any word he thought might be amusing to the Argives", and "was the ugliest man who came beneath Ilion" (2.213–16; Barker, p. 56). Barker notes a real ambiguity in the next lines: the Achaeans were "furiously angry with him" — with Thersites, or with Agamemnon, whom Thersites then voices (p. 57)? After Odysseus beats him, the army laughs "sorry though the men were" (2.270), and says this is Odysseus' best deed yet. Scholars' readings, Barker observes, "tend to betray their own ideological positions" (p. 58). His own view is double. Thersites' speech is "closely modelled on Achilles'", and the danger is that a parodist will make the audience trivialise Achilles' serious challenge. Yet "even as it suppresses a voice of dissent, the Iliad makes its audience aware that this is what it is doing" (pp. 60–61). He notes too that Odysseus departs from Athena's instruction to restrain each man "with soft words": he uses soft words on princes and the sceptre on commoners (p. 59 n.).

**Book 9.** Agamemnon repeats his speech urging flight, now in earnest. Diomedes replies, "I shall be the first to fight with your folly, / as is my right, lord, in this assembly; then do not be angered" (9.32–33). Barker stresses that "it is the custom" is used only by characters, never by the narrator: Diomedes applies the phrase "prescriptively, not descriptively". By saying it, he makes the assembly a place where disagreement with the king is allowed, building on Achilles' precedent. "Diomedes institutionalizes dissent" (pp. 62–63).

**The Trojans.** The Trojan assembly meets at Priam's door, not at a neutral place like the Achaean assembly by Odysseus' ships in the middle of the line. When Antenor proposes returning Helen, Paris refuses, and Priam simply rules for his son. The Trojan assembly "is not an institution that accommodates dissent; moreover, that difference offers one explanation for their eventual defeat" (pp. 67–69).

Barker ends with the trial scene on Achilles' shield and the funeral games of Book 23, where Achilles sets the army "in a wide agon" and settles disputes over prizes. "Truly, a new kind of politics is born" — not in the poem's world, which never reaches the *polis*, but in an audience that has experienced the assembly "as a series of struggles, not as a closed system" (pp. 84–87).

### 4. Schofield: Good Counsel as a Heroic Virtue

Malcolm Schofield's essay of 1986 takes on Finley directly. Finley had written that "never in either the *Iliad* or the *Odyssey* is there a rational discussion, a sustained, disciplined consideration of circumstances and their implications, of possible courses of action, their advantages and disadvantages", and that Homeric "counsel" meant authority, not reasoning (Schofield, pp. 231–32). Schofield suggests that Plato's "Utopian hijacking" of *euboulia* — good counsel — into guardianship has led historians of ideas to neglect it (pp. 220–21).

He shows first how highly the poem rates it. Excellence in counsel is paired with prowess in war as the two ways a man outshines his peers; heroes are called "counsellors" and "elders" regardless of age; Achilles in his wrath stays away from "the assembly where men win glory" (pp. 225–27). Diomedes' speech in Book 9 "is in its own way as much a feat of prowess as one of his exploits on the battlefield" (p. 233). This complicates Finley's "complete and unambiguous" heroic code. If good counsel is a heroic virtue, the hero committed to it must listen to reason, and reason may raise claims — prudence, pity, justice — other than honour. Esteem for *euboulia* "introduces an open-endedness into the heroic code" (pp. 236–39).

His case studies support this. Polydamas, Hector's companion "born on a single night", is a counsellor of "advantage and safety and never of honour"; the poet calls his advice good and Hector later admits, "by my own recklessness I have ruined my people" (22.104; Schofield, pp. 240–42). Hector's tragedy shows "a dynamic tension within the heroic ethic" between honour and the protection of his city and family. The hero "need not be trapped" by honour "if like Odysseus he remains heroically attentive to the demands of euboulia" (pp. 244–45). The informal council of the wounded leaders in Book 14 is exactly the reasoned deliberation Finley denied: a shared assessment, alternatives weighed, Agamemnon's defeatist plan rejected, Diomedes' solution adopted because it is best. "Finley's fact is not a fact" (pp. 246–48). Nestor's mediation in Book 1 gives reasons of justice as well as prudence, inviting each man to consider "the other man's point of view" (pp. 254–55). Schofield concludes that Homeric reasoning is narrower and less self-critical than the debate on Mytilene in Thucydides, but rejects "the primitivist step" of saying the Homeric hero "is not really rational at all" (p. 259).

### 5. Nagy: Thersites and Blame Poetry

Gregory Nagy reads Thersites not as a political figure but as a poetic one. Epic, in Nagy's account, presents itself as allied to praise poetry and opposed to blame poetry. Thersites is described in the vocabulary of blame. He is *aiskhistos*, "most base", and *ekhthistos*, "most hateful", to Achilles and Odysseus, "precisely because it is his function to blame them". His speech is introduced and concluded with *neikos*, "quarrel", and he makes *eris*, "strife", against kings. He aims at what is *geloion*, "laughable", which is Aristotle's word for comedy's function. His name parallels *Margites*, the comic poem in the Homeric tradition. And his ugliness "serves to mirror in form the content of his blame poetry" (pp. 259–63). Nagy notes that Thersites misrepresents the *Iliad* itself: he says Achilles cannot really be angry, or he would have killed Agamemnon (2.241–42), denying the poem's own subject. "In the Thersites episode of the *Iliad*, it is Epos that gets the last laugh on the blame poet" (p. 262). Nagy also notes that the phrase "not according to the established order" (*kata kosmon*) implies that blame, when justified, had a legitimate social function (p. 262 n.).

### 6. Thersites Read Five Ways

The episode is a test case for all five readings.

| Reader | Who is Thersites? | Why is he silenced? | What does it show? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Van Wees | A commoner speaking where only princes should | He breaks the rule that the people listen | The formal limits of the assembly |
| Hammer | A low-status man who "speaks accurately" | Status, not falsehood | Rank still governs who may speak, even in a plebiscitary space |
| Barker | A parodic double of Achilles | His mockery threatens to trivialise real dissent | The poem bounds dissent while exposing its own act of suppression |
| Schofield | One of the "loudmouths" who are "of no account in war or in counsel" | He offers no counsel, only abuse | Counsel, not mere speech, is what the assembly honours |
| Nagy | The blame poet within epic | Epic defeats its rival genre | Epos defines itself as praise against blame |

Lattimore's translation makes the content of his speech hard to dismiss. He says that Agamemnon's huts are full of bronze and women "whom we Achaians / give to you first of all whenever we capture some stronghold", and that Agamemnon "has dishonored Achilleus, a man much better / than he is" (2.226–40). This is Achilles' own complaint. Odysseus does not answer it. He tells Thersites, "Fluent orator though you be, … your words are / ill considered", says no one is worse, threatens to strip him, and strikes him with the sceptre (2.246–68). Earlier in the same book Odysseus told commoners, "Lordship for many is no good thing. Let there be one ruler, / one king" (2.204–05).

### 7. Assessment

The five readings can mostly be combined. Van Wees's description of the institutions is the necessary base: there are rulers, elders, heralds, sceptres and regular assemblies, and decisions are made by princes in the people's name. Hammer adds the dynamics: in practice leaders seek acclaim, and that acclaim can be manipulated, as by Agamemnon in Book 2 and by Hector in Book 18. Barker's reading of the assemblies as a sequence is convincing on its main point. The poem moves from a seer who needs a hero's protection to speak, to a prince who claims the right to oppose the king as custom. The contrast with Priam's assembly, where a son overrides Antenor and no one replies, is pointed and consequential.

Schofield's correction of Finley is the most decisive. The Book 14 council and the Polydamas scenes show sustained, reasoned deliberation, and the poem consistently praises good counsel and condemns those who ignore it. Hector's ruin is presented as a failure of *euboulia*, not of courage.

The Thersites episode resists a single reading. Nagy's evidence that the language of blame poetry surrounds Thersites is strong, and it explains the ugliness and the laughter better than a purely political reading. But his content repeats Achilles' grievance, and the narrator's syntax leaves it unclear whom the army is angry with. Barker's account fits best: the poem does draw a line, placing violent abuse by a man of no standing outside the assembly, but it lets the audience see the line being drawn and see that the complaint was just. Hammer's point stands too. Diomedes can say "as is my right" in Book 9; no one says that for Thersites.

What the *Iliad* does not show is the people deciding anything. Van Wees is right that they have no vote; Hammer and Barker are right that their response matters. The poem's political interest lies with the leaders: whether they listen, whether they can be opposed, and whether they serve the common good or "feed on" the people.

### Conclusion

The *Iliad* is a political poem in a concrete sense: its central catastrophe begins with a ruler who will not listen in a public meeting, and its plot turns on assemblies, councils and arguments about the right to speak. Van Wees shows a working system of princes, elders and a people with passive sovereignty. Hammer describes a volatile plebiscitary politics in which leaders depend on acclaim and *themis* becomes a public claim rather than a royal prerogative. Barker reads the four Achaean assemblies as the poem's founding of an institution that can contain dissent, set against Trojan assemblies that cannot. Schofield shows that good counsel is a heroic virtue and that the poem contains real reasoned deliberation, against Finley. Nagy reads Thersites as the blame poet that epic defines itself against. Taken together, they show a poem that values open debate among the elite, punishes leaders who ignore good counsel, and silences the one commoner who speaks — while leaving his complaint unanswered.
