Aristotle built a logic of **terms**: "every A is B". The Stoics, above all **Chrysippus** (c. 280–206 BC), built a logic of whole **propositions** joined by "if", "and" and "or". Its centre is a small set of five self-evident argument forms, the **indemonstrables**, with rules for reducing every other valid argument to them. Nineteenth-century historians dismissed it as hair-splitting. Twentieth-century logicians (Łukasiewicz, Mates) rediscovered it as an early propositional calculus. Since then scholars have argued about how far that comparison holds. This note covers the logic itself and sets out the two readings I studied. The *Cambridge History of Hellenistic Philosophy* (Barnes, Bobzien and Mignucci) reconstructs Stoic syllogistic as a near-formal system. **O'Toole and Jennings** argue that reading it through modern logic distorts it, and that it was a theory of inference about the world, grounded in Stoic physics, psychology and ethics. None of the Stoic books survive, so every quotation below comes from later reporters, mostly hostile ones such as Sextus Empiricus, in the translations the two works give.

### 1. Why Stoic Logic Was Forgotten and Rediscovered

O'Toole and Jennings open with the verdict of Carl Prantl's 19th-century history of logic. According to Prantl, Chrysippus "descended to a pitiful degree of dullness, triviality, and scholastic quibbling", and it was "a real stroke of luck" that his works were lost before the Middle Ages (p. 397, in Mates's translation). Zeller thought the Stoics had only dressed Peripatetic logic "with a new terminology" (p. 403). Prantl was following Kant's claim that logic had not advanced "a single step" since Aristotle. The rescue came with Łukasiewicz, who said "the history of logic must be written anew, and by an historian who has fully mastered mathematical logic", and with Mates's *Stoic Logic* (1953) (p. 398).

The Cambridge History is the product of that rescue. It states the main thesis plainly: "If Aristotelian logic is essentially a logic of terms, Stoic logic is in its core a propositional logic" (p. 92).

O'Toole and Jennings agree that the rescue was needed but warn against a "convergence" theory of history. That is the assumption that the Stoics were aiming at our propositional calculus and should be graded on how close they came (p. 398). They quote Charles Kahn: "We may not have an accurate picture of Chrysippus' enterprise in 'dialectic' if we see it simply as a brilliant anticipation of the propositional calculus" (p. 400). The rest of this note keeps both views in play.

There is also a problem with the sources. Of Chrysippus' 705 books, 311 on logic (D.L. vii.180, 198), nothing survives except some damaged papyri from Herculaneum. We depend on Diogenes Laertius, who is friendly but late, and on Sextus, Plutarch, Cicero and Alexander, who quote the Stoics mostly to refute them (O'Toole and Jennings, pp. 402–3, 413).

### 2. Who They Were: Dialecticians and the Stoa

**Zeno of Citium** (333–261 BC) taught in the Painted Colonnade (*Stoa Poikilē*), which gave the school its name (D.L. vii.5). Before founding it he studied with the Cynic Crates, with Stilpo of Megara, and with the logicians **Diodorus Cronus** and **Philo** (O'Toole and Jennings, pp. 404–5). Following David Sedley, O'Toole and Jennings argue that Diodorus belonged to a separate **Dialectical school**, not the Megarian school proper, and that this school was the real source of Stoic logic (pp. 406–7). The Cambridge History puts "Megarics" in scare quotes for the same reason.

The anecdotes give a feel for the culture:

- Zeno once paid twice the asking price for seven versions of the "Reaper" argument (D.L. vii.25) (O'Toole and Jennings, p. 408).
- Callimachus, the Alexandrian librarian, wrote that even the crows on the roofs were cawing about which conditionals are sound (S.E. M i.309–10) (p. 407).
- **Cleanthes**, a former boxer, was too poor to buy paper and copied Zeno's lectures onto oyster shells and ox shoulder-blades (D.L. vii.174). He wrote *Of Moods* and *Of Predicates* and seems to have been the first to use the word *lekton* (pp. 410–11).
- **Chrysippus** told Cleanthes he needed only to be taught the doctrines, since he would find the proofs himself (D.L. vii.179). People said that "if dialectic was possessed by the gods, it would be none other than that of Chrysippus" (D.L. vii.180), and that "if there had been no Chrysippus, there would have been no Stoa" (D.L. vii.183) (pp. 412–14).

Barnes's introduction counts Chrysippus' logical titles. There were twelve works in 23 books on the Liar alone, and in all "more than a sixth of his logical oeuvre" was given over to puzzles. Another eighteen works dealt with forms of inference, centred on "the 'five indemonstrables' which was the centrepiece of Chrysippus' logical achievement" (p. 71).

### 3. Why a Stoic Needs Logic

The Stoics divided philosophy into logic, physics and ethics, and compared it to a field: logic is the wall, physics the soil and trees, ethics the fruit (D.L. vii.40). Epictetus explains why logic comes first: "in the measuring of grain we put first the examination of the measure" (*Discourses* i.17.7–8) (O'Toole and Jennings, pp. 414–15).

O'Toole and Jennings make this the basis of their whole interpretation. The Cynics had thrown out logic and physics (D.L. vi.103). Zeno brought them back because his goal of life, "to live harmoniously with nature", which Chrysippus glossed as living "in accordance with experience of what happens by nature", requires knowing how events follow one another. Fate (*heimarmenē*) is the same thing as the world's rational order, its *logos*, and the wise man must be able to reason along it (pp. 415–22). So, they argue, the basic Stoic relations of **consequence** (*akolouthia*) and **conflict** (*machē*) were in the first place relations between states of affairs in nature, and only secondarily between sentences (pp. 400, 425). They quote A. A. Long: the major role of logic in Stoicism is "to make possible predictions about the future by drawing out consequences from the present" (p. 425).

The Cambridge History keeps the logic separate from the physics in its exposition, but it repeatedly notes how the logic was embedded in the practice of dialectical debate: arguments by question and answer, premisses "adopted by agreement" (S.E. M viii.302) (pp. 122, 124–5).

### 4. Diodorus and Philo on "If"

The great controversy began between Diodorus and his pupil Philo. All sides agreed that a conditional "announces" that its consequent follows from its antecedent (S.E. M viii.110–12). They disagreed about when it is true (Cambridge History, p. 84).

1. **Philo**: a conditional is false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent false, and true in the other three cases (S.E. M viii.113–17). This "comes very close to that of modern material implication" (p. 84). The cost is that no connection is needed. By day, "If virtue benefits, it is day" is true. And because Stoic truth is tied to times, "If it is day, it is night" is true at night and false by day (pp. 84–5).
2. **Diodorus**: a conditional is true if "it neither was nor is possible that its antecedent is true and its consequent false" (S.E. M viii.115–17). This is Philo's test applied at every time, close to strict implication. It has its own paradoxes: "If the earth flies, Axiothea philosophizes" comes out true because the antecedent is impossible (D.L. vii.75) (pp. 85–6).

O'Toole and Jennings note that Sextus lays out the debate (PH ii.110–12) as a ladder from weakest to strongest view. At each step he gives an example the weaker view accepts and the stronger rejects. So some of the counter-examples may be Sextus' own constructions rather than the original combatants' (pp. 479–81).

### 5. Possibility and the Master Argument

Diodorus defined the modalities by time alone: "Possible is that which either is or will be <true>; impossible that which is false and will not be true; necessary that which is true and will not be false" (Boethius) (Cambridge History, p. 87). Philo instead defined possibility as what a proposition is capable of "by its own nature" (p. 86). On Diodorus' view anything that never happens was never possible. If Dio never goes to Corinth, then "Dio goes to Corinth" was impossible, which seemed to rule out freedom (p. 88).

To defend this view Diodorus produced the **Master Argument**, famous enough to be discussed at dinner parties (Plutarch, *Table Talk* 615a). Only its premisses survive, in Epictetus:

> There is a general conflict between the following three <statements>: (i) every past true <proposition> is necessary; and (ii) the impossible does not follow from the possible; and (iii) something is possible which neither is true nor will be true. Being aware of this conflict, Diodorus used the plausibility of the first two <statements> in order to show that (iv) nothing is possible that neither is nor will be true. (Epictetus, *Discourses* ii.19.1) (p. 89)

The Cambridge History reconstructs the argument using the example "I am in Corinth". It needs two extra premisses. One says that if something is now the case, it was always going to be the case. The other says that if something neither is nor will be true, it was at some earlier time the case that it would never be (pp. 90–2). The verdict is that the first of these "seem[s] to smuggle in a deterministic assumption" (p. 92). The Stoics answered by rejecting one of Diodorus' premisses each. Cleanthes denied that past truths are necessary. Chrysippus denied that the impossible cannot follow from the possible (p. 116; O'Toole and Jennings, p. 411).

Chrysippus' counter-example is ingenious. Take "If Dio is dead, *this one* is dead", said while pointing at Dio. "Dio is dead" is possible. But "This one is dead" can never become true, because when Dio dies there is nothing left to point at and the statement is "destroyed" (Alexander, *On Prior Analytics* 177–8) (Cambridge History, p. 116). So something impossible follows from something possible. Chrysippus then defined possibility as what is "capable of being true and ... not hindered by external things from being true" (D.L. vii.75). This is a middle way: a log at the bottom of the sea is possibly burning for Philo but not for Chrysippus, while a book Hipparchia never reads was still possible for Chrysippus but not for Diodorus (pp. 118–21).

### 6. The Assertible: What Is True or False

The Stoic bearer of truth is the **assertible** (*axiōma*), defined as "a self-complete sayable that can be stated as far as itself is concerned" (S.E. PH ii.104) and as "that which is either true or false" (D.L. vii.65) (Cambridge History, pp. 93–4). It is a kind of ***lekton***, a "sayable": an incorporeal content, neither a sound nor a thought. Sextus reports the Stoic three-part scheme. There is the sound "Dion", the thing signified, "which the Barbarians, although hearing the utterance, do not comprehend", and Dion himself. Two of these are bodies and one is incorporeal (S.E. M viii.11–12) (O'Toole and Jennings, pp. 436–7).

The two works stress different features:

- **The Cambridge History** stresses how assertibles differ from modern propositions. Their truth is temporal: "It is day" is true when it is day (D.L. vii.65), so it changes truth-value twice a day (pp. 95–6). Some can be **destroyed** (p. 100). A true assertible is "real", a false one is not (S.E. M viii.10) (p. 95). The book catalogues the kinds of simple assertible:
  - definite: "This one is walking", said while pointing;
  - middle: "Socrates is sitting";
  - indefinite: "Someone is sitting";
  - the negation, formed by putting "Not:" in front: "Not: it is day";
  - the denial: "No one is walking";
  - the privation: "Unkind is this one" (pp. 96–103).
- **O'Toole and Jennings** stress the link to perception. The *lekton* "subsist[s] coordinately with a rational presentation" (S.E. M viii.70; D.L. vii.63). Diocles, as quoted by Diogenes, says that "presentation comes first, then thought, being capable of speaking out, discloses by means of discourse that which is experienced through the presentation" (D.L. vii.49) (pp. 427–9). On their view what is said is a state of affairs (*pragma*), something like {Dion, walking}. The same *pragma* can be asserted ("It is day"), asked ("Is it day?") or commanded, much as Searle separates the content of a speech act from its force (pp. 443–8). They deny that a *lekton* is a "meaning" in Frege's sense, since meanings are not true or false (pp. 458–9). They also note that, against Mates and the Kneales, the Stoics did not regard such epistemology as "extra-logical" (pp. 427–9).

### 7. Connectives and the Prefixed "Not"

Non-simple assertibles are built with connectives, and Chrysippus used only three: the conditional "If", the conjunction "Both ... and", and the disjunction "Either ... or" (Cambridge History, p. 105). The Stoics standardised their language to remove ambiguity:

- The negation and every connective are put at the **front**: "Not: it is day", "Both p and either q or r". As the Cambridge History notes, this does the work of brackets and makes Stoic notation "a fore-runner of Polish notation" (p. 104 and n. 72).
- Pronouns are spelled out: "If Plato walks, Plato moves", not "...he moves" (p. 105).
- "Callias is not walking" is not the contradictory of "Callias is walking", because both would be false if Callias did not exist. Only "Not: Callias is walking" is (Alexander, *On Prior Analytics* 402) (p. 102).
- The conjunction is truth-functional and can have more than two parts (p. 106).
- Universals were recast as conditionals: "Every human being is a rational, mortal animal" becomes "If something is a human being, that thing is a rational, mortal animal" (S.E. M xi.8–9) (p. 113).

The **disjunction** is where the two readings clash most sharply. The usual view, held by Łukasiewicz, Mates, Bocheński and the Kneales, is that it is the exclusive "or" (O'Toole and Jennings, pp. 498–9). The Cambridge History quotes Gellius' three conditions: "(i) all the disjuncts must be in conflict with each other and (ii) their contradictories ... must be contrary to each other. (iii) Of all the disjuncts one must be true, the remaining ones false" (Gell. xvi.8.13). It reads this as "necessarily precisely one" (p. 110). O'Toole and Jennings make a neat technical point. With more than two disjuncts, the modern exclusive "or" is true when an *odd number* of disjuncts are true, but the Stoic one is true when *exactly one* is. The two agree only in the two-disjunct case. So the Stoic "or" has no fixed number of places and is not the modern connective at all (pp. 398–9). For them it is a relation of exclusive alternatives, "a late practical refinement of the notion of the conflict of opposites" that goes back through Heraclitus to Anaximander (pp. 517–18).

### 8. Four Answers to "When Is a Conditional True?"

By Chrysippus' time Sextus lists four positions, from weakest to strongest (PH ii.110–12):

| View | Test for "If p, q" | Problem case |
|---|---|---|
| Philo | not (p true and q false) | "If it is day, I converse" is true whenever I happen to be talking |
| Diodorus | at no time was or is p true and q false | true whenever q is necessary or p impossible |
| Connection (*synartēsis*) | the contradictory of q **conflicts** with p | excludes "If p, not-p" style conditionals |
| Inclusion (*emphasis*) | q is "potentially contained" in p | even "If it is day, it is day" fails, since nothing contains itself |

Both works attribute the connection view to Chrysippus (Cic. *On Fate* 12; D.L. vii.73) (Cambridge History, pp. 106–7; O'Toole and Jennings, p. 479). "If the earth flies, Axiothea philosophizes" fails it, because the earth's flying is perfectly compatible with Axiothea not philosophizing (Cambridge History, p. 107).

The two works then go different ways on what the connection was:

- **The Cambridge History** looks for its logical strength. It counts formal conflict and probably "analytic" conflict ("If Plato is walking, Plato is moving"), and some causal cases ("If someone has a wound in the heart, that one will die"). But Chrysippus refused to let the diviners' rules count as true conditionals, even though he accepted them as exceptionless. He told the diviners to phrase them as "Not: both p and not q" instead of "If p, q" (Cic. *On Fate* 11–15) (pp. 107–8).
- **O'Toole and Jennings** look for its origin in experience. They connect it with the theory of **signs**. A sign is the antecedent of a sound conditional that reveals its consequent (S.E. M viii.245), and the examples run from smoke and fire to a scar and a wound (pp. 468–9). Sextus reports that man differs from the animals not by uttered speech, since "crows and jays and parrots utter articulate sounds", but by inner reasoning and the "inferential" presentations through which "he immediately possesses the conception of 'following'" (S.E. M viii.275–7) (p. 469). The connection view links the Stoics to modern **connexive logic**: Storrs McCall's "Aristotle's Thesis", that no proposition implies or is implied by its own negation (pp. 481–2).

### 9. The Five Indemonstrables

An argument (*logos*) has premisses and a conclusion. The non-simple premiss, the "leading premiss", comes first, then the "co-assumption", introduced by "but" or "now", then "therefore" (Cambridge History, p. 121). An indemonstrable is one that is not in need of proof (D.L. vii.79). The five are (S.E. M viii.224–6; D.L. vii.80–1):

1. **If it is day, it is light. It is day. Therefore it is light.** (later *modus ponens*)
2. **If it is day, it is light. Not: it is light. Therefore not: it is day.** (*modus tollens*)
3. **Not: both Plato is dead and Plato is alive. Plato is dead. Therefore not: Plato is alive.**
4. **Either it is day or it is night. It is day. Therefore not: it is night.**
5. **Either it is day or it is night. Not: it is day. Therefore it is night.**

The Stoics also stated them in schemata with ordinal numbers in place of whole assertibles, the "modes": "If the first, the second; now the first; therefore the second" (D.L. vii.76). Aristotle put letters in place of terms (p. 130). Each description covers several sub-types, thirty-two in all (p. 129).

Why these five? The Cambridge History offers an attractive answer: "no one could reasonably doubt their validity, simply because understanding the connectives that are used in their leading premisses implies knowing the validity of the corresponding forms" (p. 133). That is why "p, q, therefore p and q" and the chain "If p, q; if q, r; therefore if p, r" are not indemonstrables (p. 133). O'Toole and Jennings note that some later writers credited them to Theophrastus, Aristotle's successor. Prantl and Zeller believed this, and Bocheński and others doubted it. Aristotle himself had promised a treatment of arguments "from a hypothesis" and never wrote it (Alexander, *On Prior Analytics* 390) (pp. 474–5). The Cambridge History agrees that even if Theophrastus discussed these forms, he "did not anticipate Chrysippus' achievement" (p. 83).

### 10. Reducing Everything Else: The Themata

All other syllogisms were to be "analysed" back into indemonstrables by four rules, the ***themata***. The first is a version of reductio:

> When from two <assertibles> a third follows, then from either of them together with the contradictory of the conclusion the contradictory of the other follows. (Apuleius, *On Interpretation* 209) (Cambridge History, p. 138)

The third thema lets one break a chain of reasoning into steps (Simplicius, *On the Heavens* 237). A "dialectical theorem" says that premisses that yield a conclusion contain it "potentially ... even if it is not expressly stated" (S.E. M viii.231) (pp. 140, 143–4). Sextus works through an example. The argument "If the first and the second, the third; but not the third; moreover the first; therefore not the second" turns out to be a second indemonstrable followed by a third (S.E. M viii.235–7) (pp. 140–1).

Working from the second and fourth themata, which are lost, the Cambridge History concludes: "This method of reduction is practicable and easy ... The method appears to be effective, the system decidable" (p. 148). The Stoics claimed some kind of completeness (S.E. PH ii.156–7, 194), but whether they achieved it "we cannot decide" (pp. 148–9). The system is also narrower than modern logic in deliberate ways. Every argument needs at least two premisses. Premisses may not be redundant. Wholly hypothetical chains are not syllogisms (pp. 149–51).

O'Toole and Jennings resist the idea that this is a formal system in the modern sense, where any formula may be substituted for a letter. The Stoics, they say, were "more interested in disjunctions than conjunctions, and more interested in conditionals, than in negations", because their focus was "primarily upon relationships, conflict and consequence" (pp. 497–8). They cite "Cicero's clanger", a sixth "indemonstrable" in the *Topica*: "Not both this and that; not this; therefore that". It is invalid on a formal reading. Bocheński joked that asking Cicero about logic is like asking Sartre about Carnap. O'Toole and Jennings argue that on a non-formal reading it is a less absurd slip (pp. 511–12).

### 11. Valid, True and the Four Ways to Fail

An argument is **valid** if the conditional made from its premisses and conclusion is sound. For example, "If it is day, it is light; it is day; therefore it is light" is valid because "If (it is day, and if it is day, it is light), it is light" is sound (S.E. PH ii.137) (Cambridge History, p. 123; O'Toole and Jennings, p. 491). Diogenes states it negatively. Invalid arguments are "those in which the contradictory of the conclusion does not conflict with the conjunction of the premisses" (D.L. vii.77) (O'Toole and Jennings, p. 492). An argument is **true**, which we would call sound, if it is valid and its premisses are true. Because premisses change truth-value, the same argument can be true by day and false at night, a "changing argument" (Cambridge History, pp. 126–7).

Sextus lists four ways an argument fails (M viii.429–34) (Cambridge History, pp. 123–5):

1. **Disconnection**: "If it is day, it is light. Now wheat is being sold in the market. Therefore it is light."
2. **Redundancy**: adding "And also virtue benefits" to a valid argument.
3. **Incorrect form**: "If it is day, it is light. Now it is light. Therefore it is day." (affirming the consequent)
4. **Omission**: "Either wealth is good or wealth is bad. But wealth is not bad. Therefore wealth is good", which leaves out "indifferent".

The Cambridge History admits that by modern standards the second and fourth "seem to be perfectly valid" and the list makes the Stoics "look rather bad logicians". It suggests reading the list as dialectical advice on how to mend an opponent's argument (pp. 124–5). O'Toole and Jennings use the fourth case differently, as evidence that a Stoic disjunction was only "sound" (*hygiēs*) when complete. Gellius rejects the dilemma "a father's command is either honourable or base" as not a "sound and proper" disjunction, because it leaves out "neither" (Gell. ii.7.6–21) (pp. 477–8).

### 12. The Liar

Stoic paradoxes were classed as false arguments with an obviously false conclusion, where it is not clear where the falsehood lies (D.L. vii.79; Galen) (Cambridge History, p. 157). Their list included:

- **the Veiled Man**: Callias knows that Coriscus is cultivated, but cannot say whether the veiled man in front of him is, and the veiled man is Coriscus (substitution in opaque contexts);
- **the Horned Man**: "If you have not lost something, you have it; you have not lost horns; you have horns" (D.L. vii.187);
- **the Not-someone**, possibly aimed at Plato's Forms;
- **the Crocodile**, a version of the Liar.

The Greek name for the unsolved ones, *aporon*, became Cicero's *inexplicabile* and the medieval *insolubile* (pp. 158–61).

The oldest surviving form of the **Liar** is in Cicero: "If you say you are speaking falsely and you tell the truth about it, you are speaking falsely *** you are telling the truth" (*Academica* ii.95). The text has a gap where the asterisks are, and the Cambridge History argues for one particular reconstruction (pp. 163–7). Plutarch objected that Chrysippus refused to call the Liar sentence false. Cicero's objection survives:

> If assertibles of this sort cannot be explained and for them there is no criterion according to which you can answer the question whether they are true or false, what happens with the definition of an assertible as that which is true or false? (Cic. *Academica* ii.95) (p. 168)

The Cambridge History concludes that Chrysippus held the Liar sentence to be an assertible that is neither true nor false. In medieval terms he was neither a *cassans*, who said the sentence is meaningless, nor a *restringens*, who said it is simply false (pp. 167–9).

### 13. The Heap

The **Sorites** asks whether one grain is a heap (no), whether two grains are (no), and so on, so that no number of grains is ever a heap. Galen asks, "I know of nothing worse and more absurd than that the being and not-being of a heap is determined by a grain of corn" (*On Medical Experience* xvii.3) (Cambridge History, pp. 170, 173). Diogenes gives a Stoic one-argument form: "It is not the case that two are few and three are not also; ... But two are few: therefore ten thousand are also" (D.L. vii.82) (p. 172).

The Academic sceptics used it against the Stoic theory of knowledge, since there is no last clear impression before an unclear one (S.E. M vii.415–21). The Stoics defended themselves partly by making wisdom an all-or-nothing state. The sage becomes wise in a single instant, the man who was "the worst in the morning" and "the best in the evening" in Plutarch's mockery (Plu. *Progress in Virtue* 75d–e) (p. 163). Chrysippus' advice was for the wise man to stop answering before the unclear cases, "like a clever charioteer, [he] will pull up [his] horses, before [he] gets to the end" (Cic. *Academica* ii.94) (p. 174). The Cambridge History weighs two readings. On the first, there is a sharp line that nobody knows. On the second, truth comes in degrees and *modus ponens* is safe only over short chains, which would make Chrysippus "much more modern than the view represented in the Aristotelian tradition". It ends: "It is not without sacrifices that we resolve paradoxes" (pp. 174–6).

### 14. Two Pictures of Stoic Logic

Put side by side, the two works give complementary pictures of one logic:

- **The Cambridge History's picture** is a propositional logic of arguments, "not a system of logical theorems or tautologies" (p. 114). It has standardised notation, truth-functional negation and conjunction, five evident basic forms, and an effective, perhaps decidable, procedure of analysis. Its main weakness is that "conflict" is "too vague to serve as a proper criterion for validity" (p. 123). Chrysippus' work on the paradoxes is the clearest sign of his depth.
- **O'Toole and Jennings' picture** is a theory of inference, "rather than of inferability" (p. 401). It concerns how a rational mind moves from presented states of affairs to others connected with them by consequence and conflict. Its connectives are relations between alternatives and between causes and signs, not truth functions with fixed arity, and its logic cannot be pulled apart from Stoic physics and ethics. They end by sketching a model in which Stoic disjunction is a "hyper-relation" among exclusive alternatives, and they suggest that "inference should preserve dilution" of conflict is "in the logical spirit of Stoicism" (pp. 518–20). Imbert, whom they quote, puts the point sharply: Stoic inference "never presuppose[s] the existence of transcendent forms or universals" and "depends on a rhetorical function which maps utterances (lekta) on to contents of presentations" (p. 517).

The two pictures overlap more than their rhetoric suggests. The Cambridge History concedes the dialectical setting, the temporality of truth, and the non-formal "specifically valid" arguments. These include Posidonius' arguments valid "on the basis of the power of an axiom" (Galen) and Antipater's single-premiss "You see. Therefore you are alive" (pp. 152–5). O'Toole and Jennings concede that the indemonstrables look like natural-deduction rules and that the earlier formal reading was a necessary rescue (pp. 400, 497).

### Conclusion

Stoic logic was the first serious logic of whole propositions. Diodorus and Philo argued about what "if" means and arrived at positions close to strict and material implication. Chrysippus added the requirement of a real connection, and with five evident argument forms and four rules of analysis he built a method for testing arguments that later centuries forgot. The Cambridge History shows how close this comes to a modern propositional calculus. O'Toole and Jennings show what that comparison leaves out: a logic of consequence and conflict between states of affairs, rooted in a world governed by fate and a mind that learns its order through presentations. Read together, they explain why Chrysippus wrote more on the Liar and the Heap than on anything else. For a Stoic, logic was a means of living according to nature, and the paradoxes threatened that.
