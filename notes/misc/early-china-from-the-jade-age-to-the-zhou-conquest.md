The traditional story says China began with sage kings and then passed through three dynasties, the **Xia**, **Shang** and **Zhou**, one after the other. Archaeology gives a messier and more interesting picture: many regional cultures, a **Jade Age** before the Bronze Age, real cities by 3000 BCE, a Shang state that we know from its own **oracle bones**, and a Zhou conquest that was justified by a new idea, the **Mandate of Heaven**. Here's how it works:

### 1. Myth Versus Archaeology

The old histories begin with culture heroes. **Fu Xi** taught people to hunt and fish, **Shen Nong** taught farming, and the **Yellow Emperor** (Huang Di) fought off enemies. Then came the sage kings **Yao** and **Shun**, who passed the throne to the worthiest man instead of to their sons, and **Yu**, who tamed the great flood and founded the Xia (Ebrey ch. 1).

Modern scholars are cautious about all of this. Hong Xu, who has excavated Erlitou for years, points out that the Xia is not mentioned in any text until about a thousand years after it is supposed to have existed. The earliest version of the flood story, on a Western Zhou bronze (the Sui gong xu), says "Heaven commanded Yu to spread out the land", so Yu appears there as a divine being, not as the first king of a dynasty (Oxford Handbook ch. 7). Hong Xu and Liu Li sum up the problem this way: "Archaeology and legendary history are different disciplines."

The neat order Xia → Shang → Zhou is itself suspect. Smith and Fan say the linear succession is "frequently seen as a historiographical construct beginning from the Zhou and later notion of strictly successive, heaven-conferred legitimacy (tianming)" (ch. 11). Ebrey makes the same point: there were several centres of civilization at once, and the "simple story of one dynasty succeeding to another is a later projection back" (ch. 1).

- **Significance:** The Mandate of Heaven (section 10) did not only explain the Zhou conquest. It also shaped how the Zhou and everyone after them told the whole earlier history, as one line of legitimate rulers.

### 2. The Neolithic Revolution Was Slow

Farming in China began in two places with two crops (Oxford Handbook ch. 1–2):

1. **Millet in the north.** The earliest broomcorn millet comes from Cishan (8300–6700 BCE), whose storage pits could have held about fifty metric tons of grain if they were full. Later cultures include Peiligang, Dadiwan and Xinglongwa.
2. **Rice in the south.** Rice appears at Shangshan and Pengtoushan (8000–6500 BCE). Tianluoshan has the earliest paddy fields in China, and Hemudu in Zhejiang had long wooden houses and 154 plough blades made from deer shoulder blades.

Womack stresses that this "revolution" took thousands of years. Early burials are fairly equal. Social rank only shows up clearly in the Middle Neolithic (about 4000–3000 BCE):
- At **Xipo** (Miaodigou culture) there are buildings up to 240 m² and large graves with jade axes, signs of an early elite that held feasts.
- At **Banpo** (Yangshao culture, near Xi'an) the pottery is painted with fish and nets, and some sherds have incised signs that may be early writing.
- In the **Longshan** period (about 2500–2000 BCE) the number of settlements on the Central Plain was about two and a half times what it had been under Yangshao. Towns had rammed-earth walls. The Taosi cemetery had over a thousand graves: more than six hundred small ones, about eighty medium ones, and six large ones with painted coffins, alligator-skin drums and jades. This is **extreme inequality**.

### 3. The Jade Age

Childs-Johnson argues that China had no real Copper Age. Instead, from about 3500 to 2000 BCE it had a **Jade Age**. Jade was believed to hold spiritual power, and it did the job that bronze would later do: it marked who held power and who could talk to the spirits (ch. 4).

- **The earliest jade.** Slit jade rings from Xinglongwa (6200–5200 BCE) are the earliest earrings known anywhere, two thousand years before the jewellery of Ur (ch. 3).
- **Hongshan** (4500–3000 BCE, in the northeast) made "pig-dragons", cloud scrolls and arm guards, all ornaments and no weapons. At **Niuheliang** there is a "Goddess Temple" with clay statues, all of them female, some three times life size, set in a ritual landscape of stone cairns about fifty square kilometres across (ch. 1).
- **Liangzhu** (3300–2300 BCE, near modern Hangzhou) is the high point. About ninety per cent of elite grave goods are jade. The two key shapes are the **cong**, a round tube inside a square, and the **bi**, a flat disc. Many scholars read them as the round heaven and the square earth. One tomb at Sidun held a twenty-year-old man with thirty-two cong. At Fanshan, the so-called "royal cong" shows a human figure riding a beast with whirling eyes.

Lin Yun suggested that the oracle-bone character for **king** (*wang*, 王) comes from the shape of a ceremonial axe blade, the jade *yue* that Liangzhu rulers were buried with (ch. 5). If that is right, the idea of kingship in Chinese writing goes back to Jade Age symbols of power.

### 4. The First Cities: Liangzhu and Taosi

Two sites show that China had real cities and large public works long before the Shang.

1. **Liangzhu city** (Bin Liu, ch. 5): A palace platform 630 by 450 metres stood inside a walled city of about three million square metres, with a wall about six kilometres long and eight water gates. The city had fifty-one rivers and canals, most of them man-made. To the northwest, eleven dams held back a reservoir of about thirteen square kilometres. Liu calls it the oldest large water-control project in China, older than the legends of Yu the Great. One burnt granary pit held about 26,000 kg of carbonized rice, probably tribute brought in from the countryside. Liangzhu was a contemporary of early Egypt, Sumer and Harappa.
2. **Taosi** (He Nu, ch. 6) in southern Shanxi had a middle-period city of at least 2.8 million square metres, with palaces, royal cemeteries and an altar to heaven. Its most famous feature is a semicircular rammed-earth structure with twelve slots aimed at a mountain peak seven kilometres east. When it was tested in 2003–5, the winter solstice sunrise appeared through one slot, the summer solstice through another and the equinoxes through a third. A royal tomb held a lacquered gnomon for measuring the sun's shadow. He Nu argues that Taosi was keeping a solar calendar for its rites and planting, and that its rulers claimed to sit at the "centre" where earth meets heaven.

- **Significance:** He Nu's further claims about Taosi are very speculative and I am leaving them out. But the basic point holds: a ruler who controlled the calendar controlled the link between heaven and the farming year. That idea comes back in the Zhou and imperial claim that the ruler sets the calendar for All-under-Heaven.

### 5. Erlitou and the Xia Question

When Wang Guowei found the Shang king list of the *Shiji* confirmed by the oracle bones, many scholars expected that the Xia would be proven too. In 1959 Xu Xusheng found **Erlitou** in Henan, and in 1977 Zou Heng declared that "Erlitou culture is the Xia culture" (ch. 7).

What Erlitou really shows (Xu and Li, ch. 8–9):
- It covered over three million square metres, had a palace enclosure with the earliest grid of roads in China, and was laid out on a **north–south axis**. Strangely, it had **no city wall**. Its palaces were also ancestral temples.
- It had the earliest large **bronze workshop** in China, active for about two hundred years. Its seventeen bronze vessels are mostly *jue*, vessels for warming alcohol. Chinese casters carved the decoration into the moulds *before* casting, unlike Mesopotamia and Egypt.
- The burials are ranked, but even the largest is only about six square metres, and no royal tomb has been found.
- Its jade *yazhang* blades spread south as far as Hong Kong and northern Vietnam, so its political style influenced a very large area (ch. 10).

Erlitou can now be dated no earlier than 1750 BCE. Hong Xu calls it "Earliest China", the first state, and sees the walled town of Xinzhai, which ended the wars among a dozen short-lived Longshan towns, as "the beginning of that dawn". But because no writing has been found from before the Shang at Anyang, we cannot prove or disprove that Erlitou was the Xia of the texts.

### 6. The Shang: Writing, Divination and Sacrifice

The Shang is the first dynasty that speaks for itself. About **130,000 inscribed bones and shells** survive (Smith and Fan, ch. 11). In 1899 Wang Yirong noticed writing on "dragon bones" sold in Beijing pharmacies, and they were traced to Xiaotun near Anyang, the last Shang capital.

**How divination worked.** Diviners bored hollows in turtle plastrons or ox shoulder blades, pressed a hot rod against them and read the cracks. The inscription usually had four parts: the preface (date and diviner), the charge (the question), the king's prognostication, and sometimes a verification of what happened. In one example under King Wu Ding, the king predicted that misfortune would come, perhaps from outside, and the record adds that the Gong Fang then raided from the west and carried off seventy-five people (ch. 11). Ebrey gives a similar case in which the Tufang seized two settlements on the eastern border (ch. 1).

**Writing.** About four thousand characters are known, of which roughly a thousand have been deciphered. The script is already mature, and it is the direct ancestor of modern Chinese characters. Ebrey points out a long-term effect: because the script is **logographic**, the educated could read texts across centuries and across dialects, which tied the elite together (ch. 1).

**Sacrifice.** The scale is shocking. One inscription records an offering of "100 cups of wine, 300 sheep, cattle, and pigs, along with 100 Qiang prisoners" (Ebrey ch. 1). The Qiang were captives taken in war.
- **Tomb 1001** at Anyang, perhaps Wu Ding's, had 90 followers who went with the king in death and 74 human sacrifices. The royal cemetery has over 2,500 sacrificial pits (Ebrey ch. 1).
- **Lady Hao** (Fu Zi or Fu Hao), one of Wu Ding's wives, has the only Shang royal tomb found unrobbed. It held 16 human skeletons, 460 bronzes, about 750 jades and about 6,900 cowrie shells. The oracle bones say she led **13,000 troops** against the Qiang and presided over rites. Two large bronze axes in the tomb were symbols of military power (Ebrey ch. 1; Cao, ch. 16).

### 7. Shang Religion: Di, the Ancestors and Metamorphosis

The Shang high god was **Di** (or Shangdi, "Di on High"). Di sent rain, wind and disaster, but the Shang hardly sacrificed to Di directly. They fed their **ancestors**, who could intercede.

- **Reciprocity.** Ebrey describes sacrifice as feeding the dead in exchange for their help (ch. 1).
- **The king's monopoly.** Only the king could "host" (*bin*) the spirits. Keightley, quoted by Smith and Fan, says it is "hard to separate the activities and interests of the state from those of the king and the royal lineage" (ch. 11).
- **Metamorphosis.** Childs-Johnson argues that dead Shang kings did not just intercede but became divine and joined Di. She reads the famous animal mask on Shang bronzes, usually called the *taotie*, as a "metamorphic power mask" showing the change between human and animal-spirit. The name *taotie* itself is a much later label (ch. 13).
- **The sixty-day cycle.** Ten Heavenly Stems combined with twelve Earthly Branches gave a sixty-day cycle, and dead kings were named after the day on which they received sacrifice (ch. 11).

Ebrey notes that the Shang saw their capital as the **centre**, with the "four quarters" around it (ch. 1). The word *zhongguo*, "central state", which later means China, first appears on a Western Zhou bronze, the He zun (Childs-Johnson, ch. 13).

### 8. Many Centres, Not One

The Shang did not rule all of what is now China. Other cultures were flourishing at the same time:
- **Sanxingdui** in Sichuan, found in 1986, had two pits full of bronze heads, gold masks, a life-sized bronze statue and thirteen elephant tusks, in a style unlike anything at Anyang. It was abandoned around 1000 BCE (Ebrey ch. 1).
- **Xin'gan** in Jiangxi had large bronze bells.

Ebrey also stresses what came from outside. Metallurgy, sheep, wheat, the horse, the chariot (around 1200 BCE) and the compound bow all came across Central Asia from west Asia (ch. 1).

### 9. The Zhou Conquest (1046 BCE)

The Zhou came from the Wei River valley in the west. Their legend traced them back to Hou Ji, "Lord Millet". Khayutina notes that before the conquest the Zhou were part of the Shang world: Zhou oracle bones even record sacrifices to Shang ancestors, and Zhou rulers married Shang women (ch. 17).

**The date.** Scholars have proposed twenty-two different dates between 1122 and 1025 BCE. The state-sponsored Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project settled on **1046 BCE**, partly from astronomy, and most books now use it.

**The events.** King Wu defeated the last Shang king at **Muye** on a *jiazi* day; a bronze found in 1976, the Li gui, confirms the day. Ebrey says the traditional story painted the Shang king as "licentious and dissolute", provoking the anger of Heaven. Wu left a Shang prince to carry on the Shang sacrifices but died soon after. Wu's brothers then rebelled with the Shang prince, and Wu's other brother, the **Duke of Zhou**, crushed them, built a second capital at Luoyang, moved the Shang nobles there, and ruled as regent for Wu's young son King Cheng before handing power back (Ebrey ch. 1; Khayutina ch. 17).

The Duke of Zhou became the model minister for later Confucians. Ebrey quotes his prayer when King Wu fell ill: "Take me as a substitute for the king. I was kind and obedient to my father. I have many talents and skills and can serve the ghosts and spirits" (ch. 1).

### 10. The Mandate of Heaven

To justify overthrowing the Shang, the Zhou developed the idea that **Heaven** (*tian*) gives the right to rule to a virtuous house and takes it away when the house becomes wicked. Ebrey explains it this way: "a king and a dynasty could rule only so long as they retained Heaven's favour", and if they did not, Heaven sent "ominous portents and natural disasters" and withdrew its mandate. So "Moral values were thus built into the way the cosmos worked, and history could be read as a mirror of Heaven's will" (ch. 1).

The earliest evidence is on bronzes. The Da Yu ding says: "Illustrious King Wen received Heaven's blessings [and] the great Mandate. When King Wu succeeded King Wen, he created the bang, eliminated his foes, broadly possessed the Four Quarters, [and] greatly governed the people" (Khayutina, ch. 17).

Four points to notice:
1. **It was new.** The idea was not part of Shang religion. Ebrey suggests it may have begun as propaganda to win over Shang subjects (ch. 1).
2. **The name came later.** In Western Zhou inscriptions it is simply the "Great Mandate" (*da ming*). The fixed term *tianming* comes later (Vogt, ch. 18).
3. **It cut both ways.** If Heaven could take the mandate from the Shang, it could take it from the Zhou too. Later Confucians such as Mencius used it to warn rulers (see [[Eastern Zhou: The Springs and Autumns, the Warring States and the Hundred Schools]]).
4. **It lasted.** Ebrey calls it a central idea of Chinese political thought until modern times.

The Zhou king also took the title **Son of Heaven** (*tianzi*), and only he could sacrifice to Heaven. An Eastern Zhou poem, "Bei shan", states the ideal: "Under the vast Heaven there is no land that is not the king's" (Khayutina, ch. 17). But Khayutina notes that in practice the kings claimed to protect the "ten thousand *bang*", not to own all the land.

### 11. How the Western Zhou Was Governed

Older books call the Western Zhou "feudal". The Handbook authors reject this (Khayutina ch. 17; Vogt ch. 18):
- The king gave relatives and allies the right to rule regional states as a "screen" for the Zhou. Xunzi later said that of seventy-one principalities, fifty-three were held by members of the royal Ji clan. By 800 BCE there were about two hundred lords (Ebrey ch. 1).
- These grants were **appointments** made in a ceremony and recorded on bamboo, and they were tied together by **kinship and marriage**, not by a contract of vassalage as in medieval Europe. Li Feng calls the Zhou a "delegatory kin-ordered settlement state".
- Lineages followed the **zongfa** system: the eldest son of the main wife carried on the main line, and branch lineages split off.
- A real **bureaucracy** was already forming at court, with scribes, a Ministerial Bureau, and officials for land, works and horses.

The **appointment ceremony** is recorded on many bronzes. The king faced south, the person being appointed was led in, an archivist read the command from bamboo, and the recipient bowed his head to the ground and then cast a bronze vessel to record the honour "for sons of sons and grandsons of grandsons" (Cook, ch. 19).

**De.** Cook explains *de*, later translated "virtue", as at first an intangible power given by Heaven. Heaven gave it to the first Zhou king for punishing the wicked last Shang king, and each later king and official had to earn it again. Only by the Warring States did it become an inner moral quality that a person cultivates (ch. 19).

### 12. Rites, Bronzes and Songs

**From alcohol to food.** Shang tomb bronzes are mostly drinking vessels. The Zhou blamed drunkenness for the fall of the Shang and restricted alcohol, and over time their bronze sets shifted to food vessels (Sun, ch. 20). Around the ninth century there was a "ritual reform": sets of identical *ding* and *gui* vessels marked a person's rank. Texts later claimed a fixed rule (the king nine *ding*, lords seven, ministers five), but tombs show it was not always followed.

**Human sacrifice declined** under the Zhou, though it did not disappear. Divination by the **Yijing** (Book of Changes) gradually replaced oracle bones, and the animal mask vanished from bronzes (Ebrey ch. 1).

**The Book of Songs** has 305 poems from the Zhou. They give voices the bronzes don't:
- A political poem blames women for the troubles of the realm: "Clever men build cities, / Clever women topple them … Disorder is not sent down from heaven; / It is born of women."
- A love poem has a girl warning her lover: "Please, Zhongzi, / Do not leap over our wall".
- In a drought poem the speaker rebukes the ancestors: "Father and mother and the ancestors, / How can you bear to see us like this?" (all quoted in Ebrey ch. 1).

### 13. The Fall of the Western Zhou (771 BCE)

The Western Zhou had a troubled end. King Li was driven out in 842, and the first securely dated year in Chinese history, 841 BCE, falls during the regency that followed. Then **King You** set aside his queen from the state of Shen and her son, the heir, in favour of a concubine, Bao Si, and her son. The queen's father allied with the Quan Rong people, and in 771 they killed King You. The court moved east to Luoyang, beginning the **Eastern Zhou** (Khayutina, ch. 17).

Khayutina makes two points. First, the crisis was caused by breaking the marriage rules that held the kin network together. Second, the division into "Western" and "Eastern" Zhou is modern: people before the Qin saw about eight hundred years of one dynasty, whose kings kept their ritual authority for five more centuries after they lost real power.

### Conclusion

Early China was not one civilization moving in a straight line from Yao to the Zhou. It was a set of regional cultures, some of them, like Liangzhu and Taosi, already building cities, dams and observatories in the third millennium BCE. Jade came before bronze as the sign of power and of contact with the spirits. The Shang, whom we know from their own oracle bones, were a theocracy of king, ancestors and mass sacrifice. The Zhou conquest of 1046 BCE brought the most lasting idea of all, the Mandate of Heaven, which made a ruler's right to rule depend on his virtue and turned history into a record of Heaven's judgments. That idea, and the kin-based order of the Zhou lords, set the stage for the long breakdown of the Eastern Zhou.
