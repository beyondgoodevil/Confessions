> "Did not he that made me in the womb make him? and did not one fashion us in the womb?" (Job 31:15)

> "But so shall it not be among you: but whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister: And whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all. For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many." (Mark 10:43–45)

> "Nay, much more those members of the body, which seem to be more feeble, are necessary." (1 Corinthians 12:22)

In late 1887 the Danish critic Georg Brandes, who lectured on N. in Copenhagen the next spring, described his philosophy in a letter as **"aristocratic radicalism"**. N. wrote back (2 Dec 1887) that it was the most intelligent word he had read about himself so far. The label fits: it is **aristocratic** because culture exists for the sake of a few great human beings, and everyone else is the base of the pyramid. It is **radical** because it isn't conservative. The existing order is itself plebeian and commercial, so it has to be torn up, not defended. The question in the literature is how much this belongs to N.'s *philosophy* and how much is just nineteenth-century prejudice you can peel off.

N. = Nietzsche, GS = *Greek State* (the essay, not *Gay Science*, which I write out), SE = "Schopenhauer as Educator", BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, TI = *Twilight of the Idols*, A = *The Antichrist*, Z = *Zarathustra*.

## 1. The Setting: 1871

- **The Paris Commune.** In May 1871 news came that the Communards had burned the Louvre. N. wrote to Gersdorff (21 June 1871) that he was "completely destroyed" for days, in tears and doubts: all scholarly and aesthetic existence seemed absurd if a single day could wipe out the noblest works of art. The report turned out to be false, but a later fragment still links the autumn feeling to "when the Louvre was supposed to have been burnt", the "autumn-time of culture" (Losurdo ch. 1).
- **Losurdo's frame.** Read *The Birth of Tragedy* with this in mind. Its attack on Socratic optimism, the faith that knowledge can banish the tragic from life, is an attack on the **servile revolt**, on the modern belief that suffering can be abolished by changing society. He also points out that the language of "slaves" was live: Tocqueville had warned of "servile wars", and Marx called the Commune the war of the enslaved against their enslavers.
- **"The Greek State"** was written in 1871 out of material cut from the *Birth of Tragedy*. At Christmas 1872 N. gave it to Cosima Wagner as one of the *Five Prefaces to Five Unwritten Books*. It was never meant for publication, which is partly why it is so blunt.

## 2. "The Greek State" (1871–72)

The argument, step by step:

1. **"Dignity of man" and "dignity of labour" are lies.** We moderns pride ourselves on these two ideas, which are a "compensation" for a world that behaves slavishly while avoiding the word "slave". They are "the needy products of slavedom hiding itself from itself". The Greeks didn't need these "conceptual hallucinations". They said frankly that **labour is a disgrace**, because existence has no value in itself.
2. **The "cursed seducers".** The "equal rights of all" and "fundamental rights of man" are transparent lies. Those who taught them destroyed the slave's state of innocence "by the fruit of the tree of knowledge". (Note the image: the Fall is the slave learning that he's a slave.)
3. **Slavery belongs to the essence of culture.** For art to have a broad, deep soil, "the enormous majority must, in the service of a minority be slavishly subjected to life's struggle". So "we must accept this cruel sounding truth that **slavery is of the essence of Culture**". This truth is "the vulture that gnaws at the liver of the Promethean promoter of Culture". "The misery of toiling men must still increase in order to make the production of the world of art possible to a small number of **Olympian men**."
4. **Who resents this.** Hence "that secret wrath nourished by Communists and Socialists of all times", and their "feebler descendants", the Liberals, against art and against antiquity. If culture really rested on the will of the people, compassion would tear its walls down.
5. **Christianity**, oddly, gets half a compliment: an excess of compassion once opened the flood gates of culture, and "a rainbow of compassionate love and of peace" appeared with the first rise of Christianity, under which was born "Christianity's most beautiful fruit, the gospel according to St. John".
6. **"We shall perish through the lack of slavery."** If the Greeks perished through slavery, it's more certain "that we shall perish through the lack of slavery". Neither early Christianity nor the Germans found slavery objectionable. The medieval bondman, fenced into his narrow existence, is "how uplifting!"
7. **The state** is the "iron clamp" that forces the masses together so that society can form its pyramid. Its origin is conquest: "to the victor belongs the vanquished, with wife and child, life and property". Its excuse before "the tribunal of eternal justice" is that it leads Greek society by the hand like Helen, for whom the wars were fought.
8. **The danger: the "money-hermits".** Those who by "birth" stand outside the national and state instincts use the state as a means, want large balanced states so war becomes impossible, and spread "liberal optimism" from the French Revolution. N. sees behind it all "those truly international homeless money-hermits". Losurdo reads this as a coded attack on the Jews, written for Cosima, at a time when N. was still self-censoring in print (ch. 3).
9. **So: war.** "The only remedy is war and once again war." "War is just as much a necessity for the State as the slave is for society." War is Apollo, "the true divinity for consecrating and purifying the State".
10. **The conclusion about dignity.** "Every human being, with his total activity, only has dignity in so far as he is a **tool of the genius**, consciously or unconsciously." So "man in himself", the absolute man, "possesses neither dignity, nor rights, nor duties". Only as a fully determined being serving unconscious purposes "can man excuse his existence".
11. **Plato** saw this. The real aim of the state is "the Olympian existence and ever-renewed procreation and preparation of the genius". His only mistake was putting the philosopher instead of the artist at the top.

**Huddleston's reading of the essay.** There's a tension inside it. N. is still Schopenhauerian here: the slaves' suffering is a **black mark against existence** (the vulture at Prometheus' liver). So it's a dilemma: no culture and no defence against suffering, or culture and slavery. But the last section already has the idea he'll develop later: that being a tool of genius is where most people's **dignity** comes from.

## 3. The Later Texts

**"Schopenhauer as Educator" (1874)**
- "How can your life, the individual life, receive the highest value, the deepest significance? How can it be least squandered? Certainly only by your living for the good of the **rarest and most valuable exemplars**, and not for the good of the majority" (SE 6).
- The young person should regard himself as "a failed work of nature", but as a witness to nature's great intentions, and resolve to serve them so that nature may do better next time.
- Whoever attaches his heart to some great man receives "the first **consecration to culture**". Its sign is being ashamed of yourself without distress.
- **Rawls** quotes this passage in *A Theory of Justice* (§50) as the example of strict **perfectionism**, the view that society should maximise the achievement of excellence in a few. Huddleston thinks Rawls reads it too instrumentally (see §5).

**The *Zarathustra* period**
- "On the New Idol" (Z I): "State is the name of the coldest of all cold monsters." "Only where the state ends, there begins the human being who is not superfluous." So N. is no statist after the early essays. This is the evidence for the "anti-political" reading.
- "On the Way of the Creator" (Z I): "There are some who threw away their last value when they threw away their servitude." Huddleston takes this as his chapter epigraph.

**GS 377 (*Gay Science*, 1887)**
- "We who are homeless" are delighted by those who love danger, war and adventure, and "we think about the necessity for new orders, also for a **new slavery**, for every strengthening and enhancement of the human type also involves a new kind of enslavement."

**BGE 257–259 (1886), "What Is Noble?"**
- 257: "Every enhancement so far in the type 'human being' was the work of an **aristocratic society**", one that believes in "a long ladder of rank order and value-difference between one person and another and **in some sense requires slavery**" (Del Caro). Without the **pathos of distance** that grows from the difference between classes, the inner pathos, the craving for ever greater distances *within the soul*, couldn't grow either.
  - And the origin is not pretty: "the truth is harsh". Every higher culture began with "barbarians in every terrible sense of the word", predatory men falling on weaker, more peaceful peoples.
- 258: The French nobility that threw away its privileges in 1789 was **corrupt**. A healthy aristocracy doesn't feel itself to be a *function* of the monarchy or the commonwealth but its meaning and justification. So it "accepts in good conscience the sacrifice of countless people who for its sake have to be oppressed and reduced to incomplete human beings, to slaves and instruments". Society exists "only as the substructure and framework on which a choice kind of being is able to climb up to its highest task", like the Javanese climbing vine (*sipo matador*) that wraps itself round an oak until it can spread its crown in the light above it.
- 259: Refraining from injury, violence and exploitation can be good manners between equals, but as the *basic principle of society* it is "the will to denial of life". "Life simply is will to power." Even within a healthy aristocracy, where individuals treat each other as equals, the body as a whole must do to *other* bodies everything its members refrain from doing to each other.
- BGE 44: the so-called "free spirits" of Europe and America are "levelers", "slaves of the democratic taste", who want "the universal, green pasture happiness of the herd" and think suffering is something to be abolished.

**TI (1888)**
- "Skirmishes" 40, **the labour question**: "The stupidity... is that there is a labor question at all." The worker was given military service, the right to organise and the vote, and now feels his existence as an injustice. "If one wants an end, one must also want the means: if one wants slaves, then one is a fool if one educates them to be masters."
- "Skirmishes" 48: "The doctrine of equality! There is no more poisonous poison anywhere." The true slogan of justice is "Equal to the equal, unequal to the unequal", and "Never make equal what is unequal."

**A 57 (1888)**
- "The inequality of rights is the first condition for the existence of any rights at all." "**A high culture is a pyramid**: it can stand only on a broad base; its first presupposition is a strong and soundly consolidated mediocrity." Trade, agriculture, science, most of art: all suit a mediocre amount of ability. "For the mediocre, to be mediocre is their happiness."
- And a duty for the elite: "When the exceptional human being treats the mediocre more tenderly than himself and his peers, this is not mere politeness of the heart, it is simply his duty."
- The same section praises the **Law of Manu** (the Hindu caste code) against the New Testament, and attacks "the socialist rabble, the chandala apostles". (Leiter cites Brobjer: N.'s other remarks on Manu are critical, and the praise here is a stick to beat Christianity with.)

## 4. Losurdo: Political Through and Through

Domenico Losurdo, *Nietzsche, the Aristocratic Rebel* (Italian 2002; English, Brill 2020, over 1,000 pages).
- **Thesis.** The unity of N.'s work lies in his "constant eye on social conflict and the threat of socialism". Politics is not a side issue: his writing on art, truth, morality and science *is* political writing. The Paris Commune is the starting point.
- **"Hermeneutics of innocence."** His name for the academic habit of reading N. as an apolitical thinker of perspectives, masks and self-creation, a "toolbox" from which you take what you like. It hides what N. was defending.
- **"Aristocratic radicalism" and the "new party of life"** (ch. 11). Late N. wanted to found a "party of life". It breaks with conservatism and religion but has to wrest the flag of freedom, nonconformism and even "revolution" from the socialists ("we cannot help being revolutionaries", EH). The "free spirits" of BGE 44 are defined *against* the freethinkers and socialists.
- **Not proto-Nazi.** Losurdo insists N. belongs to the Second Reich, not the Third. Read him alongside Bismarck, the Kulturkampf and the anti-socialist laws, not with 1933 as a future anterior.
- Harrison Fluss's introduction connects this with Losurdo's other work on liberalism, which puts antebellum Southern defenders of slavery inside the liberal tradition. Fluss suggests N. belongs there too: a liberalism of the elite, compatible with inequality and hostile to democracy.

## 5. Huddleston: Consecration to Culture

Andrew Huddleston, *Nietzsche on the Decadence and Flourishing of Culture*, ch. 6.
- **Not just useful to the elite.** The usual reading (Rawls) is instrumental: slavery is useful for culture, though bad for the slaves. Huddleston thinks N. claims something stranger, that this "slavery in some sense" is in the **best interest of the slaves themselves**. The best life open to most people is service to culture: hauling stones for Chartres, sweeping Beethoven's floor. That is better than a comfortable "free" life spent on idle pleasures.
- **Slavery "in some sense".** In BGE 257 it means a **functional role** at the base of the pyramid, not legal chattel slavery. A 57 includes doctors, tradesmen and scientists.
- **Two concepts of dignity.** Christian and Kantian dignity is **innate, equal, supernatural**, and forbids treating anyone as a mere means. N.'s is **achieved, unequal and natural**, and for most people it is gained *precisely by being used as a means*. Huddleston lists eight features of each, and the two agree only that dignity matters more than pleasure and is the ground of respect.
- **The change.** In 1871 N. still laments the slaves' suffering (Schopenhauerian pity). By BGE he celebrates suffering and asks for a "good conscience" about the sacrifice of the many.
- **False consciousness.** Like Marx, N. thinks the masses misunderstand their own interest. But for N. the opium is the Christian belief that each person is an atom of infinite worth (A 43: "the 'salvation of the soul', in plain language: 'the world revolves around me'").
- Huddleston also thinks the view is not strictly **political**. N. never says how a modern state should keep this "slavery" in place, so it is better called a social or cultural ideal.

## 6. Owen: Agonism

David Owen, *Nietzsche, Politics and Modernity*.
- N. is a serious critic of **liberalism**: of Kant and the early Rawls through perspectivism, genealogy and will to power; of the late Rawls and Rorty through an ethics of the Overman, amor fati and recurrence (see [[Nietzsche: Amor Fati and Becoming What You Are]]).
- His positive politics is **agonistic**, from the early essay "Homer's Contest": Hesiod's two Eris-goddesses, one destructive and one good, the envy that drives competition. Every great Hellene "passes on the torch of competition". **Ostracism** originally meant removing the pre-eminent man "so that a new contest of powers can be awakened". The Greeks wanted, as protection against a genius, "a second genius".
- Owen draws out a democratic moral: no perspective may be allowed to end the contest, including a God's-eye view. He argues N.'s agonism cultivates better human beings than Rawls's overlapping consensus. This is the line that Hatab, Connolly and Honig take further, into "agonistic democracy".

## 7. Leiter (SEP): No Political Philosophy at All

- The literature splits into the **aristocratic politics view** (Detwiler, Losurdo) and the **anti-politics view**.
- Leiter's case: N. has no systematic views about the state or the justice of institutions. The aristocratic reading leans on a handful of texts, mainly A 56–57 on Manu (rhetorical, per Brobjer) and BGE 257 on "slavery". Detwiler relies on the unpublished "Greek State" and on passages with political "implications", which aren't a political philosophy. Detwiler himself admits that the political implications "are never center stage for long".
- What N. really is: an **esoteric moralist**. His target is the moral vocabulary that keeps potential higher types from flourishing. His audience is those few, not society.
- But Leiter grants the point that matters most. In *Nietzsche on Morality* (2002, quoted by Huddleston) he says the **egalitarian premise** of all contemporary moral and political theory, the equal worth or dignity of each person, "is simply absent in Nietzsche's work".

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's right**
- **Against flattery.** N. is right that modern talk of "dignity" often costs nothing, and that a society can chatter about the dignity of labour while treating workers as interchangeable inputs. His contempt for an existence aimed at the "green pasture happiness of the herd" is what Orthodox ascetics would also say about a life of comfort. Huddleston's point that most people's best life is **not** the comfortable life is in a sense the Gospel's too.
- **Against the myth of a neutral state.** "The Greek State" is horrifying, but it isn't naive. States are born in conquest, and culture has always rested on someone's surplus labour: the pyramids, Athens, the cathedrals, the cotton fields. Liberal theory likes to forget this. N. won't let us. Compare Augustine's "what are kingdoms but great robberies?" ([[Augustine's City of God]]).
- **The Greeks really were like this.** On slavery and *agon* he's a better historian than the Winckelmann tradition of serene Greeks ([[Nietzsche and the Greeks]]). Aristotle did defend natural slavery ([[Aristotle's Politics]]).

**Where it fails**
- **Dignity: given, not earned.** Huddleston's contrast is exactly right, and here the Christian simply says no. "Did not he that made me in the womb make him?" (Job 31:15): Job is already arguing that master and slave are equals before God. The ground of dignity is not achievement but **the image of God**, and it can't be lost or earned. See [[Image and Likeness]].
  - **St Gregory of Nyssa**, preaching on Ecclesiastes 2:7 ("I got me servants and maidens"), is the clearest answer to "The Greek State" from the fourth century. Paraphrasing his fourth homily: you condemn to slavery a man whose nature is free; God made him ruler over the earth, so for how many coins did you buy the image of God? Not even God enslaves what is free. N. said early Christianity found slavery unobjectionable. Gregory is the counterexample, and he says it in a sermon, not a treatise.
- **The pyramid is upside down.** "Whosoever will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all" (Mark 10:44). N. saw this was the decisive reversal, the "slave revolt in morality". But in the Gospel greatness is not abolished. It's **redefined**: the Son of man is greatest *because* he serves. And Paul's body in 1 Cor 12 is the exact antithesis of BGE 258's vine on the oak: there "the members which seem to be more feeble, are necessary", and "God hath tempered the body together, having given more abundant honour to that part which lacked". The few exist for the many, not the reverse.
- **The genius he serves is himself.** "Tool of the genius" means the masses justify their existence by serving people like Wagner, or N. But the only one with the right to require that all things serve him is the one who "came not to be ministered unto, but to minister". All genius is a gift held in trust. Even on N.'s terms, the artists he admired were more often servants than masters: Bach signed his scores *Soli Deo gloria*.
- **"Life is will to power"** is not an observation but a choice of first principle, and it is the one Thucydides put in the mouths of the Athenians at Melos: the strong do what they can. The Church's answer is that the deepest reality is not power but **self-giving love** (kenosis, Phil 2:7). Girard puts it sharply: N. saw clearly that the Gospel takes the side of the victim, and chose Dionysus, the god of the lynching crowd ([[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]]).
- **Losurdo is right that the politics can't be peeled off.** I think he overstates it (not everything N. wrote is about the Commune). But Leiter's "esoteric moralist" doesn't make the problem go away. The "money-hermits", the praise of Manu and the call for a "new slavery" are in N.'s own hand. An ethics in which the masses' value consists in being raw material for the great will have political consequences whatever the author intended. The twentieth century used them.

**For Orthodox political theology**
- Interesting overlap: N. hates the liberal state, the "coldest of all cold monsters", as much as some Orthodox critics of liberalism. But he hates it as **levelling**. The Orthodox *symphonia* ideal hates it, where it does, for being **godless**, and it remains bound to the dignity of the least. A Byzantine emperor was crowned under the icon of Christ, as a servant. See [[Orthodox Political Theology: Symphonia, Autocracy or Democracy?]], [[The Case for One Ruler: Aquinas, Dante, Maistre, Kuehnelt-Leddihn]] and [[Conservatism: Burke, de Maistre, Scruton]].
- If you want hierarchy without aristocratic radicalism, the model is a monastery: there really is an order of rank, and the abbot is the servant of all.
