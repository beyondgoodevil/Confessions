The *Genealogy* reads like a history of morality, but Ridley's point is that it's really about **conscience**: how we relate to ourselves. Conscience = being able to make yourself the object of your own thought, and so of your own will (have I done well? should I change?). It's where values get "forged and tested". So each of the *Genealogy*'s figures is a way of having a conscience, and N.'s real question is which new way is still open to us, given how we got here.

N. = Nietzsche, GM = *Genealogy of Morals* (essay.section).

## 0. The Frame: Kant's Enlightenment, Made Harder

- Ridley starts from Kant's "What is Enlightenment?". Man stays immature through laziness and cowardice and lets "guardians" think for him. Enlightenment means daring to use your own reason.
- N. agrees there's a crisis of authority, but thinks it goes much deeper than Kant saw. Kant himself is a case of it:
  - he puts man's real self in a timeless, rational, otherworldly realm, which makes everything temporal and bodily "alien";
  - that is the **ascetic ideal** under a new name.
- The "metaphysician's prejudice": the highest values can't come from this "transitory, seductive, deceptive, paltry world". The cure is **history**: show where values actually came from.
- Two terms Ridley uses throughout:
  - **bad bad conscience**: self-relation based on hatred of self and world (slave morality)
  - **good bad conscience**: the same capacity for inner tension and self-reflection, used creatively
- Man is "a bridge, a great promise" (GM II.16), a bow under tension. N. isn't telling the simple story "we were noble, became slavish, should be noble again".

## 1. The Slave

The **immanent** first phase of the slave revolt.

- **Noble valuation**: "good" comes first, spontaneously, from self-affirmation (the powerful, "high-minded"). "Bad" is an afterthought, a "contrasting shade" (GM I.11). Political superiority becomes superiority of soul (GM I.6).
- **Slave valuation**: starts with a No to what is outside and different. "Evil" (the noble, the oppressor) comes first, and "good" (oneself) is derived from it. It begins when "*ressentiment* itself becomes creative" (GM I.10).
- **Bad conscience in its raw state** belongs to nobles and slaves alike. Living under custom and the state means your instincts can't discharge outward, so they "turn inward" (GM II.16). This "internalization" is "an illness as pregnancy is an illness". It's neutral: it can go either way.
- The slaves, being most repressed, develop the most **interiority**. They suffer more, and they become more cunning than their masters.
- **The trick**: the doer/deed distinction. Separate the "doer" from what he does and you can blame the bird of prey for being one, and praise the lamb's weakness as a choice: impotence becomes "goodness", inability to take revenge becomes "forgiveness", waiting at the door becomes "patience" (GM I.13–14).
- **Why it works**: "What really arouses indignation against suffering is not suffering as such but the senselessness of suffering" (GM II.7). Before the revolt the slave could only see his pain as good *for the nobles*. Now it has his own meaning: he is **good because he suffers**, and his oppressor is evil.
- Ridley: this really is a "revolution in conscience". But it's only an "imaginary revenge". Nothing outside has changed.

## 2. The Priest

The **transcendental** second phase, which completes the revolt.

- The slave can now give meaning to suffering caused by the nobles, but not to suffering as such, to the "whole business of being alive". His position is grotesque: the "good" man still lives under the "evil" one.
- **The priest is a kind of noble**: a priestly caste can be the highest caste. He isn't simply an oppressed slave. His originality is in seeing what the slavish way of life can be used for. "Dominion over the suffering is his kingdom" (GM III.15).
- **His masterstroke: changing the direction of *ressentiment*.** "'I suffer: someone must be to blame for it', thinks every sickly sheep." The priest answers: quite so, someone is to blame, and it's *you* (GM III.15). Guilt is turned inward and then made infinite before an infinite creditor, God (GM II.21–22).
- Result: the slave no longer sees himself as a suffering animal but as a **soul** exiled in a body, and this world as a "wrong road" back to another. That is the ascetic ideal in full.
- **N.'s ambivalence.** Physiologically the ascetic ideal is an "artifice for the *preservation* of life" (GM III.13). It gave the only comprehensive answer to "why man at all?": "man was saved thereby... the will itself was saved" (GM III.28). Any meaning is better than none.
- Ridley's conclusion: **N. needs the priest**. If a revolution in conscience this big happened once, it could happen again in the opposite direction. His fight with the priest is a fight to succeed him, almost Oedipal. He's too close to the enemy to "love" him as an enemy.

## 3. The Philosopher

The first of three figures (with artist and scientist) who seem neither slavish nor priestly. N. is looking for "chinks" in the modern conscience from which change could start. But he's a modern ascetic too, so these are self-examinations.

- **The caterpillar.** For a long time the philosopher could only exist disguised as a priest or contemplative: "the ascetic priest provided... the repulsive and gloomy caterpillar form in which alone the philosopher could live" (GM III.10). Can the butterfly now come out?
- **Ascetic means, not ascetic ideal.** The philosopher's "poverty, humility, chastity" (GM III.8) are conditions for his work, an "optimum of favorable conditions" (GM III.7). He masters "something in life", not life itself. So he doesn't deny existence the way the priest does.
- **Kant again** = philosophy lacking "the courage for itself". He starts by questioning priestly authority, then opens "a secret path" to the old ideal (GM III.25).
- **Ridley's key word: *ephectic*** (from *ephexis*, holding back judgement). The philosopher refuses to fix himself under any settled self-description ("he who possesses is possessed"), and he refuses to nail down the present.
  - The priest's present is "timely": judged once and for all as a mistake.
  - The philosopher's present is "**untimely**": left open as becoming.
- **Verdict**: thin. It boils down to "be reflexively ephectic!" N. ends with a question: is there enough pride and daring "for 'the philosopher' to be henceforth possible on earth?" (GM III.10).

## 4. The Artist

- **The official, dismissive view** (GM III.2–5, mostly about Wagner):
  - Artists are "valets of some morality, philosophy or religion"; they never stand alone. So ask the priests, not the artists, what ascetic ideals mean.
  - Separate the artist from his work: he's only "the womb, the soil, sometimes the dung" it grows from (GM III.4).
  - His "typical velleity": he wants to *be* what he can only represent.
- **Beauty.** Kant's disinterested pleasure vs Stendhal's "a promise of happiness". N. says Schopenhauer's disinterestedness was itself very interested: a wish to escape sexual "torture" (GM III.6).
- **The unofficial view**, which Ridley thinks is the real one:
  - The founders of states are "artists" who impose form on a shapeless population (GM II.17). That act of form-giving is what produces the internalized soul in the first place.
  - The priest is the supreme artist of the soul. His "orgies of feeling", built on guilt, make the soul "resound with heart-rending... music" (GM III.19–20).
  - So N. needs a **counter-art of the soul**: making *yourself* beautiful, closing the gap between artist and material.
- Ridley: the official put-down of art is a rhetorical foil. Trivialize ordinary art, and your counter-art gains a "negative grandeur" without having to say much about it. N. is much clearer about the priest's art than about his own.

## 5. The Scientist

- The question (GM III.23): hasn't modern science, which gets on "without God, the beyond, and the virtues of denial", replaced the ascetic ideal? **No.**
- **Two kinds of scholar:**
  - **The anaesthetized**: people who hide in their work from "every kind of discontent". It's the priest's own prescription of "mechanical activity" (GM III.18). "I approve of their work", but they're no threat to anything.
  - **The truth-believers**: men with a real "faith in truth". These are the most recent and noblest form of the ascetic ideal, because truth is treated as unconditional, divine, beyond question (GM III.24).
- **The scientist as fifth columnist.** Christian truthfulness, trained for two thousand years, finally forbids itself the lie of belief in God (GM III.27). Then "the will to truth becomes conscious of itself as a problem".
- **Perspectivism.** "There is only a perspective seeing, only a perspective 'knowing'" (GM III.12). Ridley: this isn't relativism. It's closer to Putnam's "internal realism": truth claims are answerable to standards within a "system of purposes". What N. denies is a complete, final, view-from-nowhere truth, which he thinks is self-contradictory.
- **Where GM ends.** Without its "closed system of will, goal, and interpretation", the will is set loose. It "would rather will nothingness than not will" (GM III.28). The danger is nihilism, not relativism. See [[Nietzsche on Nihilism and Its Overcoming]].

## 6. The Noble

- "What are you really doing, erecting an ideal or knocking one down?" (GM II.24). N. raises the question and then goes back to knocking down.
- **Not a new barbarism.** The careless reading (Ridley names Russell) goes: N. prefers the nobles of GM I, whose morality involves "murder, arson, rape, and torture", so N. favours those. He's fascinated by them, but that's not what he recommends.
- **Noble vs slavish virtues differ in attitude, not behaviour.**
  - Mercy is a virtue when it comes from strength. A society strong enough can let offenders go unpunished: mercy is "the privilege of the most powerful" (GM II.10).
  - Noble gentleness doesn't allow arson. The difference is the self-relation underneath: affirmation vs *ressentiment*.
- **What's needed is an ideal that is immanent "through and through"**, one that doesn't slander the world.
- Means:
  - **severity against one's own heart**, which measures how much of existence one can affirm;
  - **style**: giving form to one's own character, so the edge of affirmation doesn't tip into vengefulness.
- **"Self-overcoming" has two meanings:**
  - overcoming your own inclinations
  - humanity overcoming itself toward the **sovereign individual** of GM II.2, who looks more like a hope or wish than a description
- **The Last Man**, who has "discovered happiness" and blinks, is both the strongest argument *against* affirming life and the proof that life can go on without a transcendent goal.
- **Ridley's conclusion**: N. can only **recommend** an ideal, seduce people toward it. He can't exhibit it. What GM shows is **commitment** to an ideal, being "a genuine battleground of these opposed values" (GM I.16). And Ridley thinks N.'s "nausea" at the small man is his own brand of *ressentiment*.

## 7. Conclusion: Thinker Envying Musicians

- Wagner could make conviction out of feeling. N., a thinker, would first have to say clearly what a new nobility looks like, which he can't. Hence Zarathustra, and his envy of musicians.
- Ridley's final description of N.: "an exhilaratingly earnest, terrifyingly exacting optimist", a child of the Protestant Enlightenment.

## 8. The Six at a Glance

| type | relation to suffering | relation to the ascetic ideal | kind of conscience |
|---|---|---|---|
| slave | good *because* I suffer; the oppressor is evil | not yet: still this-worldly | bad bad conscience, first form |
| priest | you are to blame; this world is a wrong road | inventor and administrator | bad bad conscience, completed and made transcendent |
| philosopher | uses hardship as working conditions | wears it as a caterpillar's disguise | ephectic, "untimely" |
| artist | gives suffering form (the state, the soul) | usually a valet of it; possibly a counter-art | good bad conscience, at best |
| scientist | anaesthetizes it, or seeks truth at any cost | its last and most honest form, which destroys itself | will to truth turning on itself |
| noble | affirms it, even demands it | must outgrow it with an immanent ideal | recommended, not shown |

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's sharp**
- The psychology of counterfeit virtue is real. Weakness *can* dress itself up as meekness, and spite *can* pass as justice. The ascetic Fathers say the same: humility that is really cowardice or vainglory isn't humility. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- "Senselessness, not suffering, is what can't be borne" is true and important. It's why theodicy matters to ordinary people. See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].
- Ridley's reading of the noble (strength shown as mercy, not as cruelty) is much fairer than the popular one, and it partly answers N.'s own case.

**Where it goes wrong**
- **Christian meekness isn't weakness renamed.** The model is Christ, who has power and lays it down: "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again" (John 10:18). "Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: But made himself of no reputation" (Phil 2:6–7). This is exactly the "mercy of the strong" N. himself calls noble. Max Scheler's reply (*Ressentiment*, 1912) makes this point: Christian love comes from overflow and stoops down, while modern humanitarianism is the real *ressentiment*.
- **The priest.** "You yourself are to blame" is a caricature of repentance. In the Fathers, guilt isn't infinite debt to be paid off but sickness to be healed, and the goal is joy, not self-hatred. Compare [[Recapitulation Doctrine vs PSA]] on the juridical picture N. is attacking.
- **The ascetic ideal.** Orthodox asceticism isn't contempt for creation. It is ordering the passions so the whole person, body included, can be transfigured. N.'s "this world vs another" is Plato's two worlds read back into Christianity. The Christian hope is the resurrection of *this* body and a renewed creation.
- **The noble ideal** stays empty, as Ridley admits. A self-legislated, immanent ideal with no measure outside oneself can't settle which "style" is better. That's the same problem as in [[Relativism]] and [[Is Ought]].
