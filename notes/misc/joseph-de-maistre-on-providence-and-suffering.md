The *St Petersburg Dialogues* (published posthumously, 1821) are eleven evenings of conversation on "the temporal government of Providence".
- **The speakers:**
  - the **Count**, Maistre himself, then Sardinian ambassador to Russia
  - the **Senator**, a Russian Orthodox privy councillor, probably modelled on Tamara, a former ambassador to Constantinople
  - the **Chevalier**, a young French émigré
- **Who says what:** some of the most famous passages (on war, and on hereditary guilt) are given to the Senator, not the Count. Maistre liked the salon form because it let him be provocative, a "methodical extravagance".
- **The question** for the first evening: "the happiness of the wicked and the misfortune of the just! This is the great scandal to human reason."

## 1. The Question Reframed (Dialogue 1)

- The Senator and Chevalier refuse the preachers' answer that the wicked will be punished and the just rewarded in the next life. It's true, but **dangerous on its own**. Unbelievers ask for nothing better than a world where justice happens only after death: "if a truly moral government exists in this world... why relieve [crime] of this fear?"
- The Count: we don't know what we're saying. Soldiers never complain that "musket balls hit only honest men". Goods and evils fall like "a kind of **lottery** where each, without distinction, can draw a winning or a losing ticket".
- So the real question is why the just aren't **exempt** from evils common to everyone, and why the wicked aren't **deprived** of goods common to everyone. Stated plainly, the Count thinks, it shows its own absurdity: why should anyone be exempt from the human condition?

## 2. The Executioner (Dialogue 1, the Count)

- The most famous passage: a portrait of the public executioner. Others move house so as not to live near him. He is called to the square, breaks a parricide on the wheel, "congratulates himself", is thrown his gold from a distance, eats and sleeps. "Is this a man? Yes. God receives him in his shrines."
- "And yet all greatness, all power, all subordination rests on the executioner; he is both the horror and the bond of human association. Remove this incomprehensible agent from the world, and in a moment order gives way to chaos."
- So "**God, who is the author of sovereignty, is therefore also the author of punishment.**" "For the pillars of the earth are the LORD's, and he hath set the world upon them" (1 Sam 2:8). There is "a universal and visible order for the temporal punishment of crimes".
- **Judicial error**, he argues, is rarer than people think. He doubts Calas's innocence and mocks Voltaire's campaign for him. And a man executed for a crime he didn't commit may deserve punishment for a crime nobody knew about: "Providence, for whom all things, even obstacles, are means".

## 3. Illness as Punishment (Dialogue 1, the Count)

- "If there were no moral evil on the earth, there would be no physical illness." Intemperance alone causes most disease. Seneca on Nero's Rome: "Are you astonished by this innumerable quantity of illnesses? Count the cooks."
- Diseases mirror sins: there are "actual and original, accidental and habitual, mortal and venial" ones. Named sins like murder and incest correspond to named diseases; vague faults like fraud to vague complaints like "malaise".
- Christ healed while remitting sins, and told the healed man, "sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee" (John 5:14).
- **The Count's own limit:** some illness is just "the accidental result of a general law... the most moral man must die". Of two men running, one to save a life and one to take it, either may die of pleurisy.

## 4. Original Sin and the Suffering of Infants (Dialogues 2–3)

- Man's intelligence is "originally degraded". Savages are not primitive man but **degraded** man. Language itself bears the marks of the Fall.
- On the suffering of baptised children: "The infant suffers even as he dies, because he belongs to a mass that must suffer and die." So "every evil... all suffering, is a punishment imposed for some actual or original crime".
- He adds that his main argument doesn't need this point: "if this heredity of pain embarrasses you, forget... all that I have said".

## 5. The Two Axioms (Dialogue 8, the Chevalier's Summary)

1. "No man is punished because he is just, but always **because he is a man**": "it is false to say that virtue suffers in this world, whereas it is human nature that suffers, and it always merits suffering".
2. Temporal happiness is promised "not to the virtuous man, but to virtue": the greatest happiness goes to the greatest virtue **in general**.

And "there is no such thing as a just man", so no one may refuse his share of human misery. The Senator adds that "every man suffers because he is a man, and because he would be God if he did not suffer".

## 6. Suffering as Medicine and Expiation (Dialogues 8–9)

- **The Senator**: if the just man accepts his sufferings and God accepts that acceptance, nothing could be more just or more fortunate for him. Without misfortune we'd forget God. God "chastises in this world instead of chastising more severely in the next", which leads him to **purgatory**: how else could Robespierre's soul go to God the same way as Louis XVI's?
- **The Chevalier**: "sufferings are for the virtuous man what battles are for the soldier".
- **The Count**: God acts like a surgeon who can see the hidden disease. "The event does not create wickedness, it manifests it": the viper is venomous before it bites. So God sometimes strikes a man who seems healthy to cure him before the crisis. Or He spares a known sinner because punishment would be useless, as a doctor stops tormenting an incurable patient.
- The sufferings of the just can also, "by religious acceptance be turned to the profit of the guilty".

## 7. Prayer Against the "Laws of Nature" (Dialogues 4–6)

- **The Senator**: "every physical evil is a punishment", and the "scourges of Heaven" come from a great national crime or an accumulation of individual ones. So each could be prevented, "first by a better life, and second, by prayer".
- **The Count**: the philosophers say prayer is useless against invariable laws. But why do they use fire engines and medicine, then? If it's a law that lightning destroys, it may also be a law "that prayer, sprinkled in time on the FIRE OF HEAVEN, extinguishes or diverts it". "There is no midpoint between a rigid, absolute, and universal fatalism, and the common faith of mankind in the effectiveness of prayer."
- **"Modern pride" confuses describing an effect with revealing its cause.** Finding that lightning is electricity "enlarged the miracle". "It is proved that electricity... differs little from... lightning, THEREFORE it is not God who thunders": "Your THEREFORE is only a fool!" (after Molière).
- It can never be proved that a nation prayed and wasn't heard. "Every nation that prays is heard", but none of us knows whether we're asking for something harmful. Prayer is hard: Plato admitted man "does not know how to pray".

## 8. The "Divinity" of War (Dialogue 7, the Senator)

- **The puzzle**: why has "the most honourable thing in the world... always been the right to shed innocent blood innocently"? Men are made to love and weep, yet do "with enthusiasm what [they hold] in horror". Soldiers murder tyrants, but they never refuse to fight.
- **The answer**: a "universal law of the violent destruction of living things", from plants to predators to man, who "kills to kill". Man is charged with slaughtering man, because human justice catches only a few criminals and "the earth itself [is] crying out and demanding blood". "The entire earth, perpetually steeped in blood, is nothing but an immense altar."
- "**War is therefore divine in itself**, since it is a law of the world." It is divine in five ways:
  - in its "supernatural" consequences
  - in its mysterious glory
  - in the protection of great commanders
  - in the way it breaks out, through men carried along by circumstance
  - in results that "escape the speculations of human reason"
- The other side of the same speech: Christian war in Europe had become humane, with civilities between enemies and the hospitals of St John of God and St Vincent de Paul. "If you look closely at war, never will Christianity appear more sublime."
- Battles are now lost **morally**, not by body count; Maistre tells this as an exchange with a general.

## 9. Hereditary Guilt and Solidarity (Dialogue 10, the Senator)

- If you're proud of an ancestor who died beside St Louis, you must accept shame for one who betrayed him: "you have to accept the shame in good grace, if it falls to you, or renounce the glory". "At first glance, nothing is so shocking as hereditary malediction; yet why not, since benediction is the same?"
- "The family is no doubt composed of individuals who, according to reason, have nothing in common; but according to instinct and universal persuasion, every family is one." This is most visible in royal families: "the king does not die". It's how he tries to understand an innocent king like Louis XVI perishing.

## 10. Substitution and Blood (Dialogues 9–10; Elucidation on Sacrifices)

- **The Count**: the whole discussion leads to "**substitution, which is the great mystery of the world**". Every Protestant father who pardons one child for another's sake grants "indulgences". Christianity's scales: all crimes on one side, all satisfactions and "the tears of the innocent" on the other.
- **The Chevalier**: the universal rites of circumcision say "anathema on human generation, and SALVATION BY BLOOD", until Calvary, where "we understood why man has always believed that one soul could be saved by another". Christianity "show[s] him what he is worth by showing him what he has cost".
- **The *Elucidation on Sacrifices***:
  - Antiquity held that **blood is life**, and he enlists the physiologist John Hunter for this.
  - So "heaven, provoked against flesh and blood, could only be appeased by blood". "The entire theory rests on the dogma of substitution... **the innocent can pay for the guilty**."
  - Hence animal victims: the gentlest and most useful animals, never beasts of prey, as a "substitute soul" (*antipsychon*), burned to show that fire is sin's punishment.
  - Human sacrifice was the corruption of a true principle, not its origin.
  - Christ fulfils it, and the Eucharist continues it ("all those who participate in the same victim are of one body").

## 11. The Revolution as Chastisement (*Considerations on France*)

- The same theology applied to 1789. The Revolution leads men more than men lead it; its agents are instruments. "If it makes use of the vilest instruments, it is because it punishes so as to regenerate."
- France had led Christendom ("Most Christian King"), so its fall was a punishment proportionate to its mission.
- The Revolution is "radically bad... pure impurity". The people "had only seen the Revolution; it was necessary for them to feel it".
- Blood has a strange fertility: "blood is the fertilizer of that plant we call genius". The return of the king will be "natural", without effort, as with Monck in England.

## 12. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's strong**
- He refuses to put all of God's justice off until the next life. This is a real point against a deist God who winds up the world and leaves it.
- "Because he is a man, not because he is just" fits the Fathers. Death and suffering belong to fallen nature as a whole, not as a tally of personal guilt. See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]].
- The prayer argument is sound: discovering a mechanism doesn't remove a cause. Secondary causes are how God acts, not a rival to Him.
- The universal practice of sacrifice is real evidence of a sense of guilt and of a need for a mediator.

**What's troubling**
- Making **every** calamity a punishment runs into Christ's own words: "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3); "those eighteen, upon whom the tower in Siloam fell... think ye that they were sinners above all men...? I tell you, Nay" (Luke 13:4–5). Job's friends said what the Senator says. The Count himself pulls back to "general laws" when pressed.
- The executioner and war passages sacralise violence. "War is divine" goes much further than "God brings good out of war".
- His treatment of judicial error (Calas, the innocent man who probably deserved it for something else) makes human injustice serve Providence too easily.
- **The atonement:** "heaven... could only be appeased by blood" and a tally of satisfactions is the far end of the juridical model. Compare Athanasius, for whom the problem is **corruption** and death, not a debt of blood demanded by the Father. See [[Recapitulation Doctrine vs PSA]]. Maistre's real insight is **solidarity**, that we are not isolated individuals before God. That fits the Orthodox picture of one human nature healed in Christ better than his satisfaction language does.
- On death as the enemy (not a law of the world to be made "divine"), see [[1 Corinthians 15:26: The Last Enemy That Will Be Destroyed Is Death]].
