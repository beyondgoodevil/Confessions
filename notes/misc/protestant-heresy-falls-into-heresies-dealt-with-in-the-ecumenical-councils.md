## 1. Arianism (First Council of Nicaea, 325 AD)

- **Heresy:** Arianism denied the full divinity of Christ, claiming He was a created being, subordinate to the Father.
- **Protestant Parallels:** While mainstream Protestants affirm the divinity of Christ, sects like **Unitarians** or **Jehovah's Witnesses** (though not typically included under Protestantism) revive Arian-like beliefs by denying Christ's equality with the Father. Other fringe Protestant groups may also diminish Christ's divine status, thus reflecting Arian tendencies.

## 2. Nestorianism (Council of Ephesus, 431 AD)

- **Heresy:** Nestorianism argued that Christ had two separate persons (divine and human), implying a disconnection between His two natures. It also rejected the title of Mary as **Theotokos** ("God-bearer").
- **Protestant Parallels:** Some Protestants, especially those who reject Marian doctrines and titles like **Theotokos**, could unintentionally lean towards Nestorianism by underemphasizing the unity of Christ's divine and human natures. By denying Mary as the "Mother of God," they risk separating Christ's divinity from His humanity.

## 3. Monophysitism (Council of Chalcedon, 451 AD)

- **Heresy:** Monophysitism taught that Christ had only one nature after the Incarnation, either solely divine or a fusion of divine and human, effectively minimizing His humanity.
- **Protestant Parallels:** In certain Protestant traditions, especially those emphasizing Christ's divinity and miraculous powers, His humanity may be overshadowed. This can lead to a Monophysite-like view, where Christ's full human experience (e.g., suffering, emotions, human will) is downplayed, resembling the Monophysite error.

## 4. Monothelitism (Third Council of Constantinople, 680–681 AD)

- **Heresy:** Monothelitism claimed that Christ had two natures but only one divine will, denying the existence of a distinct human will.
- **Protestant Parallels:** While most Protestant groups do not explicitly teach Monothelitism, there is often little focus on the theological significance of Christ's two wills (human and divine). Some Evangelical or charismatic movements, which heavily emphasize divine will and miracles, might unintentionally neglect the idea that Christ had a fully human will, thus leaning towards Monothelitism.

## 5. Iconoclasm (Second Council of Nicaea, 787 AD)

- **Heresy:** Iconoclasm rejected the veneration of icons, which was seen as undermining the reality of Christ's Incarnation and His ability to be depicted in human form.
- **Protestant Parallels:** Many Protestant denominations, particularly Reformed and Calvinist traditions, reject the use and veneration of icons, reflecting an iconoclastic mindset. By rejecting the incarnational theology that justifies the depiction of Christ in images, these Protestants revive a central feature of the iconoclastic heresy, undermining the significance of Christ's full humanity.
