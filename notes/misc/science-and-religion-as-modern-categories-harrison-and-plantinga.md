Most debates about "science and religion" assume that there are two things with those names, that they have always existed, and that the question is whether they get on. Two Gifford lecturers attack that assumption from opposite directions. **Peter Harrison**, a historian of ideas, argues that the two categories are recent and that the supposed perennial conflict cannot have happened, because before the nineteenth century there was nothing for the two sides to be. **Alvin Plantinga**, an analytic philosopher, keeps the categories and asks where the conflict really lies. His answer: there is "superficial conflict but deep concord between science and theistic religion, but superficial concord and deep conflict between science and naturalism" (p. ix). I give the two books equal weight. Harrison explains how the question came to be asked. Plantinga answers it in the terms it is usually asked in.

### 1. The Question

Harrison opens with a map. Suppose someone wrote about a war between Israel and Egypt in 1600. The claim would be false, and not because the dates were wrong: neither state existed, and the land belonged to the Ottoman Empire (pp. 1–2). Histories of "science and religion" make the same mistake, he says. They "involve the distorting projection of our present conceptual maps back onto the intellectual territories of the past" (p. 3). His second image is the natural kind. "Jade" turned out to name two minerals, jadeite and nephrite; whales were once fish and bats birds (pp. 4–5). The categories "science" and "religion" may be like "jade": convenient labels that do not carve nature at its joints. If so, "the idea of a perennial conflict between science and religion must be false, just as claims about an early modern conflict between Israel and Egypt must be false" (p. 5). He adds a warning: careless categories can "not only generate unhelpful conflict" but also "disguise what perhaps ought to be legitimate sources of tension between the ways of faith and the formal study of nature" (p. 6).

Plantinga begins with the New Atheists. Dawkins, Dennett, Hitchens and Harris offer ridicule and "naked contempt" rather than argument, and he calls their project "adolescent rebellion carried on by other means" (p. x). His key term is **naturalism**: the view that there is no God "or anything like God". It is stronger than atheism, and he calls it a "quasi-religion", because like a religion it answers Kant's three questions about God, freedom and immortality (pp. ix–x, 311). The book has four parts: alleged conflict (evolution, divine action), superficial conflict (evolutionary psychology, biblical criticism), concord (fine-tuning, design, the roots of science), and deep conflict (the evolutionary argument against naturalism) (pp. xi–xiii). He quotes C. D. Broad's 1939 complaint that science-and-religion debate "had acquired something of the repulsiveness of half cold mutton in half-congealed gravy" (p. xv).

Both writers therefore deny the popular picture of an ancient, ongoing war. Harrison denies it historically: the combatants are recent constructs. Plantinga denies it philosophically: the real enemy of science is a rival worldview dressed up as science.

### 2. Harrison: Religio and Scientia as Virtues

Harrison's first exhibit is Aquinas. In the *Summa Theologiae* (2a2ae), *religio* is a **moral virtue** attached to justice, giving God the worship owed to him. It consists mainly of interior acts of devotion and prayer, and it is a mean between irreligion and superstition (pp. 7–8). "There is no sense in which *religio* refers to systems of propositional beliefs, and no sense of different religions (plural)" (p. 7). For Augustine "true religion" meant the right worship of the one God, and in the *Retractions* he said true religion had existed from the beginning of the human race, Christianity being a form of it (pp. 9–10). Latin has no definite article, and translations show the shift. Calvin's *Institutio Christianae Religionis*, a training in godliness, became "Institution of Christian Religion" in 1561, "the Christian Religion" in 1762, and finally "Institutes" (pp. 10–11).

*Scientia* went the same way. For Aquinas it was an **intellectual virtue**, a habit of mind that, together with *intellectus* and *sapientia*, perfects the intellect. It grows "by addition", as when someone learns more geometry and the same habit grows in him (p. 12). Writers as late as the 1660s and 1670s still defined science as "a habit" (pp. 12–13). Harrison cites Eleonore Stump: for Aquinas intellectual excellence was possible only together with moral excellence, and carnal vice produced mental dullness (p. 13). There were many sciences; the English spoke of a science of angels, even of drinking as an "eighth liberal science" (p. 14). The 1771 *Britannica* still defined science as doctrine demonstrated from self-evident principles (p. 15).

His summary of the change: "in the Middle Ages scientific knowledge was an instrument for the inculcation of scientific habits of mind; now scientific habits of mind are cultivated primarily as an instrument for the production of scientific knowledge" (p. 15). Then the thesis of the book: "During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, both religion and science were literally turned inside out" (p. 16). While science was an intellectual habit and religion a moral one, the two could not conflict; they were different perfections of the same person (p. 16).

### 3. Harrison: Philosophy as a Way of Life

The standard story runs in three stages: science born in Greece, smothered by Christianity, reborn in the seventeenth century (p. 22). Popper said the Greek tradition was suppressed by "a victorious and intolerant Christianity" (p. 24). Harrison argues that the first stage is already false. Thales held that "all things are full of gods"; the Hippocratic oath invoked Apollo; Anaxagoras's cosmos was ordered by *nous* (pp. 24–25). More importantly, Greek natural philosophy was part of **philosophy as a way of life**, in Pierre Hadot's phrase. Epicurean physics freed its students from fear of death and the gods; the Stoics studied nature in order to live according to it; Ptolemy said astronomy made its followers lovers of divine beauty and reformed their natures (pp. 26–33). Simplicius listed the uses of physics: technologies (medicine, mechanics, war machines) and, above all, "a ladder that leads towards knowledge of God". Modernity reversed his order of priorities (p. 32). "To claim that our science was born in ancient Greece is to overlook what for ancient Greek philosophers was the main point of the exercise" (p. 33).

Early Christians did not present their faith as a "religion" among others. The *Epistle to Diognetus* called Christians a new race and way of life; Eusebius called Christianity "a new and true kind of divine philosophy" (pp. 34–40). Justin, Clement, Origen and Augustine treated it as the **true philosophy**, the fulfilment of what the schools sought (p. 41). Origen matched Proverbs to ethics, Ecclesiastes to natural philosophy and the Song of Songs to contemplation (p. 42). Augustine put all three branches under one command, "Love God, and love neighbour", and ordered knowledge as a path *through* scientia *to* sapientia (pp. 42–43). Even creeds were formative rather than informative. Augustine told catechumens to "treat your creed as your own personal mirror" (p. 46), and *credere in* meant "to cling by faith to God" (pp. 48–49). Rodney Needham called belief in the modern sense "a relatively modern linguistic invention" (p. 49).

Harrison draws three conclusions (pp. 51–54). The idea that theology suppressed natural philosophy is a myth; Aristotle and Chrysippus already put theology at the top. Christianity did shrink natural philosophy, but by taking over its moral and religious goals, which left it "the mundane technologies of medicine and mechanics, and the production of engines of war" (pp. 52–53). And there was no opposition between natural and religious explanation. It was Christians, Jews and Epicureans who denied that the heavens were divine. Philoponus, a Christian, denied it and was attacked by the pagan Simplicius, and Philoponus's theory of impetus later influenced Galileo (p. 53).

### 4. Harrison: The Two Books

Medieval Christians read **two books**, Scripture and nature, and read them together (p. 56). The warrants were Genesis 1:14, Psalm 19 and Romans 1:18–20 (p. 57). Hugh of St Victor wrote that "the whole sensible world is like a kind of book written by the finger of God", and that in divine speech "not only words, but even things have a meaning" (p. 60). The bestiaries read creatures allegorically: the pelican that revives its young with its own blood is Christ. Aquinas's *Adoro te* and Dante's *nostro pellicano* draw on it (pp. 61–62). It was "a world that was not primarily to be explained causally, nor exploited materially, but to be read and meditated upon" (p. 63). Augustine's rule was that "this world must be used, not enjoyed" (p. 64). After the Fall the reading was damaged; Bonaventure called the world "a book with writing front and back", and the liberal arts were a remedy that restored the divine likeness (pp. 65–66).

This was not natural theology in the modern sense. Harrison quotes William Alston's definition: "providing support for religious beliefs by starting from premises that neither are nor presuppose religious belief" (p. 71). The phrase "natural theology" is almost absent from patristic and medieval writing, and when Augustine and Aquinas use it (following Varro) they mean pagan theology about the gods of nature, which they call superstition (pp. 71–72). The Five Ways are a tiny part of the *Summa*, and Albert and Aquinas studied creatures as an *exercitium* forming the soul (pp. 69–73). In this kind of knowing the knower is conformed to the known: "the rational creature ... is made deiform" (p. 70).

Then the symbolic world came apart. Luther dismissed allegory as fit for "weak minds" and "idle men"; sola scriptura and iconoclasm stripped things of their meanings (p. 75). Bacon said creatures show God's power and wisdom "but not his image" (p. 76). Galileo's book of nature is "written in the language of mathematics" (p. 77); Boyle's creatures are "the stenography of God's omniscient hand" (pp. 77–78); naturalists began excluding "Mystick, Mythologick, or Hieroglyphick matter" (p. 78). Causation narrowed too. Descartes made the laws of nature direct divine volitions, which leads to occasionalism. Samuel Clarke said there is no "power of nature", only the will of God producing effects "in a continued, regular ... manner" (pp. 79–80). Harrison's verdict: "Causal explanation became a zero-sum game." There was now "one order of meaning—the literal sense—and one level of causation—efficient causation. Both of these developments were promoted by religious thinkers, and for religious reasons", and they put science and theology in "the same explanatory territory", the condition for competition (p. 80).

### 5. Harrison: The Birth of "Religion"

The Reformation dissolved the virtue model from the inside. Luther called Aristotle and Aquinas fools for thinking virtue is acquired by practice; righteousness "is not in us in a formal sense, as Aristotle maintains, but is outside us" (pp. 85–86). Grace became relational rather than a quality of the soul, and Harrison sees the parallel in nature: inner powers gave way to laws imposed from outside by God (p. 86). Total depravity reached the intellect, so the natural desire to know became suspect (pp. 86–87). Bacon called the mind a "distorting mirror"; Hooke said every man is prone "to slip into all sorts of errors" and prescribed "the real, the mechanical, the experimental Philosophy" (p. 88). Experiment was a discipline for fallen minds.

Calvin attacked "implicit faith", and religion became something one must know explicitly (p. 92). Harrison counts uses: in the 1600s "Christian religion" appears five times as often as "the Christian religion"; by the 1690s the ratio is reversed (p. 93). Some 500 catechisms were in print in mid-seventeenth-century England (p. 94). The Peace of Augsburg (1555) made "religion" a legal and political category, *cuius regio, eius religio* (p. 97). On the wars of religion Harrison says: "it is not a complete distortion to reverse the received understanding of these wars and say that the formation of the modern state was their cause, and the modern notion of religion a consequence" (p. 98). The world religions followed, projections of a fragmented Christendom onto other peoples: "Boudhism" in 1801, "Hindooism" in 1829, "Taouism" in 1838, "Confucianism" in 1862 (p. 101).

Once there were rival "religions", someone had to judge which was true (pp. 102–103). The answer was **evidence**. Isaac Barrow said "the proper object of faith is ... some proposition" (p. 106), whereas for Aquinas the object of faith was God, the First Truth (pp. 106–107). Stillingfleet called faith "an assent upon evidence" and Locke "assent founded on the highest reason" (pp. 107–108). The new natural philosophy supplied the evidence. Newton's General Scholium put discourse about God inside natural philosophy (p. 109); Kepler, Boyle and Hooke called natural inquiry a form of worship (p. 111). Natural theology in Alston's sense was born here, both "more modest" and "more ambitious" than what went before (pp. 113–114). By resting religion on scientific evidence it "gave hostages to fortune" (p. 108). Harrison endorses Michael Buckley's view that Enlightenment atheism was shaped by the Christianity it rejected, and quotes the aphorism that "no one doubted the existence of God until the Boyle lecturers undertook to prove it" (p. 115). Some resisted: Wesley wanted faith to be not "a cold lifeless assent" but "a disposition in the heart", and Barth and Bonhoeffer later rejected "religion" itself (p. 116).

### 6. Harrison: Utility, Progress and Dominion

Macaulay summed up Bacon in two words: "Utility and Progress" (p. 117). Harrison argues that the modern idea of progress depends on the reification of science. *Profectus* in Aquinas meant a person's growth in virtue (p. 120). Pascal moved progress from the person to the species: humanity is "a single man who subsists forever and learns continually" (p. 121). Knowledge became a cumulative external store, made possible by print, which also produced complaints about the "horrible mass of books" (pp. 122–124).

Usefulness was a hard sell at first. Swift's academy at Lagado extracted sunbeams from cucumbers; Shadwell's Sir Nicholas Gimcrack boasted "we virtuosos never find out anything of use"; Charles II laughed at the Royal Society for weighing air (pp. 125–126). In those days science sought legitimacy from religion; now it is the other way round (p. 126). Méric Casaubon objected that "To moralize men is the best use of any worldly thing" and that tending furnaces would not make anyone wise (p. 128). Harrison's point is that moral and practical utility had come apart: the experimental programme was "indifferent to the moral character of those who pursued it" (p. 129). Charity changed too, from a theological virtue into "a charity", an institution. Bacon wanted knowledge perfected and governed "in charity", "for the glory of the Creator and the relief of man's estate" (pp. 131–133).

**Dominion** had first been inward. Origen told his hearers "you have within yourself herds of cattle"; the beasts were the passions, and Aquinas said man's mastery of things follows his mastery of himself (pp. 137–138). When the microcosm idea declined and Genesis was read literally, dominion turned outward. Bacon ends the *Novum Organum*: by the Fall man lost both innocence and dominion over creation, and both "can even in this life be in some part repaired; the former by religion and faith, the latter by arts and sciences" (p. 138). Lynn White's charge that Christianity bears "a huge burden of guilt" for environmental damage is partly right, but it misses this earlier inward meaning (pp. 136–137). The result: "Progress had once been understood as progress toward a particular end. Now it had become an end in itself" (p. 141). And "Without goals, progress is just change" (p. 144).

### 7. Harrison: Inventing "Science" and the Conflict Myth

"Modern religion had its birth in the seventeenth century; modern science in the nineteenth" (p. 147). In 1867 W. G. Ward used "science" to mean physical and experimental science, "to the exclusion of theological and metaphysical", one of the first such uses (pp. 145–146). Until then natural theology was the shared framework. Paley's watch, the Bridgewater Treatises and Herschel's *Preliminary Discourse* all assumed that "Truth is single" (pp. 149–152). Natural theology was even hailed as "an inductive science" (p. 153), and Charles Hodge called induction "the true method of theology" (p. 154).

Then the boundary was drawn, in three moves (pp. 159–160):

- **An identity.** Whewell coined "scientist" in the 1830s, and someone called it "an American barbarous trisyllable" (pp. 160–161). Anglican clergy fell from 9.7 per cent of Royal Society fellows in 1849 to 3.1 per cent in 1899, and from nine British Association presidents in 1831–65 to none in 1866–1900 (p. 162). Huxley's X-Club wanted science "untrammelled by religious dogmas" and natural history rid of "women, amateurs, and parsons" (pp. 162–163).
- **A method.** Induction was no good as a boundary, because theologians claimed it too. The new boundary was the hypothetico-deductive method, and Huxley declared it "the only method by which truth could be ascertained" (pp. 168–170).
- **A contrast case and a myth.** Draper's *History of the Conflict between Religion and Science* (1875) and White's *History of the Warfare of Science with Theology* (1897) created the stories still repeated: medieval hostility, the flat earth, Galileo tortured, universal resistance to Darwin (p. 172). The flat earth is "sheer fabrication". In the Galileo affair the Church backed the scientific consensus of the day, and the Church was "the single most prominent supporter of astronomical research" (pp. 172–173). Draper's book ends by setting "quiescent, immobile faith" against "ever-advancing Science" (p. 175).

Ian Barbour's well-known typology (conflict, independence, dialogue, integration) does not escape the problem, because "integration" describes only the period before modern science existed (p. 175). Harrison concludes that neither term picks out a natural kind. "Religion" produces the problem of religious pluralism, "largely an artifact of the category". "Science" produces "an artificial unity", borrowing authority from mathematical models, useful technologies, natural history and causal explanation all at once (pp. 176–177). Today the rivals compete for the moral ground that natural theology once held. E. O. Wilson calls the evolutionary epic "probably the best myth we will ever have"; Harris and Dawkins expect science to answer questions of meaning and morality (pp. 178–179). Against the claim that science is true because it works, Harrison says: "The history of science is a graveyard of theories that 'worked'" (p. 181).

### 8. Harrison: The Epilogue

Harrison does not want to abolish the categories; they are too entrenched. He wants them used critically, because uncritical use produces unnecessary conflict, false tensions and false agreement (p. 184). Borrowing MacIntyre's use of *A Canticle for Leibowitz*, he suggests modern science is also a set of fragments of an older whole (pp. 185–186). Seventeenth-century natural philosophers knew of the "cataclysm", the Fall, and kept a modesty about knowledge, as when Locke judged our faculties "dull and weak". The nineteenth century lost that modesty (p. 188).

His sharpest claims come at the end. Following Stephen Gaukroger, he says Christianity shaped natural philosophy "in the image of religion" (p. 189). The neutral "Archimedean" epistemic space invented to judge between religions was inherited by science: "the epistemic imperialism of science was inherited from the supposedly neutral grounds of eighteenth-century natural theology" (p. 190). And "Modern science has thus inherited the form of the universal claims of Christianity without the supernaturalist foundation that would render them internally coherent" (p. 193). Science and religion "are not natural kinds; they are neither universal propensities of human beings nor necessary features of human societies" (p. 194). So neither can be a single cause of anything, whether of violence or of progress (p. 196). Popular atheism, he says, is "parasitic" on the modern construct "religion" (p. 197). His last target is the peacemakers. Advocates of dialogue grant science its cultural authority, treat religion as propositions and accept a neutral rational space. In doing so they are "unknowingly complicit in the perpetuation of conflict" (pp. 197–198).

### 9. Plantinga: Evolution and Unguided Evolution

Plantinga starts with the strongest case for conflict, evolution. He separates several theses: an ancient earth, progress, descent with modification, common ancestry, Darwinism (natural selection acting on random mutation), and naturalistic origins of life (pp. 8–10). Christian belief, in the sense of what the creeds share, conflicts with none of the first five. The image of God needs only that God intended rational creatures and brought them about. Charles Hodge said so in 1871: "If God made them, it makes no difference how He made them" (p. 11). Even "random" mutation is compatible with God, because in biology "random" means only that mutations do not arise because they would benefit the organism (Mayr, Sober) (pp. 11–12). "What is not consistent with Christian belief, however, is the claim that this process of evolution is unguided" (p. 12).

That claim, he argues, is not part of the science. Dawkins's *Blind Watchmaker* is subtitled "Why the evidence of evolution reveals a universe without design" (p. 14). But his argument has the form "p is not astronomically improbable; therefore p", which is like inferring a raise from the bare possibility of one (pp. 25–26). His objection that any designer must be as complex as its designs fails: we may explain a tractor by aliens without explaining the aliens, and "Explanations come to an end; for theism they come to an end in God". Besides, by Dawkins's own definition of complexity (parts unlikely to be arranged by chance), God, who has no parts, is not complex (pp. 27–29). Dennett's case rests largely on scorn, and "Argumentum ad Derisionem is hardly an approved argument form" (pp. 37–38). Plantinga concludes: "The scientific theory of evolution as such is not incompatible with Christian belief; what is incompatible with it is the idea that evolution, natural selection, is unguided. But that idea isn't part of evolutionary theory as such; it's instead a metaphysical or theological addition" (p. 63).

He takes up Kitcher's case that evolution worsens the problem of evil, with "millions of years" of suffering. His replies: "red in tooth and claw" is older than Darwin; God may have reasons we cannot see; and an *O felix culpa* theodicy holds that the best worlds include incarnation and atonement, and so include sin and suffering (pp. 55–59). On Kitcher's appeal to religious pluralism he replies that the same vertigo afflicts the sceptic, who would not be a sceptic if born elsewhere (p. 62).

### 10. Plantinga: Divine Action and the Laws of Nature

Many theologians reject miracles in the name of science. Bultmann said history is "a closed continuum of effects" and that one cannot use the wireless and believe in the New Testament world of spirits and miracles (pp. 70–71). Plantinga calls this "hands-off theology" (p. 72). His objection is that classical physics does not say this. Its conservation laws hold for **closed systems**, and the world is closed only if God is not acting in it. God could create "a full-grown horse in the middle of Times Square" without breaking the conservation of energy (pp. 78–79). The laws are best stated as conditionals: "When the universe is causally closed (when God is not acting specially in the world), P" (p. 80). On that view "it isn't even possible that God break a law of nature" (p. 83). Determinism and causal closure come not from Newton but from Laplace, who "was just assuming that God couldn't or wouldn't act specially" (p. 85). The irony is that theologians reject miracles "in the name of being scientific and up to date" while asking more of science than it says (p. 90).

Quantum mechanics makes the point stronger, because it predicts probabilities, not outcomes (pp. 92–93). Even John Earman concedes that water turning to wine is not incompatible with quantum mechanics, only very improbable (p. 95). The Divine Action Project of 1988–2003 sought "noninterventionist" divine action, and Plantinga answers its objections to intervention. To the problem of evil (why Lazarus and not Auschwitz?): "It's not as if, if he has such a reason, we'd be the first to know" (pp. 99–102). To the charge that it is "interfering": "it's really his world" (pp. 102–104). To the charge that it is inconsistent: God might be "more like a romantic artist", which would explain "a million species of beetles" (p. 107). On a collapse interpretation God could cause the outcomes of quantum collapses, guiding evolution and history without overriding the natures of things (pp. 116–117). He warns against "scientiphicalism": "calling your view 'scientific' doesn't make it so" (p. 122).

### 11. Plantinga: Superficial Conflict and Defeaters

Plantinga admits some real conflict. Some evolutionary psychology contradicts Christian belief. For Ruse and Wilson, "ethics is an illusion fobbed off on us by our own genes" (p. 134). Herbert Simon explained Mother Teresa's altruism by "docility" and "bounded rationality", an account Plantinga calls "not even close enough to be a miss" (pp. 135–136). For David Sloan Wilson, "Adaptation is the gold standard against which rationality must be judged" (p. 151). Some historical biblical criticism also contradicts it, from Strauss's "only fable" to the claim that Jesus was the first Christian atheist (pp. 157–158). He distinguishes **Troeltschian** criticism, which assumes God never acts specially, from **Duhemian** criticism, which uses only evidence that everyone accepts, so that its results are thin. Meier's imagined "unpapal conclave" of scholars locked in the Harvard Divinity School basement is his example of the latter (pp. 158–161). Other theories are compatible once their naturalist premise is removed: "Finding a 'natural' origin for religion in no way discredits it", and the "hypersensitive agency detection device" would also produce our belief in other minds (pp. 140–141).

Does this "Simonian science" defeat Christian belief? "The answer, in a word, is no" (p. 164). The key is **methodological naturalism** (MN). Judge Jones ruled in the 2005 Dover trial that intelligent design "violates the centuries-old ground rules of science by invoking and permitting supernatural causation" (p. 170). Science under MN works from an evidence base that leaves out God (weak MN) or includes his denial (strong MN) (p. 173). Under strong MN, anti-Christian conclusions are "a simple consequence of the evidence base they start with" (p. 174). Under weak MN the scientific evidence base is a subset of the Christian's. Plantinga's analogies are physicists who refuse to use memory and so produce "truncated physics" (p. 175), and a man falsely convicted of slashing tires who remembers skiing in Love Creek County Park at the time (pp. 175–176). "For the Christian, Simonian science is like truncated physics" (p. 176). The religious scientist can treat its results as reports of how things look from that evidence base, as when a receptionist says only that "the record here says it's Friday" (p. 181).

This is a version of the old question of faith and reason. Reason includes perception, memory, testimony, induction and a priori intuition; faith, for Aquinas and Calvin, is "a special gift from God", and for Calvin "a firm and certain knowledge of God's benevolence towards us" (pp. 177–178). Beliefs can still be defeated. Someone who took Psalm 104:5 to teach a stationary earth learns from the evidence that it does not teach that, and so learns "something important about the Bible ... by way of scientific investigation" (pp. 183–185). Plantinga proposes a **reduction test**: new information defeats a belief if the belief is improbable on everything else one knows, with the belief and whatever entails it removed. This is a necessary condition only, because some beliefs, like memories, carry their own warrant (pp. 186–188). His conclusion is that the conflict is real but "merely superficial, of no deep significance" (p. 188).

### 12. Plantinga: Fine-Tuning and Design Discourse

Then concord. Several physical constants must fall within narrow ranges for life to exist, and Robin Collins's cases range from one part in ten to one part in 10<sup>53</sup> (pp. 194–198). Plantinga considers four objections. The **anthropic objection** compares the argument to fishing with a net that catches only fish over ten inches. He replies that it is more like surviving a firing squad, since it reasons about this universe and does not estimate a proportion of universes (pp. 199–204). The McGrews' objection that the probabilities cannot be normalized "proves too much": it would equally rule out a message written across the night sky, "I am the Lord God, and I created the universe" (p. 207). Against the **multiverse**, he uses a poker cheat's excuse that in some universe someone deals himself four aces honestly. Even if true, this is "irrelevant" to whether this dealer is cheating (pp. 212–213). Finally there is Sober's point that we cannot assign the probabilities: likelihood versions become an "arms race", and Bayesian versions depend on a prior probability for theism that people dispute. The fine-tuning argument therefore gives "some slight support for theism". "It does, however, have the virtue of being correct" (p. 223).

Behe's biological arguments fare similarly as arguments. Paul Draper shows that irreducible complexity does not rule out indirect evolutionary routes (pp. 228–230). The probabilities are just as unknowable, since Behe's designer could be anything from "Plato's demiurge" to "space aliens from Alpha Centauri" (p. 235). Plantinga then changes the question. Perhaps design is **perceived**, not inferred, the way we believe other people have minds. Reid said: "No man thinks of asking himself what reason he has to believe that his neighbour is a living creature" (p. 240). Paley wrote that "we perceive" the watch's parts are framed for a purpose. Whewell spoke of conviction "immediately and directly" impressed on the mind (p. 244). The Duke of Argyll recalled Darwin saying design "often comes over me with overwhelming force; but at other times ... it seems to go away" (p. 245). Plantinga calls this **design discourse** as opposed to design argument (p. 246). A belief formed this way can be challenged only by defeaters, not by pointing out gaps in an argument. Darwinism gives at most a partial undercutting defeater at the level of anatomy, and none at the molecular level. Even Crick had to tell biologists "what they see was not designed, but rather evolved" (pp. 251–258). His conclusion is deliberately modest: a "wet noodle conclusion", but "my job here is to tell the sober truth" (p. 263).

### 13. Plantinga: Deep Concord

"Modern Western empirical science originated and flourished in the bosom of Christian theism and originated nowhere else" (p. 266). Plantinga's explanation is the **image of God**. God is the supreme knower, and Aquinas says the intellect is most in God's image because it can most imitate him (pp. 268–269). The medievals' *adequatio intellectus ad rem*, a fit between mind and world, is what science needs. Chomsky calls it "blind luck"; Plantinga answers that "From the point of view of theistic religion, this is not blind luck. It is only to be expected" (pp. 269–270). He lists the forms the fit takes:

| What science needs | Theistic account | Naturalism |
|---|---|---|
| A regular, law-governed world, and belief in it (Whitehead: "an instinctive conviction in the existence of an Order of Things") (p. 272) | God's providence and rationality; Aquinas's primacy of intellect over Ockham's will (pp. 272–274) | "a piece of enormous cosmic luck" (p. 283) |
| The necessity of laws, stronger than mere truth but weaker than logic | Laws are "finitely inviolable": no creature can break them, but God could have made them otherwise (pp. 280–281) | "stands mute" (p. 283) |
| Mathematics that applies to the world and that we can grasp (Wigner's "unreasonable efficacy") | Dirac: "God is a mathematician of a very high order" (pp. 284–286) | The "nerdiness factor": set theory did not help anyone on the Pleistocene savannah (pp. 286–287) |
| Numbers and sets | Divine thoughts; sets as collections need "an infinite mind—one like God's" (pp. 288–290) | Hard to place |
| Induction and simplicity | God made us inductive reasoners and the world inductively tractable; he values simplicity as we do (pp. 291–299) | No reason to expect simple theories to be true |
| An empirical method | The contingency of creation. Cotes: the laws show "not the least shadow of necessity", so must be learned "from observations and experiments" (pp. 301–302) | — |

In a note he suggests that the fertile soil was a synthesis of Ockham, for whom God freely chooses this world, and Aquinas, for whom that world is regular (p. 302, n. 53).

### 14. Plantinga: Deep Conflict, the Evolutionary Argument Against Naturalism

Naturalists "wrap themselves in the mantle of science like a politician in the flag" (p. 307). The book's last chapter argues that naturalism (N) and evolution (E) cannot both be rationally accepted. Plantinga does not say they cannot both be true. Its ancestors include C. S. Lewis's *Miracles* (n. 4). It starts from what he calls **Darwin's doubt**. Darwin wrote: "With me the horrid doubt always arises whether the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy" (p. 316). Nietzsche, Nagel, Stroud and Patricia Churchland say similar things. Churchland: a nervous system serves "feeding, fleeing, fighting and reproducing", and "Truth, whatever that is, definitely takes the hindmost" (p. 315).

The argument (pp. 317–345):

1. P(R/N&E) is low, where R is the claim that our cognitive faculties are reliable.
2. Anyone who accepts N&E and sees that P(R/N&E) is low has a defeater for R.
3. Anyone who has a defeater for R has a defeater for every other belief, including N&E itself.
4. If accepting N&E gives one a defeater for N&E, N&E is self-defeating and cannot rationally be accepted.

For premise 1, he takes naturalism to include materialism. A belief is then a neural structure with neurophysiological (NP) properties and a content. Natural selection rewards the NP properties for producing adaptive behaviour, but "natural selection just has to take potluck" with the content they happen to determine (p. 330). A frog needs accurate *indicators* to catch flies, but "indication is not belief" (pp. 327–331). With each belief as likely false as true, the chance that three-quarters of a thousand independent beliefs are true is below 10<sup>-58</sup> (p. 333). The obvious objection, that true beliefs help survival, is "true. But it is also irrelevant", because on materialism a belief causes behaviour by its NP properties, as a ball breaks a window by its mass and not by being a birthday present (pp. 335–337).

The defeater cannot itself be defeated. It is like a drug that makes 95 per cent of users unreliable: any argument for one's reliability would rely on the faculties in doubt. A visit to an "MIT cognitive-reliability laboratory" would not help either. Reid made the point: to prove reason trustworthy by reasoning is like proving a man honest by his own word (pp. 342–346). If N&E is too strong, he offers a weaker premise about metaphysical beliefs alone, since "only the occasional member of the Young Atheist's Club" has better prospects of reproducing for being a naturalist (p. 349). The verdict reverses the popular picture: "there is indeed a science/religion conflict, all right, but it is not between science and theistic religion: it is between science and naturalism. That's where the conflict really lies" (p. 350).

### 15. Comparing the Two

The books overlap more than their methods suggest. Both cite Samuel Clarke's line that the "course of nature" is the will of God acting in "a continued, regular, constant, and uniform manner" (Harrison p. 80; Plantinga p. 274). Harrison reads it as the moment efficient causation became a "zero-sum game" between God and nature. Plantinga reads it as theism's support for the regularity science needs. Both discuss David Sloan Wilson's *Darwin's Cathedral*. Harrison treats its use of catechisms as "measurable" data as a symptom of religion's reduction to propositions (pp. 83–84). Plantinga treats its functional account of Calvinism as one theory among others, superficially in conflict with Christian belief (pp. 145–152). Both reject White's account of Galileo (Harrison pp. 172–173; Plantinga pp. 6–7), and both see Dawkins and his allies offering science as a substitute worldview: for Harrison a successor to natural theology's moral role (p. 180), for Plantinga a "quasi-religion" (p. 311).

| | Harrison | Plantinga |
|---|---|---|
| Discipline | History of ideas, conceptual history | Analytic epistemology and philosophy of religion |
| The categories | Modern constructs, not natural kinds | Accepted: "theistic religion", "naturalism", "science" |
| Where conflict lies | In the boundary-drawing of the nineteenth century and its myths | Between science and naturalism |
| Natural theology | A seventeenth-century novelty that "gave hostages to fortune" | Fine-tuning gives "slight support"; design is better seen as perception |
| Why science arose in Christian Europe | Christianity reshaped natural philosophy "in the image of religion", including its universal claims | The image of God makes the world knowable to us |
| Human faculties | Seventeenth-century thinkers stressed fallen, "dull and weak" faculties | The imago dei and *adequatio*; naturalism cannot trust our faculties |
| Faith | Once trust and a disposition, later made into "assent upon evidence" | A source of knowledge alongside reason, which need not be propositionally proved |

Two tensions stand out. First, Plantinga's "Christian evidence base", a set of propositions from which probabilities are reckoned, is exactly the kind of propositional religion whose rise Harrison traces. And his concord chapter could look like a repeat of the Boyle lecturers' strategy. Harrison would say that tying God to the success of science gives "hostages to fortune" again. Second, Plantinga avoids the charge that Harrison brings against dialogue advocates. He rejects the "Archimedean" neutral space: on his account, science under methodological naturalism is a deliberately truncated perspective, not the neutral judge of all claims. His faith is a gift and his design beliefs are perceptions, not conclusions of evidential arguments. In that respect he is closer to the older modes of knowing Harrison describes than to the eighteenth-century evidentialism he criticizes. He also asks whether Christians should practise a science not limited by methodological naturalism (p. 189). That is the kind of question that only arises once Harrison's neutral ground has been abandoned.

They also differ in what they want. Harrison wants the categories to be used with historical awareness, and he thinks some genuine tensions are hidden by the false ones. Plantinga wants to show that a Christian can accept all of current science, including evolution, without loss of rationality, and that the naturalist cannot.

### Conclusion

Harrison shows that the war between science and religion is a story told in the nineteenth century about categories formed in the seventeenth. For Aquinas, *scientia* and *religio* were virtues of one soul and could not compete. The Reformation, the new experimental philosophy and the wars of the state turned both into bodies of propositions, and the conflict myth then drew a border between them. Plantinga, working in the modern terms, finds that the alleged conflicts with evolution and with divine action disappear once the naturalist add-ons are removed. The real conflicts in evolutionary psychology and biblical criticism come from a truncated evidence base. Fine-tuning and design support theism modestly, the image of God explains why science is possible, and naturalism combined with evolution undermines its own rationality. Read together, the historian explains why we ask whether science and religion conflict, and the philosopher answers that the deeper conflict is between science and naturalism.
