Between September 1787 and the summer of 1788 the American states argued over whether to ratify the Constitution drafted at Philadelphia. Those who favoured it took the name Federalists; those who opposed it "for lack of a better term, came to be known as the Anti-Federalists" (Carey and McClellan, p. xli). The best-known product of the argument is *The Federalist*, eighty-five essays published in New York newspapers under the name Publius by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay. On the editors' count Hamilton wrote fifty-one, Madison twenty-nine and Jay five (p. xliv). The other side has no single book. Its writings were scattered pamphlets, newspaper letters and convention speeches. Herbert Storing collected them in seven volumes as *The Complete Anti-Federalist* (1981), and the essay he wrote to introduce them, *What the Anti-Federalists Were For*, is still the main account of their thought.

This note reads the debate from both sides. It uses Storing's essay and Murray Dry's one-volume abridgment of the Anti-Federalist writings; four central papers of *The Federalist* (10, 39, 51 and 78) in the Liberty Fund edition; and five pieces from the *Cambridge Companion to The Federalist*.

### 1. Why the losers matter

Storing begins from a claim about the founding itself. The founding generation saw the Constitution as unusual because it came out of deliberation: it was drafted by a large body over three months and then "widely, fully, and vigorously debated in the country at large." On that view the opponents played "an indispensable if subordinate part." The Constitution that came out of the debate was not the one that went in, since it was accepted on the understanding that a bill of rights would follow. And the debate did not end in 1788: "The political life of the community continues to be a dialogue, in which the Anti-Federalist concerns and principles still play an important part" (Storing, p. 3). The Anti-Federalists are therefore "entitled ... to be counted among the Founding Fathers, in what is admittedly a somewhat paradoxical sense" (p. 3).

They have seldom been treated that way. The older picture made them narrow local politicians who could not see past their own states. Charles Beard's economic reading of the Constitution made them more sympathetic, as democratic farmers set against commercial oligarchs. But Storing finds that "the corrected Beardian eye betrays still its original squint" (p. 3). Cecelia Kenyon called them "men of little faith." Gordon Wood turned a debate that was for them "fundamentally political" into one that was "fundamentally sociological" (p. 4). Storing proposes to read them from the inside, "seeing the questions as they saw them, following the arguments as they made them" (p. 4).

Two further points govern his reading. First, the Anti-Federalists did not agree among themselves. "It would be difficult to find a single point about which all of the Anti-Federalists agreed," and some men, such as Edmund Randolph, were on both sides at different times (p. 5). Storing therefore looks for what is fundamental rather than what is typical. On that test a few writers matter most: the Federal Farmer, Brutus, the [Maryland] Farmer and the Impartial Examiner (p. 6). Second, the two sides shared more than they disputed. Both held that "the purpose of government is the regulation and thereby the protection of individual rights" and that the means is limited republican government. Their quarrels were "differences within the family." Storing sums this up: "The nation was born in consensus but it lives in controversy" (pp. 5–6).

The Federalists' modern readers have also come to doubt how much the essays decided. The Liberty Fund editors point out that *The Federalist* "had virtually no effect on the ratification," since nine states had already ratified when New York and Virginia did (p. xlvi). They call it "forthrightly a campaign tract" (p. l). They also quote Douglas Adair: it was "special pleading designed to secure ratification," and both Hamilton and Madison "lived to regret theories and interpretations they had advanced" under the name of Publius (p. liii). David Siemers, opening the *Companion*, notes that *The Federalist* owes its existence to dissent. Centinel began publishing on 5 October 1787, Brutus about two weeks later, and Federalist 10 appeared four days after Madison had told Washington of "a spirit of criticism" in the air (Siemers, *Companion* ch. 1).

### 2. Who had the right to the name "federal"

The two names were part of the fight. In 1787 "federal" meant a league of sovereign states, which is what the Articles of Confederation set up. Those who wanted to keep that principle could claim to be the real federalists. Storing shows that the word was ambiguous. It could mean support for the federation's existing institutions, or the principle of a league of states. On the second meaning the Constitution's opponents had the better claim. On the first, "the Federalists had a legitimate claim to their name" (Storing, pp. 9–10).

Melancton Smith made the point with a joke in the New York ratifying convention. Hamilton had just shown that the Constitution aimed at "a reduction of all the states into a consolidated government." Smith hoped Hamilton would "be complaisant enough to exchange names with those who disliked the Constitution," since by Hamilton's own concession the opponents were the Federalists and its advocates the Anti-Federalists (AF, p. 334). Michael Zuckert argues that the names were less unfair than Smith made out. A federal system has two parts, the states and the federal head, and the Constitution's supporters strengthened the federal head (Zuckert, *Companion* ch. 5). Mansfield's version is blunter: "The Federalist steals its name from the traditional confederal league of republics and applies it to a novel form" (Mansfield, *Companion* ch. 16).

### 3. Consolidation

The first and most common Anti-Federalist charge was that the Constitution would consolidate the states into one government. Brutus put it most carefully in his first essay. The plan "does not go to a perfect and entire consolidation, yet it approaches so near to it, that it must, if executed, certainly and infallibly terminate in it" (AF, p. 110). The reason is in the necessary and proper clause and the supremacy clause. Together they mean that "so far therefore as its powers reach, all ideas of confederation are given up and lost" (p. 110). The taxing power is unlimited in amount and in mode. It "connects with it almost all other powers," and it is "the great mean of protection, security, and defence, in a good government, and the great engine of oppression and tyranny in a bad one" (p. 111). The federal courts "will swallow up all the powers of the courts in the respective states" (p. 112). Brutus does not say Congress will at once abolish the states. He says that the states' remaining powers will be "a clog upon the wheels" of the general government, which will be "naturally inclined to remove it out of the way." For "every man, and every body of men, invested with power, are ever disposed to increase it" (pp. 112–13).

Centinel, the first major Anti-Federalist writer, who was probably Samuel Bryan of Philadelphia, argued the same way. The taxing, legislative and judicial powers "must in their operation, necessarily absorb the state legislatures and judicatories." He thought the framers had left the state governments standing only "as a blind" (AF, pp. 17–18). Patrick Henry, in the Virginia convention, went straight to the preamble: "who authorised them to speak the language of, We, the People, instead of We, the States? States are the characteristics, and the soul of a confederation" (AF, p. 297).

Madison's reply is Federalist 39. He tests the Constitution by the opponents' own standard, the difference between a federal and a national government, and applies it to five aspects of the plan. The act of ratification is federal, since it is the assent of "the people ... as composing the distinct and independent states." The House is national; the Senate is federal; the executive comes from "a very compound source." The operation of the government on individuals is national. Its extent is federal, since it leaves the states "a residuary and inviolable sovereignty over all other objects." Amendment is "neither wholly national, nor wholly federal" (Fed. 39, pp. 196–99). His conclusion: the Constitution "is, in strictness, neither a national nor a federal constitution; but a composition of both" (p. 199).

Storing points out that the opponents could not accept this. To them it was "a solecism in politics for two coordinate sovereignties to exist together." Luther Martin called the plan "just so much federal in appearance" as would win the support of those who wanted a federal government (Storing, p. 12).

### 4. The small republic

Behind the consolidation charge stood a claim from political theory: a free republic can exist only over a small territory. Brutus quotes Montesquieu: "It is natural to a republic to have only a small territory, otherwise it cannot long subsist." In a large republic, "the public good is sacrificed to a thousand views" (AF, p. 113). History agrees with him, since Greece and Rome were free while small and became tyrannies when they conquered. So does reason. In a free republic the people consent to laws through representatives, and in a country as large as the United States a representation that really knew the people's minds would be too numerous to do business (pp. 113–14). Brutus also states the condition of homogeneity: "In a republic, the manners, sentiments, and interests of the people should be similar" (p. 114). The American states differed in climate, produce, manners and law. A large republic would also lose the people's confidence. The people of Georgia and New Hampshire "would not know one another's mind," would distrust a distant legislature and would not support its laws. The government would then have to execute them "at the point of the bayonet—a government of all others the most to be dreaded" (p. 116).

Storing finds three concerns in the small-republic argument: voluntary attachment, responsibility of rulers, and the forming of citizens (Storing, p. 16). The states were "the natural homes of individual liberty" (p. 15). A government that the people know and trust can be obeyed without force; a distant one cannot, which is why the Anti-Federalists kept returning to standing armies. And a small republic is "a school of citizenship." It can attend to education and religion, and make government less necessary by rendering "the people more capable of being a Law to themselves" (pp. 21–23).

Agrippa, probably James Winthrop of Massachusetts, put a commercial twist on the argument. Carthage, the "great commercial republick of antiquity," kept her freedom longer than Rome, which proved that "the spirit of commerce is the great bond of union among citizens" (AF, p. 230). The idea of an "uncompounded republick, on an average, one thousand miles in length," and holding six million people "all reduced to the same standard of morals, or habits, and of laws, is in itself an absurdity, and contrary to the whole experience of mankind" (p. 236). Storing notes how much this asks. Agrippa wants union by commerce in place of union by power, and that depends on everyone recognizing their interest voluntarily (Storing, pp. 24–25).

### 5. Madison's answer: faction and the extended sphere

Federalist 10 turns the small-republic argument upside down. Its starting point is the old complaint against popular government. "The instability, injustice, and confusion, introduced into the public councils, have, in truth, been the mortal diseases under which popular governments have every where perished" (Fed. 10, p. 42). Madison defines a faction as a group, "whether amounting to a majority or minority of the whole," united "by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community" (p. 43).

One could cure faction by removing its causes, but only in two ways. Destroying liberty is worse than the disease: "Liberty is to faction, what air is to fire." Giving everyone the same opinions is impossible while reason is fallible and tied to self-love, and while men have unequal faculties of acquiring property (p. 43). "The latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man" (pp. 43–44). Their "most common and durable source" is "the various and unequal distribution of property." The regulation of the resulting interests, landed, manufacturing, mercantile and monied, "forms the principal task of modern legislation" (p. 44). But "no man is allowed to be a judge in his own cause," and legislation on debts or tariffs makes every legislator a party in his own cause (p. 44). And "enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm" (p. 45).

So relief must come from controlling the effects. A minority faction is defeated by the vote. The real danger is a majority faction, and how to check it while keeping popular government is "the great desideratum" (p. 45). Moral and religious motives will not do it; they lose their force "in proportion to the number combined together" (p. 46). A pure democracy has no cure, and such democracies "have been as short in their lives, as they have been violent in their deaths" (p. 46). A republic, by which Madison means "a government in which the scheme of representation takes place," offers two remedies. Representation may "refine and enlarge the public views," and a larger territory makes a majority faction less likely to form and harder to organize: "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests" (pp. 46–48). A "rage for paper money, for an abolition of debts, for an equal division of property" might seize one state but not the Union (p. 48). The essay ends: "In the extent and proper structure of the union, therefore, we behold a republican remedy for the diseases most incident to republican government" (p. 48).

Alan Gibson's chapter is a close reading of the essay. He argues that the "great desideratum" has two parts. It is to control majority factions without breaking the republican principle, and also to reconcile impartial government with popular control. He thinks Madison's definition of faction is normative: there are no benign factions, and "the permanent and aggregate interests of the community" assumes a real common good. This tells against pluralist readings like Robert Dahl's (Gibson, *Companion* ch. 8). Madison's first plan at the Convention was stronger than Federalist 10. It joined the extended republic to a congressional veto over all state laws, which would make Congress a "disinterested & dispassionate umpire." The veto was lost, and the essay relies on what remained. Gibson's main claim is that the large electoral districts, though much quoted, are secondary. In a preliminary version of the argument Madison himself called them an "auxiliary desideratum." The main mechanism is the extent of territory itself, through distance, number, variety of interests and moral restraint: "where there is a consciousness of unjust or dishonourable purposes, communication is always checked by distrust in proportion to the number whose concurrence is necessary" (Fed. 10, p. 48). His conclusion is that in Madison's scheme the extent of territory does the job that the monarch or the aristocracy did in mixed government. It supplies impartiality without a sovereign above the people.

Federalist 51 restates the point at the end of the series on separation of powers. There are two ways to protect a minority: "creating a will in the community independent of the majority," as hereditary governments do, or "comprehending in the society so many separate descriptions of citizens, as will render an unjust combination of a majority of the whole very improbable." In the extended republic "a coalition of a majority of the whole society could seldom take place upon any other principles, than those of justice and the general good" (Fed. 51, pp. 270–71). Madison then reverses Montesquieu: "the larger the society, provided it lie within a practicable sphere, the more duly capable it will be of self-government" (p. 272).

How much did the theory matter at the time? The *Companion* editors call Federalist 10 and 51 the modern "ur-texts" of American government (Rakove and Sheehan, Introduction). Siemers quotes Larry Kramer: "It took twentieth-century scholarship to rescue Madison's essay from obscurity." Treanor quotes him on the same point: "[t]he theory of the extended republic ... played essentially no role in shaping the Constitution or its aftermath" (Siemers, ch. 1; Treanor, ch. 14).

### 6. Representation: likeness or refinement

On representation the two sides held different ideas of what a representative is for. The Federal Farmer wrote that the "essential parts of a free and good government are a full and equal representation of the people in the legislature, and the jury trial of the vicinage." A full and equal representation is one "which possesses the same interests, feelings, opinions, and views the people themselves would were they all assembled" (AF, p. 39). Every order of men, "professional men, merchants, traders, farmers, mechanics," should be able to send its best informed men, and so the body must be "considerably numerous" (p. 39). Brutus went further. Representatives "should bear the strongest resemblance of those in whose room they are substituted." For an assembly to be "a true likeness of the people," it must be numerous, and "sixty-five men cannot be found in the United States, who hold the sentiments, possess the feelings, or are acqainted with the wants and interests of this vast country" (AF, p. 125).

Melancton Smith gave the fullest statement. "The idea that naturally suggests itself to our minds, when we speak of representatives is, that they resemble those they represent; they should be a true picture of the people." They need political and commercial learning, but also the knowledge of ordinary life that "men of the middling class of life are in general much better competent to" (AF, p. 340). He proposed one representative for every twenty thousand inhabitants until the House reached three hundred (p. 338). He also objected to the three-fifths clause on principle: representation means every free agent shares in governing himself, and "slaves have no will of their own" (p. 336).

Publius took the opposite view. In Federalist 10, representation passes the public views "through the medium of a chosen body of citizens, whose wisdom may best discern the true interest of their country," and the public voice so pronounced may be "more consonant to the public good, than if pronounced by the people themselves" (Fed. 10, p. 46). Storing quotes Federalist 63's description of American government as "the total exclusion of the people in the collective capacity," and Fisher Ames's remark that "the representation of the people is something more than the people" (Storing, pp. 43, 45). Smith's theoretical aim was likeness. His practical aim was narrower: a House that, alongside the natural aristocracy that would always fill much of it, included a due share of the middling class (Storing's introduction, AF, p. 331).

### 7. The natural aristocracy

The phrase "natural aristocracy" was used on both sides. Smith did not deny that such a class existed; he insisted on it. "It is true it is our singular felicity that we have no legal or hereditary distinctions of this kind; but still there are real differences." Birth, education, talents and wealth "create distinctions among men as visible and of as much influence as titles, stars and garters" (AF, pp. 340–41). In a small House chosen from large districts, these men will win: "The great easily form associations; the poor and middling class form them with difficulty," and only a popular demagogue could unite the common people against them (p. 341). And the great "possess the same feelings, and are under the influence of the same motives, as an hereditary nobility." By contrast, "the substantial yeomanry of the country are more temperate, of better morals and less ambition than the great" (p. 341).

Smith did not want the first class shut out. "They would be more dangerous out of power than in it." He wanted enough of the middling class admitted "to controul them" (p. 342). Brutus agreed that "the natural aristocracy of the country will be elected" because "wealth always creates influence" (AF, p. 125). The Federal Farmer counted the natural aristocracy at "about four or five thousand men" and set it apart from the "aristocratic faction," a junto of unprincipled men (AF, p. 77).

The Federalists could accept the term and give it a different value. James Wilson defined the natural aristocracy as those "most noted for wisdom and virtue," and Federalist 57 said the aim of every constitution is "to obtain for rulers men who possess most wisdom to discern, and most virtue to pursue, the common good" (Storing, pp. 44–45). Mansfield thinks this was deliberate. Publius makes room in a "wholly popular" government "for the ambitious few," and shows "respect for the fact of their existence despite the supposed equality of men by nature" (Mansfield, ch. 16).

### 8. Simple government against complex government

Centinel attacked the theory of balance directly, in John Adams's *Defence of the Constitutions*. Adams held that rulers always act from private interest and ambition, so the only security is to set three bodies against each other. Centinel answered that such balanced orders had never existed, and that even if they could be made, "if the administrators of every government are actuated by views of private interest and ambition, how is the welfare and happiness of the community to be the result of such jarring adverse interests?" (AF, p. 15). His alternative was responsibility. The best form for free men is the one "which holds those entrusted with power, in the greatest responsibility to their constitutents." Responsibility requires simplicity. In a complicated government the people "will be perplexed and divided in their sentiments about the source of abuses," some blaming the Senate and some the House. Centinel recommended the Pennsylvania model: a single legislature, elected for a short term, with rotation (p. 16). Storing quotes the [Maryland] Farmer to the same effect: complex governments "bid defiance to all responsibility" (Storing, p. 56).

Storing places this inside an old argument. Adams's balance is the mixed regime of the one, the few and the many. The Anti-Federalist alternative is simple, responsible government. The Constitution looked to its critics like "an imitation of the British government, built without the essential British materials" (Storing, p. 58). Henry put it this way: "tell me not of checks on paper, but tell me of checks founded on self-love" (p. 58). Wilson's answer was that the Constitution is "purely democratical" in principle, applied in different forms. Storing calls the result a mixed government made entirely of popular elements, "a mere constitutional imitation of mixed government" (pp. 62–63).

Federalist 51 gives the Federalist case. Paper barriers do not hold, so each department needs "the necessary constitutional means, and personal motives, to resist encroachments of the others." "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man, must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place" (Fed. 51, p. 268). Then come the famous sentences: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary." The task is to "enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself" (p. 269). Dependence on the people is the primary control, "but experience has taught mankind the necessity of auxiliary precautions" (p. 269). Because "in republican government, the legislative authority necessarily predominates," it is divided into two branches, and the weaker executive is fortified (p. 269).

Mansfield reads this as a deliberate use of ambition. Publius uses "the class of the ambitious with their love of power" to check itself, and "unlike simplistic republicans and the Anti-Federalists, Publius does not frown upon ambition" (Mansfield, ch. 16). He also notes that the Anti-Federalists made "two contradictory criticisms of the Constitution: that it was too complex and too consolidated." In Publius's view both were virtues.

### 9. Energy and unlimited powers

The Anti-Federalists accepted much of the Federalist account of the crisis. They agreed that the Articles were inadequate, though some blamed luxury and bad manners more than the form of government (Storing, pp. 25–28). Their quarrel was with the powers granted, above all over the purse and the sword. Here Storing thinks the Federalists had their strongest argument. Publius held in Federalist 23 that "the means ought to be proportioned to the end," and that since the dangers to the Union cannot be foreseen, "there can be no limitation of that authority" which provides for them (Zuckert, ch. 5). "The contingencies of society are not reducible to calculations." Storing calls this argument "almost sufficient to justify the Constitution" (Storing, p. 29).

The Anti-Federalists replied in two ways. First, they said, grant power cautiously. "There is no public abuse that does not spring from the necessary use of power," wrote the [Maryland] Farmer, and "all governments find a use for as much money as they can raise" (Storing, p. 30). Second, they asked what the power was for. Henry: "You are not to inquire how your trade may be increased, nor how you are to become a great and powerful people, but how your liberties can be secured; for liberty ought to be the direct end of your Government" (Storing, p. 31). Brutus offered a concrete limit on armies. He proposed a clause forbidding a standing army in peacetime except garrisons and arsenal guards, unless an attack threatened, and requiring two-thirds of both houses for any peacetime troops (AF, p. 161).

Mansfield gives "energy" a history. It is a term from physics and entered political vocabulary with *The Federalist*. "Energy is not virtue; it is neutral as to its good or bad use." It allows a republic to take from monarchy "a neutral version of its virtue" without the name (Mansfield, ch. 16).

### 10. The new federalism

Zuckert argues that Publius invented a new kind of federal system. The defect of the Articles, as Federalist 15 says, was "LEGISLATION for STATES or GOVERNMENTS, in their CORPORATE or COLLECTIVE CAPACITIES," as opposed to legislation for individuals. Laws without sanctions are mere advice. If a government can coerce only states, then "every breach of the laws must involve a state of war." The new government acts on individuals through courts and magistrates instead (Zuckert, ch. 5). Martin Diamond held that the founders knew only two forms, confederal and national, and that Federalist 39 describes a mixture. Zuckert thinks what they saw as a mixture we can see as a third form. He cites Madison's late "Sketch Never Finished," which calls the Union "a system without a example ancient or modern," combining "a federal form with the forms of individual Republics."

Storing finds an irony on the Anti-Federalist side. Many of them came to defend this new federalism, a government "in which neither the whole nor the parts are supreme," more firmly than its authors (Storing, pp. 32–33). The trouble was that the new federalism kept dissolving into "the stark alternative of the old federalism versus consolidation." The Anti-Federalists' "commitment to the new federalism, whose very principle is equivocation, was greater" (p. 37). Their remedies show the difficulty. They proposed an express reservation of state rights, state control of the militia, and requisitions with federal direct taxes only on states that refused to pay. Henry turned Randolph's description of taxation as the "soul" of government against him: "they shall not have the soul of Virginia" (pp. 35–36).

### 11. Brutus and Hamilton on the judiciary

The judiciary debate is the closest exchange between the two sides. Storing calls Brutus's discussion "the best in the Anti-Federalist literature," and his essays "the most direct Anti-Federal confrontation of the arguments of The Federalist" (AF, p. 103). Hamilton's essays on the judiciary, Federalist 78–83, appeared in late May 1788. William Treanor thinks they had little effect on ratification but were "a stunning intellectual achievement" (Treanor, ch. 14).

Brutus said he had "not met with any writer, who has discussed the judicial powers with any degree of accuracy" (AF, p. 163). The federal judges would be "totally independent, both of the people and the legislature." The clause extending the judicial power to cases "in law and equity" under the Constitution would let them explain it "according to the reasoning spirit of it, without being confined to the words or letter" (pp. 163–64). From the Supreme Court "there is no appeal." Its decisions would work "in the most certain, but yet silent and imperceptible manner" toward "an entire subversion of the legislative, executive and judicial powers of the individual states" (p. 165). The court would lean toward the general government for three reasons: the Constitution's broad terms invite it, the judges have an interest in their own power, and there is precedent in the English Court of Exchequer, which had stretched its jurisdiction by legal fiction (pp. 165–67). "This power in the judicial, will enable them to mould the government, into almost any shape they please" (p. 167).

Brutus XV draws the conclusion. "I question whether the world ever saw, in any period of it, a court of justice invested with such immense powers, and yet placed in a situation so little responsible" (AF, p. 183). English judges hold office during good behaviour, but the House of Lords corrects their errors and Parliament can overrule them by statute. American judges would answer to no one: "they are independent of the people, of the legislature, and of every power under heaven. Men placed in this situation will generally soon feel themselves independent of heaven itself" (p. 183). Good-behaviour tenure made sense against a hereditary king, but America has none (p. 184). Constitutional questions would arise in suits between individuals and set precedents before the public noticed, so the states could be absorbed "gradually, and by insensible degrees" (p. 186). Brutus wanted the legislature to decide the meaning of the Constitution, since legislators "would have explained it at their peril" and could be removed at the next election. "A constitution is a compact of a people with their rulers." If it is construed by men who are not accountable, "no way is left to controul them but with a high hand and an outstretched arm" (p. 187).

Hamilton's Federalist 78 starts from the opposite estimate. The judiciary "will always be the least dangerous to the political rights of the constitution." The executive "holds the sword," the legislature "commands the purse," and the judiciary "may truly be said to have neither Force nor Will, but merely judgment" (Fed. 78, p. 402). Permanent tenure is therefore "the citadel of the public justice and the public security" (p. 403). In a limited constitution the courts must "declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the constitution void," or "all the reservations of particular rights or privileges would amount to nothing" (p. 403). This does not make judges superior to legislators. "To deny this would be to affirm that the deputy is greater than his principal." The courts are "an intermediate body between the people and the legislature," and when the two laws conflict they must prefer "the intention of the people to the intention of their agents." This "only supposes that the power of the people is superior to both" (pp. 403–04). Hamilton grants that judges might "exercise will instead of judgment," but says the objection would prove there should be no judges at all (p. 405). Independence also guards against "ill humours" in the people that lead to "serious oppressions of the minor party," and it would take "an uncommon portion of fortitude in the judges" to resist laws backed by the majority (pp. 405–06).

Treanor reads the exchange as old against new. Brutus trusted juries and legislatures and was suspicious of judges; Hamilton respected the people's constitution-making and the expertise of judges, and was wary of juries and passing majorities. Treanor reports that most scholars now take Brutus to have been Melancton Smith. Storing was less sure: the usual attribution to Robert Yates was "somewhat questionable," and he found no evidence either way (AF, p. 103). He also traces the background. Before 1787 judges had commonly been treated as part of the executive. Hamilton had already argued a form of judicial review in Rutgers v. Waddington (1784), and Smith had attacked that decision for transferring the people's power to "judges who are independent of the people." Treanor reads "tenor" in Federalist 78 as "general drift," a structural standard that is neither natural law nor limited to clear violations. He ends with Kramer's line: "Hamilton was the law's Mozart" (Treanor, ch. 14).

### 12. The jury and the bill of rights

The Anti-Federalists cared about two rights more than any others: trial by jury and liberty of the press. Centinel noted that the Constitution guaranteed a jury in criminal cases but not civil ones, and gave the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction "both as to law and fact," which seemed to permit reviewing a jury's verdict (AF, p. 20). The Federal Farmer counted "the jury trial of the vicinage" as one of the two essential parts of free government (p. 39). Storing explains why. The jury is popular control of government at the point of administration, and the [Maryland] Farmer thought it more important than representation in the legislature (Storing, pp. 18–19). Hamilton's reply in Federalist 83, his longest essay, admits that the Anti-Federalists treat the jury "as the very palladium of free government." He replies that no general rule is possible and that "the establishment of the trial by jury in all cases would have been an unpardonable error" (Treanor, ch. 14).

On a bill of rights Storing sees a paradox. "In one sense, the success of the Bill of Rights reflects the failure of the Anti-Federalists," because "a truly federal government needs no bill of rights" (Storing, p. 65). Wilson argued that under enumerated powers "everything which is not given is reserved," so a bill of rights was superfluous and even dangerous (p. 65). The Anti-Federalists answered that implied powers and the taxing and commerce powers could reach the press. They also pointed out that the Constitution already contained a partial bill of rights, such as habeas corpus and the ban on titles of nobility, which undercut Wilson's argument (p. 66). Samuel Chase warned that "a declaration of rights alone will be of no essential service. Some of the powers must be abridged" (p. 67).

The Anti-Federalists' best argument concerned education. A bill of rights teaches the people. The Federal Farmer wrote that by declaring rights "we give existence, or at least establish in the minds of the people truths and principles which they might never otherwise have thought of, or soon forgot." He wanted them printed "in the front page of every family book" (Storing, p. 70). The Federal Farmer also said a complete federal bill of rights was "very practicable." If the states really could not agree on rights as basic as the civil jury, that was "a very strong argument against their attempting to form one entire society" (AF, pp. 40–41).

### 13. Virtue, commerce and greatness

Storing finds the deepest disagreement over civic virtue. The Anti-Federalists saw "civil society as a teacher, as a molder of character, rather than as a regulator of conduct" (Storing, p. 47). Centinel held that "a republican, or free government, can only exist where the body of the people are virtuous, and where property is pretty equally divided" (AF, p. 16). Smith warned that the government shapes the people as much as the people shape the government: "Government operates upon the spirit of the people, as well as the spirit of the people operates upon it" (AF, p. 343). The Federalists relied on interest instead. Noah Webster wrote that virtue "never was and never will be, till mens' natures are changed, a fixed, permanent principle and support of government," and Madison's rule was "Divide et impera" (Storing, pp. 46–47).

Storing does not let the Anti-Federalists off easily. They were not old-fashioned agrarians but "children of the modern commercial world who worried about its implications" (Storing, p. 46). And the Federalists did not do without virtue entirely. Madison said that "to suppose that any form of government will secure liberty or happiness without any virtue in the people is a chimerical idea." Federalist 55 says that republican government presupposes good qualities "in a higher degree than any other form" (pp. 72–73). The Federalists, on Storing's account, took virtue for granted. The Anti-Federalists asked whether the regime would "release and foster a certain type of human being who will be likely to destroy that very regime" (p. 73).

Henry tied this to national ambition. "If we admit this Consolidated Government it will be because we like a great splendid one. Some way or other we must be a great and mighty empire; we must have an army, and a navy, and a number of things: When the American spirit was in its youth, the language of America was different: Liberty, Sir, was then the primary object" (AF, p. 306). Britain is great "not because their Government is strong and energetic; but ... because liberty is its direct end and foundation." Now "the American spirit, assisted by the ropes and chains of consolidation, is about to convert this country to a powerful and mighty empire" (p. 306). He also said the Constitution "squints towards monarchy": "Your President may easily become King" (p. 310).

Mansfield accepts that Publius wanted greatness and thinks this was right. A republic large enough to "aim for greatness" gives the ambitious a worthy object. The presidency is the first strong executive that is part of a republic rather than an exception to it, as the Roman dictator, the Venetian doge and the Cromwellian Protector were. Publius appeals to "the love of fame, the ruling passion of the noblest minds" (Mansfield, ch. 16).

### 14. Publius as a partisan

Siemers argues that *The Federalist* was "a devoted partisan document with a tendency to vilify opponents, to shade and to spin as much as to inform." The opponents are "bigoted idolizers of state authority" and "distempered enthusiasts," and they show "a rage for objection, which disorders their imaginations and judgments." Federalist 29 compares their warnings about the militia to Milton's "Gorgons, hydras, and chimeras dire." Madison was more measured but still wrote of "the incoherent dreams of a delirious jealousy." The editors of the *Documentary History of the Ratification* judge that Jay was "deliberately misrepresenting" his opponents when he said they wanted three or four separate confederacies. The French chargé d'affaires Louis-Guillaume Otto thought the book "is not at all useful to educated men and it is too scholarly and too long for the ignorant." Centinel's verdict was that Publius, "mistaking sound for argument, has with Herculean labour accumulated myriads of unmeaning sentences" (Siemers, ch. 1).

The Anti-Federalists had their own rhetoric. Centinel suggested that Washington's "unsuspecting goodness and zeal" and Franklin's "weakness and indecision attendant on old age" had been exploited by the framers (AF, pp. 14–15). The Federal Farmer was more even-handed. He granted the need for a federal government of some sort and called the plan "a plan of accommodation" (AF, pp. 33, 37). Smith asked the convention to leave out "declamation and appeals to the passions" (AF, p. 333).

Siemers's judgment is that "one cannot clearly conclude that the Federalists won the ratification debate." Americans "were not willing to place full faith in the Federalists' theories about government. And they were wise not to do so." He thinks "it was the struggle between the Federalists and Anti-Federalists that created a nation. This dialogue produced a better government than what would have been created by either group alone" (Siemers, ch. 1).

### 15. What each side got right

Storing's conclusion is that the Anti-Federalists "lost the debate ... because they had the weaker argument. They were, as Publius said, trying to reconcile contradictions" (Storing, p. 71). They wanted union and the states, the great republic and the small community, commerce and civic virtue (p. 6). The Federalist system was built on "the reliable inclination of men to follow their own interests, fairly narrowly understood," and it has been "remarkably, if not gloriously, successful" (p. 72). But Storing holds that "it is in the dialogue, not merely in the Federalist victory, that the country's principles are to be discovered" (p. 72). He ends with Lincoln's Lyceum address of 1838, which saw that the founders' passions were fading. Lincoln's answer, on Storing's account, required "a synthesis of the Federalist solution and the Anti-Federalist reservation": the American polity "had to be a moral community if it was to be anything" (p. 76).

Siemers lists where each side was proved right. The executive has gained power, as some Anti-Federalists feared; the nation has gained on the states; and studies showing that policy follows the preferences of the wealthy describe "the precise fear articulated by many Anti-Federalists." The Federalists were right that the Union needed real powers, but their confidence in their own political science was not borne out by experience. Publius tried to refine public opinion and found himself swept along by it. Madison and Hamilton soon became party leaders themselves (Siemers, ch. 1).

Mansfield turns this round. Publius "did not conquer his adversary, the old republicanism of responsive government," and the "constitutional distance" between government and people that he designed has been worn down by parties, popular election of the president and polling. "Yet if Publius did not conquer his adversary, he won the battle he entered when the Constitution was ratified" (Mansfield, ch. 16).

### Conclusion

The Federalists won the vote and the Anti-Federalists won several of the arguments. The Constitution was ratified, and *The Federalist* became the standard commentary on it. But a bill of rights was added at once, and the fears the Anti-Federalists named have been the subjects of American political argument ever since: consolidation, a distant elite, an unchecked court, and a citizenry that no longer governs itself. Brutus foresaw a Supreme Court that would expand federal power through a series of private suits, and that is roughly what happened. Madison's extended republic did not stop majorities from forming, as the rise of parties showed within a decade. The best of both sides, Madison and Hamilton, Brutus, the Federal Farmer and Smith, were arguing inside the same tradition and largely in the same terms. That shared vocabulary is why Storing could call the losers founders.
