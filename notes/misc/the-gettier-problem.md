The Gettier Problem is a philosophical challenge to the traditional definition of knowledge as "justified true belief" (JTB). It shows that having a belief that is both true and justified doesn’t necessarily mean it qualifies as knowledge.

In the traditional view, to "know" something, three conditions must be met:

- Belief: You must believe the proposition.
- Truth: The proposition must be true.
- Justification: You must have a good reason (justification) for believing it to be true.

Gettier's Argument: Edmund Gettier (1963) presented cases where all three conditions are satisfied, but the belief still doesn’t seem to be genuine knowledge because it is based on luck or coincidence. These are now called Gettier cases.

A Classic Example: Imagine this scenario:

- Smith is justified in believing the following proposition: “Jones will get the job, and Jones has 10 coins in his pocket.” (Smith knows Jones applied for the job and counted the coins in Jones’ pocket.)
- From this, Smith infers a new proposition: “The man who will get the job has 10 coins in his pocket.”
- But unbeknownst to Smith, he himself will actually get the job, and he also happens to have 10 coins in his pocket (he just didn’t know it).

Thus, the proposition “The man who will get the job has 10 coins in his pocket” is true, Smith is justified in believing it, and he believes it—yet it doesn’t feel like Smith truly knows this fact because it was based on false reasoning about Jones.

Key Insight of the Gettier Problem: The problem exposes that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge because it allows for situations where "knowledge" is achieved by coincidence or luck. Essentially, Gettier cases show that justification must be reliable or connected to truth in a stronger way than the traditional definition allows.

Relation to the Concept of Justification and Knowledge: The Gettier Problem challenges the reliability of justification. In the context of knowledge acquisition:

- If justification isn’t sufficient to exclude cases of mere coincidence, then justification as a component of knowledge is problematic.
- The Gettier Problem inspired attempts to refine the definition of knowledge, leading to alternative theories like reliabilism or causal theories of knowledge. These emphasize that the justification must be causally or systematically connected to the truth, not coincidental.

If you're thinking about this in the context of epistemology broadly, it’s a direct critique of classical empiricism or any approach that doesn't account for the reliability of the justification process.
