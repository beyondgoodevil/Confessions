> "And many other signs truly did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book: but these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name." (John 20:30–31)

John calls Jesus' miracles **signs** (*sēmeia*), and he builds the first half of his Gospel round seven of them. Water becomes wine, a cripple walks, five thousand are fed, a man born blind sees, a corpse four days dead comes out of its tomb. Around them are the great images: living water, the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the true vine. What kind of thing is a sign? Is it a miracle that proves a claim, an event that also means something, or a picture of something that happens inside the reader? The note [[John's Gospel and Greek Philosophy]] dealt with where John's **Logos** came from. This note takes up a different question, how John's signs and symbols carry meaning, and reads three very different books on it with equal attention. **C. H. Dodd**'s *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel* (1953) is the classic historical and theological reading. **John Sanford**'s *Mystical Christianity* (1993) is a Jungian analyst's commentary on the Gospel's "interior, psychological, and esoteric sense". **Peter Kreeft**'s *Probes* (2019) is a book of nearly fifteen hundred questions for Bible study groups, written by a Catholic philosopher who refused to read any commentaries first.

### 1. Three Readers and One Question

The three books ask the same question from three places.
- **Dodd** asks what John meant, and to whom. He reconstructs the religious world of a "great Hellenistic city such as Ephesus" (p. 9): the Hermetic tracts, Philo, the rabbis, the Gnostics. Then he asks what John did with ideas he shared with them. His preface states the governing thought: "among the leading ideas of the gospel is the idea that certain historical events are a manifestation of the eternal and divine" (p. vii).
- **Sanford** asks what the Gospel means for the soul now. He does not claim a new method. He follows Origen and Gregory of Nyssa, who read Scripture on several levels, and he translates their "spiritual" sense into the language of Jung and Fritz Kunkel: ego, Self or Center, shadow, individuation (pp. 1–3, 11).
- **Kreeft** asks the reader. "This book consists entirely of questions" (Introduction). He wants the reader to dig the answers out of the text, with "both your reason and your faith, both your head and your heart".

All three agree on the starting point. John's narratives are not decorated sermons, and his discourses are not detachable doctrine. Story and meaning belong together, and the reader is expected to look through the event to something more. They disagree about what that "something more" is, and about how firmly it is tied to what actually happened.

### 2. Dodd: Where the Symbols Come From

Dodd spends the first third of his book on background (pp. 3–130). He does this because "without context" a reader risks "imposing upon it a subjective interpretation" (p. 4). He also warns that "the thought of this gospel is so original and creative that a search for its 'sources' ... may easily lead us astray. Whatever influences may have been present have been masterfully controlled by a powerful and independent mind" (p. 6).

| background | what John shares with it | what John changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hermetic tracts** (Egypt, 2nd–3rd c.) | Salvation as knowledge of God; light and life as names of God; rebirth (C.H. XIII); a heavenly Man who descends and ascends. The parallels have "an unmistakable 'Johannine' ring" (p. 33) and suggest "a common background of religious thought" (p. 36) | The Hermetic "vague and abstract *nous*" becomes "a concrete revelation of that which is truly divine in an historic life" (p. 212) |
| **Philo** | A symbolism of light, fountain, shepherd; *alēthinos* as "the real as distinguished from the phenomenal" (p. 72); the Logos as the world of Ideas | Philo's Logos "is never personal, except in a fluctuating series of metaphors"; John's is "fully personal ... having a place in history". "The Logos of Philo is not the object of faith and love. The incarnate Logos of the Fourth Gospel is both lover and beloved" (p. 73) |
| **Rabbinic Judaism** | Semitic idiom ("irresistible", p. 75), rabbinic argument (circumcision overriding the Sabbath, 7:22–24), the Torah as water, bread, wine and light | The contrast of Torah and incarnate Word "is one of the governing ideas of the gospel, appearing in various symbolic forms" (p. 83). The Torah "claims to be, but is not, the divine Wisdom, the light of the world, the life of men" (p. 86) |
| **Gnosticism** | Above and below, light and darkness, descent and ascent, a heavenly Anthropos | Gnostic knowledge is "knowledge about the structure of the higher world and the way to get there" (p. 101). In John, what Christ reveals "is God Himself, who so loved the world that He sent His Son" (p. 113) |
| **Mandaism** | Light and darkness, living water, John the Baptist | Too late to be a source. Against Bultmann, Dodd refuses to believe "that the shorter form is dependent on the longer" (p. 123) |

Dodd's own summary (p. 133) is that the evangelist knows rabbinic Judaism "but only partly sympathetic[ally]", is "more sympathetically in touch with Hellenistic Judaism as represented by Philo", is akin to the Hermetica, and relates to Gnosticism "more by contrast than by affinity". The method matters more than the list. "The distinctive character of Johannine Christianity is brought out by observing the transformation it wrought in ideas which it holds in common" with its neighbours (p. 133). The parallels show what John's first readers would have heard. The differences show what John meant.

### 3. Dodd: The Symbol Absorbed into the Thing Signified

The short chapter on **symbolism** (pp. 133–143) is the heart of Dodd's reading.

**John's symbols are not parables.** A Synoptic parable is "a picture of real life" that invites judgment on one point, and its details have no independent meaning (p. 134). The Good Shepherd is different. "Long before the allegory is at an end, the figure of the shepherd is fused with that of Jesus Himself." "No shepherd is also a gate ... But Jesus is both shepherd and gate." So "Christ is the real subject of all the statements made, and shepherd and gate are cryptograms" (p. 135). The vine discourse also uses a word for pruning that is not a vine-grower's word (Dodd checked the papyrus vineyard leases), and "abiding in the vine" is no natural expression: "The language indeed changes to and fro between the literal and the metaphorical" (p. 136).

**The symbol is almost absorbed into the thing signified.** The images "are taken from workaday experience" but "derive relatively little of their significance from the part they play in such experience. The symbol is almost absorbed into the thing signified" (p. 137). The key word is *alēthinos*, which in John usually means "real, as opposed to that which is either fictitious or a mere copy" (p. 139). So:

> "His *phōs alēthinon* is the archetypal light, *auto to phōs*, of which every visible light in this world is a *mimēma* or symbol; his *artos alēthinos* is the reality which lies within and behind every visible and tangible loaf ... Bread, vine, water, light, are not mere illustrations or analogies. A vine, in so far as it is a vine at all, bodies forth the eternal Idea of Vine." (p. 140)

Dodd does not think John read Plato. But the doctrine of Ideas "had entered into the texture of thought" (p. 139).

**Events are symbols too.** "The relation of symbol to thing symbolized, where the symbol is an historical event, is not essentially different." In a world where everything takes its reality from the eternal, "there may be some things and events which more perfectly than others embody or represent the Idea. Such are the events of the life of Jesus" (p. 140).

**What a *sēmeion* is.** The Hebrew *ōth* was a token or a prophetic symbolic act, like Ezekiel's, and "in the symbol was given also the thing symbolized" (p. 141). John's sign stands nearer to the prophetic act, but it refers first "to timeless realities signified by the act in time. Yet not wholly so." Christ's gift of life is accomplished "in reality and in actuality, by the historical act of His death and resurrection. In that sense, every *sēmeion* in the narrative points forward to the great climax" (p. 142). Dodd had already put the point sharply in discussing the Messiah: "to the evangelist a *sēmeion* is not, in essence, a miraculous act, but a significant act, one which, for the seeing eye and the understanding mind, symbolizes eternal realities" (p. 90). The crowds want "mere miracles; yet when they see a miracle they fail to see the 'sign'".

**So history and symbol do not compete.** "To a writer with the philosophical presuppositions of the evangelist there is no reason why a narrative should not be at the same time factually true and symbolic of a deeper truth" (pp. 142–143). John "writes in terms of a world in which phenomena—things and events—are a living and moving image of the eternal, and not a veil of illusion to hide it, a world in which the Word is made flesh" (p. 143).

### 4. Dodd: Knowledge, Faith and Agape. Is John a Mystic?

The signs exist to give **eternal life** (20:31), and eternal life is "that they might know thee the only true God" (17:3). Most of Dodd's second part asks what this knowledge is.

- **Eternal life is present.** Martha believes in the resurrection "at the last day"; Jesus answers, "I am the resurrection and the life". The resurrection Jesus speaks of "may take place before bodily death" (p. 148). Lazarus' bodily return "is a symbol of the real resurrection by which a man passes from a merely physical existence, which is death, into the life which is life indeed" (p. 148). Its everlastingness "is a function of its divine quality" (p. 149).
- **Knowledge is Greek and Hebrew at once.** Greek knowing is contemplation of essences. Hebrew knowing is acknowledgement of God in his works and response to his claims, "essentially intercourse with God" (p. 152). The world's failure to know God "is a failure on the ethical plane. It is wilful rejection of God" (p. 159). The mediating principle is found "not in abstractions, but embodied in a living person", and for this "no exact parallel can be found" (p. 168).
- **Truth is reality.** *Alēthinoi proskynētai* (4:23) are "not 'sincere worshippers', but 'real worshippers'", whose worship actually approaches God and is not "a shadowy ritual which either counterfeits or at best merely symbolizes the approach to God" (p. 170). But Jesus is not only the revealer of truth; "He is Himself *hē alētheia*", and this identification of ultimate reality with a historical person "transforms the effective meaning of the term" (p. 178).
- **Faith is seeing.** "Faith, then, is a form of vision. When Christ was on earth, to have faith was to 'see His glory'—to apprehend and acknowledge the deity through the veil of humanity. Now that He is no longer visible to the bodily eye, faith remains the capacity for seeing His glory" (p. 186). So faith "is not a stage preliminary to knowledge, but is itself the knowledge of God" (p. 201).

**Union with God.** John's language of mutual indwelling ("I in them, and thou in me", 17:23) has Hellenistic parallels. Dodd insists on the difference. It does not mean "an impersonal inclusion, or absorption, into the divine, conceived pantheistically", nor "an ecstatic possession by a divine afflatus". It is "a personal relation with a living God, mediated through a concrete, historical personality" (p. 197). Is John a mystic, then? Walter Bauer said he never trod the way of ecstasy and established relations with God through keeping the commandments. Dodd answers that to reduce John to legalism "is certainly to misunderstand him". Vision and obedience meet in **agape**, the love "which gives itself", unlike the "upward striving" of Platonic eros (p. 199). The passage ends with one of the best-known sentences in the book:

> "a personal community of life with the eternal God, which has the character of *agapē*, which is essentially supernatural and not of this world, and yet plants its feet firmly in this world ... because the crucial act of *agapē* was actually performed in history, on an April day about A.D. 30, at a supper-table in Jerusalem, in a garden across the Kidron valley, in the headquarters of Pontius Pilate, and on a Roman cross at Golgotha. ... Whether this should be called 'mysticism' I do not know." (p. 200)

**Judgment by the light.** John's judgment (*krisis*) is a sifting. The light comes with a purpose "wholly positive and creative", but "by an inevitable reaction" its coming shows up the ultimate distinction among men: "Men by their response to the manifestation of the light declare themselves, and so pronounce their own 'judgment'" (p. 210). Dodd calls this "a great creative achievement of religious thought" (p. 211). Two further points sharpen the picture. "God is spirit" (4:24) is not "God is a spirit", which would be "the most gross perversion of the meaning" (p. 225). And ancient thought "had no clear conception of personality" (p. 249). John's Son of Man "labours, grows weary, thirsts, feels joy and sorrow, weeps, suffers, and dies", and his glory "is the transfiguration of a human life by a supreme act of self-sacrifice" (p. 249). The human career of Jesus is "a projection of this eternal relation (which is the divine agape) upon the field of time" (p. 262).

### 5. Dodd: Seven Episodes and the Supreme Sign

In Part III Dodd reads the Gospel in order. The **Book of Signs** (chs. 2–12) "divides itself into seven episodes, each consisting of one or more narratives of significant acts of Jesus, accompanied by one or more discourses designed to bring out the significance of the narratives" (p. 290). The **Book of the Passion** (chs. 13–20) reverses the order: discourses first, then the story. The first chapter promises Nathanael that he will see heaven opened, and the signs that follow are that vision: "these 'signs' are history. ... Whatever else, therefore, the Gospel story is to be, it is to be a realized apocalypse" (p. 294).

Some of his readings show the method at work.
- **Cana** (2:1–11). "On the face of it we have here a naive tale about a marvel at a village wedding. There is realism ... (the stone waterpots hold from seventeen to twenty-five gallons apiece) ... And yet we are told, 'Jesus did this as the beginning of His signs, and so manifested His glory.' ... The story, then, is not to be taken at its face value" (p. 297). The water is "the entire system of Jewish ceremonial observance—and by implication for religion upon that level, wherever it is found" (p. 299). Origen put it more briefly: before Jesus, Scripture was water; since Jesus it has become wine.
- **What a sign is, in one line.** "For John a 'sign' is something that actually happens, but carries a meaning deeper than the actual happening" (p. 301).
- **Bethesda** (ch. 5). "Have you the will to become a healthy man?" Dodd glosses: "The law might show the way of life; it was powerless to create the will to live. The will to live, together with the power to live, is given in the word of Christ" (p. 320).
- **The Bread of Life** (ch. 6). A Hellenistic reader would not be impressed by healings, since "he could even learn for himself a spell for raising the dead" (p. 332, note on the magical papyri). The "works" in question concerned "the initiation into divine life which was the goal of the contemporary religious quest" (p. 332). The signs are "in some sort *verba visibilia*", visible words (p. 342), and the Christian reader "cannot miss the reference to the sacrament of the Eucharist" (p. 338). But John writes for a public that includes pagans and "will not directly divulge the Christian 'mysteries'" (p. 342).
- **Irony.** Jesus' brothers say "show yourself to the world" (7:4). "On the surface, we are reading about a rustic prophet who leaves the obscurity of the provinces ... In their deeper meaning they are an appeal to the Messiah to manifest Himself to Israel. But if we go deeper still, they speak of the manifestation of the eternal Logos" (p. 351). The trial of the man born blind is "rich in the tragic irony of which the evangelist is master" (p. 357).
- **Lazarus** (ch. 11). "Not the raising of Lazarus, as *sēmeion*, is the fulfilment, but that which the sign signifies" (p. 366). The Hellenistic world "was haunted by the spectacle of *phthora*", the decay of all things, and "Christ overcame death in dying" (p. 366).
- **The limit of signs** (ch. 12). The signs give only signs of life: sight for the blind, food for the hungry, bodily life for the dead. "Sign and reality are indeed intimately related, but the reality lies beyond the sign ... in order that we may pass beyond signs to the reality, the death and resurrection must be actual, must be carried through into history" (p. 372).

Dodd's summary of the Book of Signs is that its movement "is more like that of a musical fugue". Every act of Christ "contains within it the whole truth of the Gospel, and should disclose this truth if it is sufficiently pondered and probed" (p. 383). "Act and word are one; and this unity of act and word is fundamental to the Johannine philosophy, and distinguishes it from the abstract intellectualism or mysticism of much of the thought of the time" (p. 384).

**The Passion as the supreme sign.** The washing of feet "is a 'sign' of the incarnation of the Son of God, consummated by His self-oblation in death" (p. 402), and "it was night" (13:30) "recalls the whole symbolism of light and darkness" (p. 402). John "seeks symbols of spiritual truth in the most banal features of nature and human experience", and since the Word was made flesh, "everything He did or said or suffered must be scrutinized for inner meanings" (p. 434). Crucifixion is the lowest step of the descent, yet John calls it being "lifted up": "the death of Christ is at once His descent and His ascent, His humiliation and His exaltation, His shame and His glory" (p. 435). Every earlier sign is gathered into this one (p. 438). But it differs from them all:

> "Here is something that happened in time, with eternal consequence ... in it the two orders of reality, the temporal and the eternal, are united; the Word is made flesh ... Thus the cross is a sign, but a sign which is also the thing signified. The preliminary signs ... are true—spiritually, eternally true—only upon the condition that this Event is true, both temporally (or historically) and spiritually." (p. 439)

**Eschatology and the Farewell Discourses.** John "has chosen to treat the death and resurrection as eschatological events". Christ's death is his ascent to the Father, and his return to his disciples, bound up with the coming of the Spirit, "is His second advent" (p. 395). In chapters 13–17 the vocabulary shifts. *Zōē* occurs 32 times in chapters 1–12 and 4 times in 13–17; *phōs* 23 times and then not at all; *agapan* 5 times and then 31 (p. 398). The intention is "to emphasize the truth that the final reality of life and light is given in agape" (p. 399). The great prayer of chapter 17 resembles the hymn that ends a Hermetic initiation (pp. 420–22), with three differences. Initiation here happens "within a body of 'friends'"; union with God is "interpreted in personal and ethical terms as agape"; and it is "mediated by an historical transaction", the actual death and resurrection (pp. 422–23).

**History.** In an appendix Dodd sums up his view: the Gospel is "in its essential character a theological work, rather than a history", but "it is important for the evangelist that what he narrates happened" (p. 444). The evangelist "has meditated deeply upon the meaning of the Gospel story, taken as a whole. He then turns back upon the details of the story, and seeks in each particular incident the meaning of the whole" (p. 445). A quest for the historical Jesus that leaves John out "leads to an impoverished, a one-sided, and finally an incredible view of the facts" (p. 446), and the formula "the hour cometh, and now is" is "essentially historical" (p. 447).

### 6. Sanford: Reading John from the Inside

Sanford's commentary has thirty-two chapters, each taking a passage of John and drawing out its psychological meaning. Its premise is in the first paragraph. The book views John's Gospel "as an expression of the mystical side of Christianity. By mystical I mean to refer to the Gospel's interior, psychological, and esoteric sense" (p. 1).

**The lineage he claims.** Sanford insists he is "not inventing a new hermeneutic" (p. 1). Origen distinguished history from contemplation (*theōria*), and Gregory of Nyssa read the life of Moses as the soul's ascent. Modern consciousness is "rationalistic, materialistic, and extraverted", the early Church "more introverted" (p. 2), and "early Christian commentators were natural depth psychologists. They saw the soul as a battleground of archetypal forces" (p. 3). What the Fathers called the unseen world, Jung and Kunkel call the unconscious, which they treat "as real, even more real" than the outer world (p. 4).

**Where the discourses come from.** Sanford thinks the Christ who speaks in John "is not the historical Jesus ... but the Risen or Cosmic Christ" (p. 8). The evangelist received his discourses by something like Jung's **active imagination**, an inner dialogue with the Risen Christ, and "nothing is made up in such an experience. The figures or images with which the ego dialogues are real" (p. 8). He cites B. H. Streeter's *The Four Gospels* for the view that a mystic's visions may be veridical (p. 9). Yet the evangelist is also "a thoroughly historical character" who knows Malchus' name and the porticoes of Bethzatha (p. 177). Sanford holds both at once: "while John's Gospel is the most mystical of all the Gospels it also has the ring of history" (p. 306).

**Symbol, not allegory.** Sanford rejects reading Cana as allegory: "John uses symbolic discourses to convey his message and not allegories" (p. 44). He draws the distinction at the Good Shepherd. The usual reading makes the sheep Christians, which hardly flatters Christians. "Something allegorical lets one known thing stand in the place of something else that is known; a symbol uses something known to refer to something largely or entirely unknown" (p. 210). So the sheep are our instincts, which know their shepherd's voice. Origen read them this way too.

**Against both literalisms.** Rationalist scholarship explains the feeding of the five thousand away and is left with "the corpse of what was once a living reality". Literalism keeps the miracle but "aborts the possibility of seeing more deeply into the meaning of Scripture" (p. 145). Symbolic thinking is the way between them, and John insists on it: "Literal thinking leaves a person spiritually in ignorance; psychological and spiritual truth is opened up only through symbolic thinking. This is why John does not let his reader forget for a minute the narrowness of literal thinking" (p. 177). Nicodemus' trouble is "his inability to think symbolically" (p. 83). And "John's Gospel is the most unliteral document one could imagine" (p. 269).

**The vocabulary.** Sanford's key terms come from Kunkel as much as Jung. The **ego** is the conscious centre. The **Center** or **Self** is the deeper centre of the whole personality, the *imago Dei*. **Egocentricity** is the ego's defensive self-enclosure, which Kunkel sorts into types (the Turtle, the Clinging Vine, the Star, the Nero, pp. 260–64). **Individuation** is the lifelong process in which the centre of personality shifts from ego to Self. Born again means exactly this: "the replacing of the ego as the sham center of the personality by a new and genuine Center ... the New Testament calls it the Christ within. The psychology of individuation describes empirically the process that the New Testament describes in a mythological or supernatural language" (p. 82).

### 7. Sanford: The Signs as Inner Events

Read this way, each sign becomes something that happens in the soul as well as in Galilee.
- **Water and Spirit** (ch. 3). There is no article before "water" or "spirit" in 3:5, so the two are "one experience" (p. 85). Water is "the dark, the moist ... the feminine", in dreams "the most prevalent of all symbols for the unconscious" (p. 86); wind is the "moving, spermatic yang" (p. 87). "Above" and "below" in the New Testament "do not refer to literal geography but to inner or spiritual geography" (p. 81).
- **Living water** (ch. 4). The woman's well is a *phrear*, a cistern; Jesus promises a *pēgē*, a spring (p. 109). Sanford quotes Etty Hillesum: "a really deep well inside me, and in it dwells God" (p. 110).
- **The loaves** (ch. 6). Following Edward Edinger, the Self multiplies its effects, like the alchemists' stone in its *multiplicatio*; and it is broken to be shared, so each person carries "a portion" of the Self. Hence the Church as the Body of Christ, and the Eucharist (pp. 148–49).
- **Walking on the water.** The rationalist reading of *epi* as "by the lake" "may satisfy the rational intellect, but not the soul" (p. 149). The disciples meet the **numinous**, Rudolf Otto's word. Jung held that "the approach to the numinous is the real therapy" (pp. 151–52).
- **The bread of life.** Food in a dream means something is ready to be assimilated by consciousness. Christ as bread is a new consciousness "now ready for emergence in the human psyche" (p. 153). Origen's spiritual senses fit here: the soul too "sees, hears, feels, smells, and tastes" (p. 164). "If our minds can think only literally ... we cannot appreciate the tremendous subtlety and variety of the images of Christ that we find in John's Gospel" (p. 166).
- **The man born blind** (ch. 9). Jesus refuses the theory that illness comes from sin. Notably, the man is told to wash (*niptō*) only his eyes, not bathe (*louō*), so no cleansing from sin is implied (p. 204). "The great psychological blindness comes when we mistake our ignorance and error for truth" (p. 208).
- **Lazarus** (ch. 11). The cave is a place of mystery and the feminine. The raising is "one of a type of stories that tell us that new life can only emerge when there has been the death of the old life", the alchemical *mortificatio* (p. 226). Yet "the Fourth Gospel calls upon us not to believe simply in Jesus' resurrection, but in resurrection", which is "a certain state of being, much more than a single event" (p. 224).
- **The grain of wheat** (12:24). "Verses 23–24 contain the essence of John's mysticism" (p. 247). Paul's seed and plant have little in common. John's grain grows by continuous process into the plant, as a perfected soul grows from the death of the ego. But "the ego is an essential part of the plan of salvation, but is not the point of the plan", and much later Christianity turned the resurrection into "a kind of cult of the perpetual glorification and salvation of the ego" (p. 248).
- **The footwashing** (ch. 13). Feet are our "standpoint" in life (p. 257). Augustine on Psalm 9:15: "The foot of the soul is well understood to be its love" (p. 258). Sanford admits "it is not possible ... to know whether or not John was aware of the symbol of the foot", but the symbol was "in the air" (p. 259).
- **The vine** (ch. 15). This is "pure mysticism ... one of the most important passages in the Bible for the idea of deification" (p. 279). Branch and vine are the **ego-Self axis**; cut off from the Center, the ego withers (p. 281). The pruning Father is "the dark side of God", which destroys what is unfit to exist, unlike evil, which destroys what is sound (p. 282). Christ is "the 'true Dionysus'", the giver of a real ecstasy, though "there is no direct connection between Christ and Dionysus" (pp. 285–86).
- **The 153 fish** (ch. 21). Fish are unconscious contents to be caught and eaten, that is, made conscious (p. 333).

### 8. Sanford: "The Jews", the Archons and the Shadow

Sanford's psychological reading changes how he handles the Gospel's opponents.

**"The Jews."** In John they are symbolically "all persons who have an entrenched consciousness that excludes direct experience with the inner world ... a psychological attitude, not a religious or ethnic class" (p. 92). The disputes of chapters 7–8 are best read as active imaginations in which two archetypes, the old consciousness and the new, are set against each other. "Approaching the dialogue portions of John's Gospel in this way we free ourselves from being caught in ethnic controversies ... This is not to say that there were not historical encounters" (p. 184). The pattern is universal: Cronus swallowing his children, Herod killing the innocents, the Church against Galileo, the Viennese doctors who ignored Semmelweis (pp. 184–87).

**The archons.** Following Gerd Theissen, the "rulers of this world" are both human authorities and archetypal powers: the "dominant consciousness of society, which is experienced as a compulsive power" (p. 190). The New Testament is not a bare monotheism of God and humanity. It assumes "a vast and multitudinous spiritual realm" (p. 191), and "from the point of view of depth psychology the biblical viewpoint is entirely correct" (p. 192).

**The devil and choice.** *Diabolos* means one who throws something across our path, and what he throws is **choice** (pp. 195, 197). Sanford tells the story of a German soldier in a firing squad who whispered to a Jewish woman, "When I shoot, run" (p. 197). "Christ is there at every moment of choice, regardless of a person's religious belief system" (p. 198). Light and darkness are not opposites to be blended, like red and blue into purple, but two roads of which only one can be taken (p. 221).

**Where Sanford leaves Jung.** He parts from his master on evil. Jung sometimes placed the source of evil in the Self, which makes the Self "equally good and bad", and Sanford calls this "dire psychological theory", one that "some Jungians seem to be able to swallow ... with remarkable ease" (p. 234). "Where Jung sometimes seemed to believe that this archetypal energy for evil was a constituent part of the Self, the Bible saw it as alien to and destructive of our truest Self" (p. 237). In John evil has no *ousia*: it is "a contingent reality, not an ultimate reality" (p. 295). He also corrects Jung on the Trinity. Jung thought three a mutilated four that left out the feminine. Sanford answers that the Spirit is grammatically neuter, the Father is not a sexual designation, and the Son is Sophia (p. 295).

**Judas, Peter and Pilate.** Judas slides by degrees: a devil (6:70), a thief (12:6), the devil puts the betrayal into his heart (13:2), Satan enters him (13:27). "His will, once inclined toward evil, is now possessed by evil" (p. 238). Peter's denials are his meeting with his own shadow; he is "psychologically crucified", and his tears save him where Judas' remorse did not (pp. 311–12). In the Pilate scenes "the person who is really on trial is not Jesus but Pilate himself—and, indirectly, all of us" (p. 314).

### 9. Sanford: Where Psychology Stops

The most interesting chapter is titled "Beyond Psychology" (ch. 25). It shows where Sanford's method runs out.

**The unknowable Father.** In John the Father never appears. He is "the utterly transcendent aspect of God, who can be known only through the Son" (p. 270). Sanford brings in Gregory of Nyssa's negative theology: "we have learned to honour by silence that which transcends reason and thought" (p. 271). He also cites Otto's *Mysticism East and West*: the mystic contemplates a different God from the God of simple theism or of scholastic reason, and "Mysticism is not first of all an act of union, but predominantly the life lived in the 'knowledge' of this 'wholly other' God" (p. 272). The Son can be known because the Logos dwells in the soul as the *imago Dei*. That is "a psychological approach to a religious epistemology: one knows God because the image or imprint of God lives within the soul" (p. 274).

**Jung's box.** Here Sanford names Jung's limit. Jung was "a Kantian", for whom "the structure of the mind is responsible for anything we may assert about metaphysical matters" (Jung, quoted p. 276). On that view the psyche is "shut up in its own box". Sanford answers with an analogy: "Impress a hot coal on the body and we feel pain. Impress spiritual reality on the psyche and we feel numinosity" (p. 277). The numinous may come from outside the psyche, and the thought that God would cease to exist if all humans perished "seems to amount to a monumental hybris" (p. 277). He had already quoted Jung's own disclaimer: "I do not feel the slightest need to put the self in place of God" (p. 141).

**Deification and individuation.** On "Ye are gods" (10:34) Sanford gathers the Fathers on deification (*theōsis*): Irenaeus, Clement, Origen, Hippolytus ("Know thyself ... discover God within thyself"), Athanasius, Gregory of Nazianzus. He finds "the similarities between the ancient doctrine of deification and the modern idea of individuation" striking (p. 218). The difference is that for the early Christians the process came "only through Christ", while Jung and Kunkel thought it "open to all persons". Kunkel, however, held that the inner Center "was coincident with what the Christians would know as the Christ within" (p. 219).

**The absolute "I am."** In the arrest (18:5–6), the soldiers fall back when Jesus says *egō eimi*. Following Philip Harner, Sanford argues for an absolute "I am" echoing Exodus 3:14. He notes that Bultmann reads every instance with an implied predicate, which "removes from the expression the mystical or mythological element that Bultmann found so abhorrent" (p. 308). Modern translations hide the ambiguity with "I am he" (p. 309).

**The cross: symbol, process, cosmos.** On atonement Sanford finds that "in John's Gospel there is scarcely a word" about the penal-substitution theory (p. 327). John's cross is a *mystērion* of transformation, a mandala grounded in the earth. Then he guards his own reading against collapsing into psychology:

> "The cross can thus be understood as a symbol for this process of transformation, but it is more than a symbol; it is also that process itself. It is not only a historical event that took place on that first Good Friday but is a continual event ... It would be a mistake, however, to identify the crucifixion and resurrection with a purely psychological process, for the crucifixion and resurrection are also a cosmic process." (p. 329)

He ends on a grammatical point (from Allan Anderson). In 21:22 *heōs erchomai* should mean "while I am coming", not "till I come". So "Christ is not coming at some definite point in the future but is coming all the time" (p. 336). Peter is the activist who feeds the flock. The beloved disciple abides, and changes the world by the quality of his consciousness.

### 10. Kreeft: Probing, Not Answering

Kreeft's book is a different kind of thing. It contains about 1,450 numbered questions, labelled E for easy and H for hard, with no answer key: "This is not a quiz book ... and there are no answer keys" (Introduction). It is meant for groups who meet weekly, everyone rowing "the same boat (the text) down the same river".

**Its assumptions.** Kreeft states them plainly: "(1) the truth of the historic Christian faith and (2) the infinite depths and riches of Scripture, which endlessly rewards endless explorations, or 'probes'" (Introduction). And: "This is not a work of 'scholarship'. I deliberately did not read any scholarly commentaries on John's Gospel when writing this book". The Bible is not a mirror but "a window, where Someone Else is watching you watching him". Reading it is "a kind of spiritual lovemaking with the Word of God, which is primarily Christ himself and secondarily his inspired book". Near the end he calls the method *midrash*, "poring over and probing into Scripture deeply. (This whole book tries to do that; that's why it's called Probes!)" (John 12, Q837).

**Two keys to the whole Gospel.** Two verses come back again and again.
- **"What seek ye?"** (1:38), Jesus' first words in John, "may be just about the most important question in the world" (John 1, Q139–48). Kreeft returns to it at the arrest ("Whom seek ye?", Q1139, Q1143), at the tomb (Q1321), and in explaining why the Jews misread their prophecies: their answer to "What do you seek?" was worldly power, so they took the "enemies" to be Rome and the "kingdom" to be political (John 20, Q1308).
- **"If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine"** (7:17) is a rule of reading: "How is this the secret of interpreting the Bible?" (John 7, Q508). Kreeft returns to it on "Come and see" (Q154: "Why does he put 'come' before 'see'?"), on the hard saying of chapter 6 (Q457), and on 8:43 (Q614: "Is their mind moving their will, or is their will ... moving their mind?"). What the mind sees depends on what the heart loves (Q1308).

**Method.** Kreeft often asks readers to imagine themselves into a scene "as Saint Ignatius Loyola in his Spiritual Exercises advises" (John 11, Q767; also Q804, Q1169). He recommends the medieval **moral sense** as a thought-experiment: "each character in the Gospels being a part of our own personality and each event like an event in our lives ... Is there a Judas in you? A Peter? A John? A Mary?" (John 13, Q880). That comes surprisingly close to Sanford. And he values the imagination: "Sometimes the concrete sensory imagination can teach us more than the abstract spiritual intellect" (John 19, Q1216).

### 11. Kreeft: Literal and Symbolic Together

Kreeft's questions keep coming back to one issue: is this literal, symbolic, or both? His answer is almost always both.
- **The sign as pointer.** "Is it right to interpret a passage (especially, but not only, a passage about a miracle) both literally (physically) and allegorically (spiritually)? ... The Greek word for 'miracle' (*sēmeion*) means 'sign'. When you see a sign ... you both look at it and along it toward the thing to which it points. How does this fact explain the answer to question (A)?" (John 6, Q415).
- **Eating the sign.** Christ's words and miracles are both signs. "Suppose you are starving and you see a sign, 'Free food this way'. Can you eat the sign? (C) How were Jesus' unbelieving audience eating their signs?" (John 5, Q384). The scholars who search the Scriptures but will not come to Jesus are like Romeo "mooning" over Juliet's picture while she knocks at his door (Q381).
- **"And it was night."** "Are those last words literal, symbolic, or both? (B) If both, and if that is common in the Gospels, then why do we usually argue about whether a given passage is to be interpreted literally or symbolically?" (John 13, Q887–88).
- **Providence writes with things.** "We creatures can signify meanings with signs ... but not with the things and events in the universe, which God makes. But God, in his divine providence, can signify and communicate and reveal to us truths by means of real things and events as well as by means of words. What are the consequences of this doctrine of divine providence concerning whether it is legitimate to interpret the things and events narrated in Scripture both literally and symbolically?" (John 19, Q1245). He asks whether it was providence that the pool was named "Sent" (Q667), and whether Pilate's yo-yo movement in and out of the praetorium is "a perfect, visible symbol of what is happening in his soul" (Q1224).
- **Which is the image of which?** Kreeft makes a Platonist point. "My flesh is meat indeed" uses a word meaning "real". "We naturally interpret this symbolism 'up' rather than 'down', that is, we interpret physical, natural bread or food as literal and the 'food' or 'bread of life' that Christ speaks of here as symbolic. How does Christ turn this natural interpretation upside down ... Which is 'the real thing', and which is a picture?" (John 6, Q461). On "the true vine": "Is Christ a little bit like a vine, or is a vine a little bit like Christ? Does *bios* resemble *zoe*, or does *zoe* resemble *bios*?" (John 15, Q972). Paul does the same with fatherhood in Ephesians 3:14–15 (Q462, Q1334). Here Kreeft agrees exactly with Dodd's *alēthinos*.
- **Matter and spirit.** This reversal "corrects our natural view of matter. How do matter and material bodies mean more in this view (Jesus' view) rather than less?" Movie souls as "thin, wispy fogs" get spirit wrong (John 6, Q464). Why does one word, *pneuma* or *ruach*, mean air, breath, life and spirit? "We moderns wonder how the same word can mean both something physical and something spiritual. People in pre-modern societies, like ancient Israel, did not. Why do you suppose that is so?" (John 20, Q1350; cf. Q226). And the grain of wheat: "Why is there such a similarity between the biological principle of earthly, physical growth and the spiritual principle of heavenly sainthood? (Hint: Where did they both come from?)" (John 12, Q821).
- **"Literally" is not "physically."** Did Satan literally enter Judas? "'Literally' does not always mean 'physically' ... When we say 'that idea never entered my mind', we mean it literally though not physically" (John 13, Q885).
- **Open symbolism.** Many questions simply invite symbolic reading. What does a garden naturally symbolize (Q1137, Q1287)? What about the seamless tunic, "There is no single answer to this question" (Q1250)? Or the blood and water (Q1276), the cave and stone as "archetypal symbols" (Q773), "Unbind him, and let him go" (Q792), the locked doors (Q1340), the empty night's fishing and Christ on the shore (Q1391, Q1393), bread and fish rather than "nuts, berries, and milk" (Q1410)? He also passes on the medieval allegory of Peter as faith and John as reason at the tomb, "not to deny that it also happened literally" (Q1304).

### 12. Kreeft: Eyewitness Detail and the Charge of Myth

Where Sanford stresses the inner meaning, Kreeft stresses that it happened. His main argument is from **detail**.
- **Fairy tales have no such details.** "Why do you suppose the Gospels are so full of specific physical, visible, and literal geographical facts like these, that seem to have no deeper spiritual meaning? ... Contrast the absence of such little eyewitness details in a beautiful fairy tale ... What does that say about the idea that the Gospels are a fairy tale?" (John 1, Q124; also Q722: "once upon a time" or "in a galaxy far away"). Why does John name Malchus and say which ear (Q1150)? Why give the place and hour at Gabbatha (Q1233)? Why describe the two piles of grave cloths (Q1305)? Why include the exact number of fish (Q1405)? Why do we never learn what Jesus wrote in the dust, a detail from which "nothing comes" (Q557)?
- **Interpretation is not belief.** "Interpretation means figuring out what the writer believed and meant, not what the reader believes. Why is it, therefore, bad literary science to interpret Jesus' miracles as mere fables, parables, myths, fictions, or symbols for abstract, invisible, universal truths? (B) What does the literary style of eyewitness description have to do with your answer? (C) Even though Jesus' miracles literally happened in this visible world, they also often symbolize our future heavenly destiny" (John 9, Q666).
- **The myth theory.** "'Liberal' or 'Modernist' theologians claim that the Gospels are also myths ... What do little details like John knowing the maid at the door or ... Peter warming their hands by a charcoal fire do to that literary theory? (B) If you compare the Gospels with all the pagan myths, what striking difference in style do you find? (C) Is it reasonable that a few Jewish fishermen invented modern realistic fantasy nineteen centuries before Tolkien? Or is that the fantasy?" (John 18, Q1161; cf. Q659). Why is all "Jesus fiction" so "pitifully shallow" compared with the Gospels (Q506, Q659, Q1376)?
- **Testimony.** John certifies an eyewitness in 19:35; why (Q1248, Q1278)? If the story were invented, why make women the first witnesses when their testimony was inadmissible (Q1293)? Why make the male heroes "such dummies" (Q1310)? Are the differences between the angel accounts more or less credible "by the standards of human credibility on the part of witnesses in court?" (Q1311).
- **Bultmann.** Kreeft quotes a saying he attributes to Bultmann and the Jesus Seminar, that if Jesus' bones were found "all the essentials of Christianity would remain unchanged", and asks what that could possibly mean (John 20, Q1327). Sanford's absolute "I am" made the same target of Bultmann's demythologizing.

**An exoteric faith.** One question goes to the heart of the contrast with Sanford. "Most religions in the ancient world were esoteric ... their core teachings could be fully understood only by the few, the elite, the 'insiders', or the mystics. This was true also of ... the Essenes. However, mainline Judaism has always been exoteric ... How does the dialogue between Annas and Jesus show which kind of religion Christianity is?" (John 18, Q1166). The answer is 18:20: "in secret have I said nothing".

**Truth as a person.** Kreeft returns to the Johannine paradox that general things are a particular person. How can Jesus "not only show or teach the way but be the way" (Q916)? How can "the resurrection" be one man (Q755: "Perhaps it is something none of us can really understand")? "All other sages and philosophers and mystics gave us their mind and said, 'This is my mind.' Jesus gave us his body and said, 'This is my body.' How is that 'the truth'?" (Q1203). And he insists on the difference between "theology" learned from others and "religion" as personal relationship (Q1194, on Pilate's "Sayest thou this thing of thyself?").

### 14. Assessment

| | Dodd | Sanford | Kreeft |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What a sign is** | A significant act that symbolizes eternal reality and points to the cross | An outer event that mirrors and triggers an inner one | A thing to look at and along; both literal and symbolic |
| **Where meaning comes from** | The evangelist's mind, shaped by Jewish and Hellenistic religion | The unconscious, through active imagination; archetypes | The author's intention and God's providence |
| **Relation to history** | Essential: the signs are true only if the cross happened | Affirmed but secondary; the inner meaning can be found "quite apart from the issue of its historicity" (p. 145) | Essential and argued for from eyewitness detail |
| **Is John a mystic?** | "Whether this should be called 'mysticism' I do not know": agape, not ecstasy | Yes, John's Gospel is "pure mysticism" (p. 279) | Asks whether the mystics "see" God (Q104) and whether a vision would bless more than faith (Q1372) |
| **"The Jews"** | Historical opponents; John "external and detached" towards the Torah (p. 82) | A psychological attitude, "not a religious or ethnic class" (p. 92) | Not "all Jews": "Prove by verse 13" (Q500; cf. Q195) |
| **Method** | Comparative history of religions and close exegesis | Patristic spiritual sense in Jungian dress; dream analysis | Questions, prayer, imagination, group dialogue |

**Dodd's strengths.** Dodd shows better than anyone why John's symbols work as they do. The parallels with the Hermetica and Philo are real, and he never treats them as sources. He sets the parallel beside the difference every time, so the reader sees John transforming his neighbours rather than borrowing from them. His one sentence about the symbol being "almost absorbed into the thing signified" explains why the Good Shepherd breaks the rules of a parable, and his account of *alēthinos* explains "the true vine". His picture of the cross as "a sign which is also the thing signified" holds his whole interpretation together. Because the earlier signs are "true ... only upon the condition that this Event is true", Dodd can read John as symbolically as Sanford and still hold, with Kreeft, that the history matters. He also corrects the cruder alternatives, Bultmann's Mandaean theory and Reitzenstein's priority of myth.

**Dodd's weaknesses.** Some of his history has dated. The Dead Sea Scrolls appeared as he wrote and later showed a Palestinian Jewish dualism of light and darkness, which weakens the need for Hellenistic parallels. His Philonic Logos, the "world of Ideas", is read into the evangelist with a confidence the text does not always support. Several of his judgments are stated more firmly than argued. He says the Apocalypse cannot come from the same mind, he plays down any idea of the death of Christ as expiation in John, and he reads "Behold the man" as possibly veiling "Behold the heavenly Man" (p. 437). For all his insistence on history, he grants that the Samaritan woman and Pilate scenes are largely the evangelist's dramatic composition (p. 450).

**Sanford's strengths.** Sanford takes seriously something Dodd only states: that John wants the reader changed, not just informed. His patristic citations are many and apt, and his claim to stand in Origen's line has substance. His distinction between allegory and symbol is good, and his reading of the sheep as instincts is fresher than the usual one. He shows real independence of Jung. He rejects the evil in the Self, defends the Trinity, refuses to make God a function of the psyche, and insists that the cross is "also a cosmic process". The "Beyond Psychology" chapter, with its hot coal and its use of Otto and Gregory of Nyssa, is a fair statement of where depth psychology must stop if it is not to swallow religion.

**Sanford's weaknesses.** Psychology still sets the agenda. The claim that the discourses come from active imagination with the Risen Christ cannot be checked, and it makes the Gospel the record of one man's inner life rather than testimony. His Greek is sometimes shaky. He calls *agapē* "the word used for the love of friendship" (p. 334), and he draws a large lesson from the subjunctive of 13:34 (p. 260), which is simply the normal mood after *hina*. His reading of "the Jews" as an attitude is well meant, but it is a modern reading of John, not John's own meaning. Several symbols are admitted to be uncertain ("it is not possible ... to know whether or not John was aware of the symbol of the foot", p. 259). Finally, his appeal to the "M effect" as evidence that one person's consciousness spreads through a species (p. 331) is speculation, not science.

**Kreeft's strengths.** Kreeft's questions return the reader again and again to the text. They are often sharp: "Can you eat the sign?"; "Is Christ a little bit like a vine, or is a vine a little bit like Christ?"; "Are those last words literal, symbolic, or both? ... then why do we usually argue ...?" Without having read Dodd, he arrives at Dodd's *alēthinos* (the spiritual is the real, the physical its image) and Dodd's link of sign and history. His argument from eyewitness detail is the strongest reply to the purely symbolic reading, and it matches Dodd's insistence that the narrative is "much more than a dramatic vehicle for ideas". His use of 1:38 and 7:17 as keys is a real interpretive thesis: in John, seeing depends on willing.

**Kreeft's weaknesses.** Kreeft's refusal of scholarship is honest, but it costs him. Some of his assertions are wrong. For instance, "world" in John is *kosmos*, not the *gaia* and *aiōn* he offers (Q1015, Q1197). Several questions are apologetics in question form: the trilemma, the swoon theory, Frank Morison. Many questions lead towards a Catholic answer on baptism, the Eucharist and Mary, though he usually states the other side fairly. The detail argument is weaker than he thinks. Dodd shows that realistic detail and freely composed dialogue can sit side by side in John, and Kreeft never engages the critical case. Most of all, a book of questions cannot tell us what John meant. It can only make sure we keep asking.

**What the three together show.** The three books end up closer than their methods suggest. All three reject the reading that stops at the surface miracle: Dodd's crowds see miracles but not signs, Sanford's rationalists keep "the corpse", Kreeft's audience eats the sign. All three reject the reading that floats free of the event: Dodd ties every sign to the cross, Sanford calls the cross "more than a symbol", Kreeft argues from the charcoal fire. And all three agree that in John the symbol is the more real thing. Dodd's bread "bodies forth" the eternal Idea, Sanford's vine is the ego's actual root in the Self, and Kreeft's earthly bread is the image of the true. Their real disagreement is about where the deeper meaning lives. For Dodd it lives in history, in what God did "on an April day about A.D. 30". For Sanford it lives in the psyche, though finally, he admits, beyond it. For Kreeft it lives in the Person whom the reader must meet. Dodd's position seems the strongest, because it alone explains why John needs both the sign and the event. The others each take one half of what Dodd holds together.

### Conclusion

John's signs are acts that mean. Dodd shows where the language came from: the light, life and rebirth of Hellenistic piety and the water, bread and wine of the Torah. He shows how John made these images into symbols "almost absorbed into the thing signified", and tied every one of them to a death that is both a sign and the thing signified. Knowledge of God, in his reading, is neither speculation nor ecstasy but agape, mediated by a historical person. Sanford reads the same symbols as maps of the soul's transformation, with real help from Origen, Gregory of Nyssa and Jung. Then he stops where he must: before the unknowable Father, the numinous that comes from outside, and a cross that is "also a cosmic process". Kreeft asks fourteen hundred questions to keep the reader from settling for either half. He insists that John's details are an eyewitness's and that John's symbols are true in Jesus' own sense: the spiritual bread is the real one and the earthly bread its picture. Taken together, the three books suggest that the Fourth Gospel can only be read symbolically if its symbols are read as things that happened, and that its history can only be understood if what happened is read as a sign.
