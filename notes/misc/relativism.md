Relativism is wrong because it undermines logic and truth itself. If all truths are relative, then the claim "truth is relative" cannot be universally true, creating a self-contradiction. Furthermore, relativism makes meaningful communication impossible because it denies objective standards by which ideas, beliefs, or morals can be evaluated. Without objective truth, there is no foundation for logic, morality, or reason, as all perspectives would be equally valid, even if they are contradictory. This collapse of coherence demonstrates that relativism refutes itself and cannot be a viable worldview.

## Kinds of Relativism

| Kind | Claim | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **truth** (alethic) | truth itself is relative to a person or culture | "true for you, not for me" |
| **epistemic** | standards of justification vary, none better | "science is just the West's way of knowing" |
| **moral** | right and wrong are relative to a culture or person | "who are we to judge?" |
| **cultural** (descriptive) | cultures do in fact differ in beliefs and values | anthropology. True, but a fact, not a theory of truth |

Descriptive cultural relativism is just observation. The others are philosophical claims, and they don't follow from it.

Adler's definitions (*Ten Philosophical Mistakes* ch. 5) help keep the words straight:
- **subjective** = differs for you, for me, for everyone; **objective** = the same for everyone.
- **relative** = varies with time and circumstances; **absolute** = doesn't.
- He thinks most people fall into two camps that never really argue: those who think moral judgments are mere taste ("one man's meat is another man's poison") and a dogmatic camp sure of absolute standards but unable to defend them except by faith. "To that extent, both are equally dogmatic."

- **Truth relativism**, the classic statement (Protagoras): "**Man is the measure of all things**, of things that are, that they are, and of things that are not, that they are not." What seems so to each person is so for them.
- **Moral relativism**, the common version: what Williams calls "**vulgar relativism**", "possibly the most absurd view to have been advanced even in moral philosophy". It has three parts:
    1. "right" means "right for a given society"
    2. "right for a given society" means what's functionally useful for it
    3. **therefore** it's wrong for one society to condemn or interfere with another's values

## How It Refutes Itself

- Plato's reply to Protagoras, *Theaetetus* 170–171 (the *peritropē*, "turning around"): most people think Protagoras is **wrong**. If what seems true to each is true for them, then Protagoras's theory is false for most people; and Protagoras must admit his opponents are right. So it refutes itself.
- Also: if every judgement is equally true, no one is wiser than anyone else, and Protagoras has no business being paid as a teacher.
- Vulgar relativism (Williams): (3) uses "wrong" in a **non-relative**, universal sense, which (1) says doesn't exist. Tolerance is offered as a universal moral rule by a theory that denies universal moral rules.

## Arguments For, and Replies

| Argument | Reply |
|---|---|
| **Disagreement**: cultures disagree, so there's no fact of the matter | people disagree about the shape of the earth too. Disagreement doesn't show there's no truth. And the deep agreement (don't kill the innocent, keep faith, care for children) is greater than the surface differences (C. S. Lewis, *Abolition of Man*, appendix) |
| **Tolerance**: relativism makes us tolerant | tolerance is a **universal** value; relativism can't support it. A relativist has no reason to object to intolerant cultures |
| **Humility**: who are we to judge? | relativism doesn't make us humble; it makes reform impossible. Abolitionists, reformers and martyrs were "wrong" by their society's standards |
| **Cultural conditioning**: our beliefs come from our culture | the **genetic fallacy**: where a belief comes from doesn't decide whether it's true. And the claim applies to relativism too |

**The root mistake** (Adler, *Ten Philosophical Mistakes*): modern relativism comes from confusing **knowledge** with **opinion** and from the empiricist idea that the mind knows only its own ideas, not things (Locke). If we only know our ideas, each person's world is their own. The cure is realism: the mind knows **things**. See [[Critique of Empiricism]].

- **Knowledge vs opinion** (ch. 4). "False knowledge" is a contradiction, "true knowledge" redundant; but "true opinion" and "false opinion" both make sense. Adler's three grades:
    1. **Self-evident truths**: the opposite can't be thought. A finite whole is greater than its parts; a triangle can't have diagonals. We *know* these; to say we "believe" them is a misunderstanding.
    2. **Corrigible knowledge**: opinions backed by enough evidence and reasons to be held true "at the time", beyond reasonable doubt or by a preponderance of evidence: science, history.
    3. **Mere opinion**: prejudice and taste, held with no evidence at all.
- **Reality** is "that which exists whether we think about it or not". And it must be public: "Nothing that is knowable by one person alone can have the status of knowledge."
- **Hume's** mistake: only mathematics and experimental reasoning count as knowledge; metaphysics and divinity go to the flames as "sophistry and illusion". That became positivism and scientism.
- **Kant's** mistake, the "more serious": the mind forms experience through its own categories, so what we know is never independent of us. The only mind-independent things are unknowable things-in-themselves, so "the real is the unknowable". That's idealism replacing realism.
- **Popper's** falsifiability isn't the only line between knowledge and opinion. Philosophy, like mathematics, works from **common experience** (not lab data) but, like science, claims truth about reality, and it can be **refuted by argument**. "If philosophy were mere opinion there would be no philosophical mistakes."

**How moral relativism grows** (Adler ch. 5):
- **Hedonism**: if good = pleasure, and pleasures differ, then goods differ. But nobody really thinks wealth, health, friends and knowledge aren't good. Plato (*Philebus*): a life of pleasure plus wisdom beats pleasure alone, so pleasure isn't the only good. Aristotle against Eudoxus: "the pleasure proper to a worthy activity is good and that proper to an unworthy activity is bad."
- Epicurus and **Mill** rank "higher" and "lower" pleasures ("better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied"), which needs a standard of goodness other than pleasure. Adler's fix: two senses of pleasure, the sensual pleasure we desire and the **satisfaction** of any desire. "Higher pleasures" are really higher goods.
- **Spinoza**: we don't desire things because they're good; we call them good because we desire them. Then, since desires differ, good is relative. This is the real root.
- **Adler's answer**: **needs** vs **wants**. Natural desires (needs) are the same in everyone and are always for **real goods**; "there are no wrong needs". Acquired desires (wants) differ and aim at **apparent goods**, which may be bad for us. Real goods are the basis of **natural rights**. Without them there's no ground for universal human rights, and we're left with "might makes right".

## What Is Right in It

*Mine.*

- Some things really are relative: manners, customs, legitimate variety in law, liturgical practice. Paul: "to the Jews I became as a Jew" (1 Cor 9:20). Distinguish **customs** from **truth**.
- Humility about our own knowledge is good. But that's **fallibilism** (I might be wrong), not relativism (there's no being wrong).
- Christ "**the Truth**" (John 14:6): truth is ultimately a Person, not a cultural construction, and not just a set of propositions. See [[John 14:6: Jesus Is the Truth]].
