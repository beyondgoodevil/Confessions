> "A nihilist is a man who judges of the world as it is that it ought not to be, and of the world as it ought to be that it does not exist." (*Will to Power* 585)

Reginster calls nihilism "the central problem of Nietzsche's philosophy". Most of what N. says about it is in unpublished notebooks (the *Will to Power* material), and he attaches eighteen different adjectives to the word. Reginster's book is the clearest way through.

N. = Nietzsche. GS = *Gay Science*, GM = *Genealogy*, WP = *Will to Power* notes, BT = *Birth of Tragedy*, A = *Anti-Christ*, BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, Z = *Zarathustra*.

## 1. What "Meaningless" Means

- Two senses of a "meaningful life":
  - **a specific value** among others, e.g. making a difference or belonging to a tradition. A life could lack it and still be worth living (righteous, happy);
  - **a generic value**: the existentialist question "is life worth living at all?". This is N.'s sense. "Meaningless" and "valueless" are the same word for him (WP 12).
- Nihilism = **goallessness**: "the goal is lacking; 'why?' finds no answer" (WP 2).
- **Reginster's key analysis**: a goal can inspire you to live only if two things hold:
  1. you think it is **valuable**;
  2. you think it is **realizable**, i.e. the world allows it.
- Lose either and the goal stops inspiring. So nihilism has two possible sources, which give two different nihilisms.

## 2. Two Nihilisms: Disorientation and Despair

| | **disorientation** | **despair** |
|---|---|---|
| condition lost | value | realizability |
| what's wrong | our values (they lack the authority we thought) | the world (it can't realize our values) |
| typical text | "the highest values devaluate themselves" (WP 2) | "it would be better if the world did not exist" (WP 701) |
| typical feeling | "Is there still any up or down? Are we not straying as through an infinite nothing?" (GS 125) | the world "ought not to be" (WP 585) |

- **Disorientation** is the usual scholarly reading: no objective values, so nothing matters. It rests on a hidden premise Reginster calls **normative objectivism**: values have authority *only if* they are objective.
  - It is not scepticism (there's a truth but we can't reach it). It is anti-realism: "every belief... is necessarily false because there simply is no true world" (WP 15). "Nothing is true, all is permitted" (Z IV).
  - But then the logical response would be indifference: if nothing matters, it shouldn't matter that nothing matters. Yet N.'s nihilists *lament*. That shows the **need for meaning** is natural to man: "man has to believe, to know, from time to time why he exists" (GS 1).
- **Despair** is Reginster's main thesis about N.: most of the notebook material treats nihilism as **despair**, the belief that what matters most can't be had.
  - It must be **necessary**, not contingent. If I can't write the great novel because I lack talent, my life lacks meaning, but I'm not a nihilist: I'd still like to live, just as someone else.
  - **Pessimism vs nihilism.** The pessimist says this world is worth less than we thought but hopes for another, "true" world. The nihilist keeps the verdict on this world and then discovers the other world was "fabricated solely from psychological needs" (WP 12, 37). "All one has left are the values that pass judgment—nothing else."
  - **Active vs passive nihilism** (WP 22–23) only make sense as despair: passive = resignation to an evil world; active = "a violent force of destruction" against it. Both still hold the old values.
- GS 346 states the dilemma: "Either abolish your reverences or—yourselves!" Keeping the values and condemning ourselves "would be nihilism". N.'s project is to show that abolishing the reverences *needn't* be.
- The two conflict: if values are devaluated, there's nothing to despair about. Reginster's book is the story of how N. handles that.

## 3. Highest Values and Expectations

Why doesn't failing to get your highest goal just make you settle for less (the musician who becomes a contented teacher)? Because of two features of "highest values":

- **They condition all other goods.** "Everything of value... must be proved to be of moral value" (WP 382). Reginster's example: if my highest ideal is deep friendship, failing at it makes my intellectual work feel pointless, even though its value isn't *only* instrumental to friendship.
- **They are expected, not merely aspired to.** An unfulfilled aspiration leaves a lack. An unfulfilled **expectation** is a positive evil, a grievance. If I expect friendship, its absence makes life "untenable".
- With both features, failure doesn't just leave life less good. It makes life something that "rationally should not exist" (WP 701).

## 4. "God Is Dead"

- Not "God has been refuted". God, if He exists, can't die. What has died is the **belief**: "the belief in the Christian god has become unworthy of belief" (GS 346).
- **Discrediting vs refuting.** N.'s two-step argument:
  1. No proof of God has succeeded.
  2. Then show the belief's "human, all-too-human" origin in psychological need. That gives a reason to stop looking for better proofs.
- "In former times, one sought to prove that there is no God—today one indicates how the belief that there is a God could arise... a counter-proof that there is no God thereby becomes superfluous" (*Daybreak* 95). And: "the ideal is not refuted—it freezes to death" (*Ecce Homo*).
- Reginster's analogy: a child says there are ghosts in her room. You find nothing. That doesn't prove there are none, but then you learn she'd been frightened by a ghost story that evening.
- Against Kant: proving God *can't* be disproved just opens "a secret path to the old ideal" (A 10).
- **The shadow.** "God is dead; but... there may still be caves for thousands of years in which his shadow will be shown" (GS 108). God's role can be taken over by secular stand-ins: historical progress, Hegel's world-spirit, pure reason.
- **Girard's note on GS 125** (the madman): the text doesn't say God died of old age. It says "We have killed him—you and I. All of us are his murderers." Commentators turn it back into the harmless "death of God". See [[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]].

## 5. The Hidden Premise: Life-Negating Values

- N.'s distinctive contribution: nihilism does **not** follow from the death of God by logic alone. It can even feel like "a new and scarcely describable kind of light, happiness, relief, exhilaration... dawn" (GS 343). Without a further premise it is only "psychologically necessary" (WP 55).
- **The hidden premise**: our highest values are **life-negating**. They can't be realized in this world at all, only in another. So when the other world goes, they become unrealizable everywhere.
- "We have measured the value of the world according to categories that refer to a purely fictitious world" (WP 12).
- Moral values are life-negating in three connected senses:
  1. this life can't realize them (the core sense);
  2. they were invented to condemn life, out of revenge (*Ecce Homo* IV.7);
  3. following them harms life (A 6: "values of decline... hold sway under the holiest names").
- **Two versions in the Christian–Platonic tradition:**
  - **Platonic**: becoming is mere appearance; "what is does not become; what becomes is not" (*Twilight* III.1).
  - **Christian**: this life is real, and so is its suffering, but it's a bridge to another life that will make up for it. This is the ascetic ideal, "life as a wrong road" (GM III.11).
- The ascetic conclusion: "So to live that there is no longer any meaning in living: that now becomes the 'meaning' of life" (A 43).

So the argument is:
1. our highest values can only be realized in another world (negation of life);
2. there is no other world (death of God);
3. so life is meaningless.

N. accepts (2), so he has to attack (1). That's the **revaluation of values**.

## 6. Ridley: How the Will to Truth Brought It About

- The Christian virtue of truthfulness, trained for two millennia, is what kills belief in God. Christian morality, by its own honesty, "conquered the Christian God".
- Then "the will to truth becomes conscious of itself as a problem" (GM III.27). Even faith in truth is the ascetic ideal in its last form.
- The ascetic ideal had at least "offered man meaning... the will itself was saved" (GM III.28). Now the will is loose and "would rather will **nothingness** than not will".
- So "incomplete nihilism" (WP 28) is the condition of those who accept the death of God but haven't seen what follows. N. wants to **complete** nihilism: to trace it to its source in our values. "I have hitherto been a thoroughgoing nihilist" (WP 25), but in the sense of one who has "lived through the whole of nihilism, to the end" (WP Preface 3).
- See [[Nietzsche's Character Types: Slave, Priest, Philosopher, Artist, Scientist, Noble]] sec. 5.

## 7. First Attempt: Deny That Values Are Objective (Reginster ch. 2)

- **The obvious move**: show that the life-negating values have a "shabby origin" and no objective standing. Then there's nothing to despair over: "the universe seems to have lost value... but that is only a transitional stage" (WP 7).
- **The problem**: this trades despair for **disorientation**. If *no* value is objective and objectivity is required for authority, nothing matters.
- **N.'s way out**: reject normative objectivism. Values can have authority without being objective. He rejects Kant's demand for "pure", unconditional reasons. Values are rooted in our affects, and the "esteemer" (Z I.15) is what man *is*. Reginster compares a kind of **fictionalism**: evaluating is not discovering facts, and the artist's "lie" has "a good conscience" (GM III.25).
- **But this cuts both ways.** If lack of objectivity doesn't devalue values in general, it doesn't devalue the life-negating ones either. So we're back at despair.
- The metaethical stage is a necessary first step, not the solution. What's needed is a **substantive** revaluation: show *from inside our own values* that the life-negating ones aren't highest.

## 8. The Real Revaluation: Suffering (Reginster ch. 3–4)

- **Will to power** (Reginster's reading): not wanting a particular outcome but wanting the **overcoming of resistance** itself. "The feeling that power increases—that a resistance is overcome" (A 2).
- So it essentially wants **resistance**, which means it wants **suffering**. And no state of final satisfaction can exist: power is "becoming", not being. See [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]].
- That reverses Schopenhauer:
  - for Schopenhauer, pleasure is only the absence of pain, and life swings "between pain and boredom";
  - so the morality of **compassion** treats suffering as simply evil, and the goal is "resignation", the negation of the will.
- **The target of the revaluation**: the view that suffering is "evil" and "ought to be abolished" (BGE 225). Three applications:
  1. **Compassion.** N. objects to compassion that "considers suffering and displeasure as evil, hateful, worthy of annihilation" (GS 338). Such pity is "practical nihilism... compassion persuades to nothingness" (A 7). But he doesn't reject compassion as such:
     - "a man who is by nature a master—when such a man has compassion, well, this compassion has value!" (BGE 293)
     - His "higher" compassion is for how man makes himself small. "You want, if possible... to abolish suffering. And we? It really seems that we would rather have it higher and worse than ever... The discipline of suffering, of great suffering—do you not know that only this discipline has created all enhancements of man so far?" (BGE 225).
  2. **Greatness.** Greatness = creativity = the will to power valuing resistance. A morality of "green-pasture happiness... security, absence of danger, comfort" that wants suffering "abolished" blocks it (WP 957). "Sometimes compassionate hands can interfere in a downright destructive manner in a great destiny" (*Ecce Homo* I.4).
  3. **Happiness.** The old idea of happiness is passive: "rest", "peace", "satiety", the "sabbath of sabbaths" (BGE 200, naming Augustine). N.'s "new happiness" is active ("not contentment, but more power; not peace at all, but war", A 2). Suffering is an ingredient of it: "happiness and unhappiness are sisters and even twins" (GS 338).
- **Result**: if the will to power values resistance, life in this world isn't hostile to our deepest value but required by it. The world of becoming is no longer an objection. That makes **affirmation** possible, and eternal recurrence is its test. See [[Nietzsche: Eternal Recurrence]].
- **Dionysus versus the Crucified** (Reginster ch. 6): the two figures stand for two meanings of suffering. For one, suffering is an objection to life. For the other, being is "holy enough to justify even a monstrous amount of suffering" (WP 1052).

## 9. Heidegger: Nihilism as the History of Platonism

- For Heidegger, nihilism isn't a mood or a political label. It is **the fundamental event of Western history**, and "the whole of Nietzsche's philosophy is rooted" in his experience of it.
- **N.'s philosophy is a countermovement.** It wants to **invert** the whole of Western philosophy, which is Platonism. That's why it has to be a "revaluation of *all* values": it aims at the uppermost values Plato set up.
- Heidegger's reading of "God is dead" (in a separate essay, "Nietzsche's Word: 'God Is Dead'") = **the end of the supra-sensible world** in the Platonic sense.
- **Recurrence as the hardest test.** At first eternal recurrence looks like the *most extreme* nihilism: "existence as it is, without meaning and goal, yet inevitably recurring... the nothing ('meaninglessness') eternally!" (WP 55). Thought as a **decision in the moment** ("do you want this again?"), it becomes the overcoming of nihilism. "Only the one whose thinking ventures forth into the uttermost need of nihilism will be able to think the overcoming thought."
- **Heidegger's verdict**: inverted Platonism is still Platonism. "Value" itself is a metaphysical idea, so N. is the last metaphysician, not the first one free of it.
- **Girard's objection to Heidegger**: he cleans the religious struggle out of N. ("Dionysus versus the Crucified") and turns it into abstract metaphysics.

## 10. Was Nietzsche a Nihilist?

- He calls himself one ("our pessimism"), but Reginster says these are **relative** terms: pessimist about the Christian–Platonic values, not about life. "The world might be far more valuable than we used to believe" (WP 32).
- From the Christian side, the "Antichrist and antinihilist" (GM II.24) will inevitably look like a nihilist.
- **It goes back to his first book.** BT already asks how the Greeks survived the "wisdom of Silenus" ("the very best thing... is not to be born... the second best is to die soon", BT 3) without sliding into "Buddhistic denial of the will" (BT 7). See [[Apollo and Dionysus: The Birth of Tragedy]].

## 11. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's right**
- The two-condition analysis (value and realizability) is genuinely useful. It explains why loss of faith often feels like grief, not liberation, and why "you can make your own meaning" rarely consoles.
- N. is right that nihilism follows only given certain values. A faith whose highest good is wholly elsewhere, and treats this world as a waiting room, does collapse into nihilism when the "elsewhere" goes. Much modern Christianity was like that.
- The shadow of God point is sharp. Secular faith in progress is borrowed eschatology.
- He's right that a love which only wants to remove suffering, with no idea what suffering is *for*, is thin. Patristic pastoral care knows that some suffering heals.

**Where it goes wrong**
- **"Life-negating" misdescribes Christianity.** The Orthodox hope isn't escape from this world to a "true world" but the **transfiguration** of this one: resurrection of the body, "a new heaven and a new earth" (Rev 21:1), creation "delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Rom 8:21). The goal is realized *here*, beginning now, in the sacraments. So premise (1) is false for the Fathers, and N.'s argument hits Platonism more than Christianity. See [[Christian Platonism]].
- **"God is dead" by genealogy** commits the genetic fallacy unless the proofs have really failed. N. assumes they have (via Kant) and doesn't engage the classical arguments. See [[The Unmoved Mover]], [[The Moral Argument for God]].
- **The need for meaning** is itself evidence. N. treats it as a fact about us to be managed. But a natural need that nothing in a godless world can satisfy (he says so himself: we can't make the best of a bad situation) fits better with Augustine's "our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee". Compare "He hath set the world in their heart" (Eccl 3:11).
- **Suffering.** Christianity doesn't say suffering is simply evil to be abolished, or simply good to be wanted. Death is "the last enemy" (1 Cor 15:26), yet "tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope" (Rom 5:3–4). Suffering is redeemed, not affirmed for its own sake. N.'s "rather have it higher and worse than ever" has no answer to suffering that crushes rather than strengthens: children, the demented, the broken.
- **Power for its own sake** has no content. If what you want is just overcoming resistance, any resistance will do, and the noble and the cruel can't be told apart from inside the theory. That's the [[Is Ought]] gap again, and the [[Relativism]] problem.
