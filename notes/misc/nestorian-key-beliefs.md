Nestorians hold the following key beliefs:

1. **Two-Natures Doctrine**: They emphasize a clear distinction between Christ’s divine and human natures, viewing them as separate but coexisting, rather than fully united in one Person.
    - **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (d. 428), Nestorius's teacher, is the real source (Meyendorff):
        - the Word dwells in the man Jesus "**by good pleasure**" (*eudokia*), as in a temple (John 2:19-21 read this way): more than in the prophets, but the same kind of indwelling
        - two natures, each with its own **prosopon**, joined in one prosopon of union: one "face" toward us
        - the man Jesus grows morally, is tested and, by obedience, merits his exaltation
        - **Theodore**: the man Jesus "enjoys the cooperation of the Word **in proportion with his determination** toward the good". That leads to a "humanist asceticism": salvation as our own effort, imitating Jesus' effort (Meyendorff).
    - **The core worry behind it**: protecting God's **impassibility** and Christ's real **humanity**. If the Word is the subject of the suffering, then God suffers and changes; if the humanity has no subject of its own, it isn't fully human.
    - The vocabulary of the time had no adequate terms for **person** as distinct from **nature**. Antioch, wanting to keep both realities, spoke of "the Word **assuming**" and "the man Jesus **assumed** by the Word" (Meyendorff).
2. **Rejection of *Theotokos***: They refuse to call the Virgin Mary *Theotokos* ("God-bearer") because they believe that Mary gave birth only to Christ's human nature. Instead, they use the term *Christotokos* ("Christ-bearer"). This leads them to say that she is the "Mother of Jesus" but not the "Mother of God," as they argue that the divine nature of Christ cannot be born of a human.
    - Ephesus made *Theotokos* a dogma because Christ's flesh had no independent existence: it was "**the flesh of God**" (Meyendorff).
    - **Nestorius's position as Cyril reports it**: Mary could be called Mother of God and mother of the man, but "strictly speaking" she was mother of **Christ**, "God conjoined to man". Cyril's syllogism: if Mary isn't "strictly speaking" Mother of God, Jesus isn't "strictly speaking" God.
3. **Christological Controversy**: This doctrine was condemned as heretical by the Council of Ephesus (431), which upheld the teaching that Christ is one Person with two united natures. Nestorianism was accused of dividing Christ into two separate persons—one divine and one human.
    - **Cyril's answer**: the Word suffers **in the flesh**, "impassibly", i.e. in His own human nature, not in His divine nature. The subject is one; the natures are two. That's the "theopaschite" formula later defended at Constantinople II: "**one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh**".
        - The dialogue (c. 438) is Cyril's mature statement; its title states the thesis. Cyril had by then moved from frontal attack to a gentler apologetic, though he still called Diodore and Theodore "the teachers and sources of Nestorius' heresy" (Cyril, *On the Unity of Christ*).
        - Against the "assumed man": if the Word only dwelt in a man, He should be called not "man" but "**mannish**", as a resident of Nazareth is called a Nazarene, not "Nazareth" (Cyril).
        - Christ is "the Heavenly Man" not because His flesh came from heaven but because the Word came down and was born of a woman (Cyril).
        - If the "name above every name" (Phil 2:9) was given to a separate son of David, how could the Word receive what He already was? So the one who is exalted is the Word **in His flesh** (Cyril).
        - Christ's flesh is "the veil" (Heb 10:20), as the Temple veil covered the Holy of Holies (Cyril).
        - The worst outcome: the Godhead "thrown over" a man "like a **mask**", with "an alien glory". That's what Cyril rejects.
    - Theodore was condemned posthumously at the Fifth Council (553). See [[The Fifth Ecumenical Council]]
    - **Theodoret**, after reconciling with Cyril, replaced the "assumed man" with abstract terms like "humanity" (Meyendorff).
    - The **Monophysite** side: Cyril: "in the union what is united can no longer be separated: one Son, and his nature is one". Philoxenus: "there is no nature without person, just as there is no person without nature." Severus: "one is the agent, the incarnate Word; one is the activity; the works are varied." (Meyendorff)
    - **Leontius of Byzantium** (6th c.): the Word's hypostasis is common to both natures. **Julian of Halicarnassus**'s aphthartodocetism (Christ's body incorruptible from conception) was condemned even by moderate Monophysites: evidence the East as a whole ignored a doctrine of "original sin of nature" (Meyendorff). See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]].
    - Meyendorff's thesis: "neo-Chalcedonianism" (reading Chalcedon through Cyril, condemning the Three Chapters in 553) wasn't a concession to Monophysitism but rooted in the patristic view of God and man: deification requires that the subject in Christ be the Word.

## What's Lost If Nestorius Is Right

- **Salvation**: a man joined to God by grace is just a very holy man; his death doesn't destroy death. See [[Penal Substitution Leads to Nestorianism]].
- **The Eucharist**: we'd receive a man's flesh, not God's life-giving flesh.
- **Theosis**: human nature isn't really united to God in Christ, so it can't be in us.
    - The incarnation is an "**economy**": not chiefly about the relation of God and man as two things, but "the complete reconciliation of God and Man in Jesus". "What he was by nature, we become by grace." (Cyril)
    - The change involved doesn't destroy the human condition but **fulfils** it: its destiny is "an ever deepening communion with God" (Cyril).

## The Church of the East Today

- The **Assyrian Church** rejects Ephesus, venerates Theodore and Nestorius, uses "two qnome (hypostases) in one parsopa". The 1994 Christological declaration with Rome suggests the differences may be partly verbal.
