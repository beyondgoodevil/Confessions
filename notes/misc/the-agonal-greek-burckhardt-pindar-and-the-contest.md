Jacob Burckhardt, lecturing at Basel in the 1870s and 1880s, called the centuries from the Dorian migration to about 500 BC the **agonal age**. In it the *agon*, a contest among equals before chosen judges, "was a motive power known to no other people", working on "the will and the potentialities of every individual". Oswyn Murray calls this the most important insight that Burckhardt and his younger colleague Nietzsche shared. This note sets out Burckhardt's account, with C. M. Bowra on the **heroic outlook** behind it, Walter Donlan on the society that produced it, and Stephen G. Miller on what the games actually were: who won, who paid, and who died.

### 1. Burckhardt's Agonal Age

- **The period.** Burckhardt describes the agonal age as "the Middle Ages of Greek history". Its marks were rule by aristocracy and tyranny, a "firm belief in breeding", and the ideal of *kalokagathia*, "the unity of nobility, wealth and excellence". He compares the nobility to "the patriarchate in mediaeval cities, particularly the Italian": a society living together in the city, for which "the ethos of the agon would have sufficed to unite such a group even in the absence of other factors". Tyranny, being utilitarian, "was inimical to the agon". Sparta stood apart, as "a conquering people ruling harshly".
- **The claim.** "While on the one hand the polis was the driving force in the rise and development of the individual, the agon was a motive power known to no other people - the general leavening element that, given the essential condition of freedom, proved capable of working upon the will and the potentialities of every individual. In this respect the Greeks stood alone."
- **Why only Greece.** Despotism and caste ruled out contest among equals in Egypt and Asia; there an Egyptian's ambition stopped at a royal honour. "Only small free aristocracies could allow the expression of the will to self-distinction among equals before judges." The Romans, "who differed from them chiefly in their dislike of anything 'useless'", would never have invented it.
- **Gymnastics follows the contest.** Gymnastics "was not the cause but the consequence of the agon". Its aim was "to develop the body to the highest perfection of beauty", by a discipline as severe as an artist's training. "The full development of the individual depended on his constantly measuring himself against others in exercises devoid of any direct practical use."
- **Burckhardt and Nietzsche.** Murray writes that "the realization that individual contest and the desire to be supreme lay at the centre of early Greek attitudes to the world is their joint discovery. Nietzsche seems to have realized the importance of the agon or contest, even before he arrived in Basle; but Burckhardt had already formulated it independently." The idea still shapes study of Greek ethics, "often seen as a conflict between competitive and cooperative virtues". Nietzsche made it the theme of his early essay "Homer's Contest".

### 2. Homer: The Contest Before the Agon

- **An "innocent first step".** For Burckhardt the agon in Homer is still undeveloped. The great heroes perform their labours alone, and "men engaged in war have no need of jousting". But Homer already knows every event. The Phaeacians compete in running, jumping, wrestling, discus and boxing, and Laodamas tells Odysseus that a man's greatest fame lies in his hands and feet (*Odyssey* 8.97ff.). The beggars' fight between Odysseus and Irus is "the most ancient description of a boxing match conducted according to rules and under supervision", with a haggis as the prize and the suitors promising to be impartial (*Odyssey* 18).
- **Patroclus' games.** The funeral games of *Iliad* 23 include nearly every later event. The prizes, however, are bronze, animals and slave-women, not wreaths, and there are consolation prizes for losers. Achilles stops the armed duel before anyone is killed.
- **The motto.** Glaucus' father sent him to Troy "to be always among the bravest, and hold my head above others" (*Iliad* 6.208, Lattimore), and Peleus told Achilles the same. Bowra quotes it as "ever to be the best and to surpass others" and notes that Achilles interprets it widely: strength, beauty, eloquence and counsel together.
- **Donlan's sociology.** The Homeric chief "endlessly competed with his fellows for prestige (*kydos*, *timē*), with the goal of being recognized as 'best' (*aristos*); his greatest fear was failure and its accompanying communal humiliation". His worth was public: "how one 'seemed' not what one 'was'". Donlan adds that the agonistic impulse "exhibit[s] a remarkable tenacity throughout Greek history", long after the tribal society that produced it had gone.

### 3. Hesiod's Good Strife

Burckhardt finds a second, non-aristocratic root of the agon in Hesiod's *Works and Days*. There are two kinds of Strife (*Eris*). The bad one breeds war. The good one, born first, "awakens even the indolent and unskilled to industry", so that "neighbour vies with neighbour in striving for wealth". Hesiod himself won a bronze tripod for a song at the funeral games of Amphidamas at Chalcis and dedicated it to the Muses (650–59), which gave rise to the legend of a contest between Homer and Hesiod. Burckhardt concludes: "Thus after the decline of heroic kingship all higher life among the Greeks, active as well as spiritual, took on the character of the agon... noble victory without enmity... the ancient expression of the peaceful victory of an individual."

### 4. The Heroic Outlook (Bowra)

Bowra gives the ethic behind the contest: "The essence of the heroic outlook is the pursuit of honour through action." "Fame is the reward of honour", and the creed "runs through Greek history from Homer's Achilles to the historical Alexander".

1. **Honour against morality.** Bowra distinguishes three ways in which honour differs from ordinary morality:
   - Honour is "more positive than negative": it demands effort. Pericles applies this even to money: there is no shame in poverty, but "the real shame is in not taking practical measures to escape from it."
   - "The ultimate test of honour is human dignity", and that standard grows more exacting over time.
   - "The only court of appeal is a man's own feelings." No plea from his comrades moves Achilles.
2. **The philosophers' tribute.** Pythagoras compared life to the Olympic Games. Seekers of knowledge are the spectators, seekers of honour the competitors and seekers of gain the hucksters. Heraclitus preferred those who "choose one thing above all others, immortal glory among mortals" to the many "glutted like beasts".
3. **From hero to city.** The heroic ideal survived because it was attached to the polis. At Thermopylae the dead lie "in obedience to their words". In the Funeral Oration Pericles praises the nameless fallen, whose death was "a culmination of glory, not of fear". "It was no more difficult for the Greeks to attach the heroic ideal of manhood to a city than it was for the men of the Middle Ages to attach it to Christendom."
4. **The darker side.** Honour "almost made hatred a virtue and vengeance a duty"; "the Greeks never thought it possible or desirable to love their enemies". Injured honour drove Demaratus, Themistocles and Alcibiades to serve their country's enemies.
5. **The Mean.** Poets and philosophers preached the Mean. But "the attention which the Greeks paid to the Mean suggests not so much that they observed it as that, in the fullness of their blood, they felt that they needed some curb for their more violent ambitions". Pericles' last speech, "the brilliance of the present is the glory of the future, stored up for ever in the memory of men", is "a complete denial of the Mean".
6. **Death as completion.** "The hero fulfils himself in death". The shadowy afterlife makes glory the only lasting reward. In Pericles' words, "famous men have the whole earth as their memorial".

### 5. The Games That Made Hellas

- **Beyond the city.** "It was altogether the nature of the agon to transcend territorial boundaries", and chariot racing in particular needed neutral ground. For Burckhardt the Panhellenic games were "uniquely decisive in breaking down enmity between tribes, and remained the most powerful obstacle to fragmentation into mutually hostile poleis". "It was the agon alone which united the whole nation as both participants and spectators"; those who stayed out, like the Aetolians, "forfeited to a greater or lesser extent the right to be counted among the Hellenes".
- **Olympia.** At Olympia people slept in the open, in heat and thirst, yet "all this was made up for by the tremendous mood of exaltation". The judges (Hellanodikai) trained for ten months. Women were excluded, except the priestess of Demeter Chamyne. There "the size and geographical spread of the Greek nation was on display", "freely and spontaneously held together in its shared intensity by the contests". Gorgias spoke there on Hellenic unity, and Cleomenes recited Empedocles. Even time was counted by Olympic winners. Miller adds that the register of victors is ultimately how we know that Socrates died in 399.
- **Spread.** Local contests were "innumerable". A rich man "could spend the whole year in such occupations", and champions like Theagenes collected crowns by the hundred: 1,400 according to Pausanias, though Plutarch adds that "most of them were trivial".

### 6. Victory and Its Rewards

- **The prize.** Crowns of olive, pine and bay replaced Homer's treasure, "but the true motive for the contest was victory itself... the highest thing on earth; for it assured the champion he had attained the aim of all Greeks, that is to be admired in his lifetime and praised at his death" (Burckhardt).
- **Honours.** Thucydides could find no better comparison for Brasidas' welcome at Scione than that "they adorned him with ribbons and ran to meet him as if he were an athlete" (4.121). Victors guarded the Spartan kings. In Athens they dined for life in the Prytaneion: Miller cites the decree (*IG* I³ 131). Solon reportedly set cash prizes of 500 drachmas for an Olympic victory; Burckhardt suspects he was propping up "an enthusiasm that was already diminishing". Cimon's thrice-victorious mares were buried opposite his tomb (Herodotus 6.103).
- **Money.** Miller dismisses the myth of ancient amateurism. Theagenes' 1,376 victories in prize games would, at Panathenaic rates, have earned him a fortune, and "there was big money to be made in athletics even in the earlier, 'amateur' days".
- **Statues.** "Fame won at the agon" was "for them the true and almost the only kind of fame". Burckhardt argues that the victor's statue is the origin of Greek portraiture: "portraiture, which in this case begins, by and large, with the whole, necessarily naked figure, never again had such an origin anywhere in the world." Eubotas of Cyrene had his statue made in advance, and Milo carried his own into the sacred precinct. Miller adds a caution: Lysippus' perfect bodies "create an illusion that is far from the oily, sweaty, dusty athletes in whom the arete resided."
- **Horses.** Owners, not charioteers, took the crown. Cimon ceded his chariot victory to Peisistratus to buy his return from exile. To show the Spartans that horse-breeding "was only a matter of wealth and not of skill", Agesilaus entered a chariot in his sister Cynisca's name.

### 7. The Dark Side

Burckhardt does not idealise. "No true happiness could result from the concentration of the whole of life on a few seconds of terrible tension."

- **Violence.** Forty chariots crashed in one race at Delphi (*Pythian* 5). Boxers and pankratiasts were left with cauliflower ears. Eurydamas swallowed his knocked-out teeth so his opponent would not see them, and finger-breaking was allowed. Men died and were crowned dead. In Lucian's epigram a boxer says: "At Olympia I still had one ear, in Plataea one eye, and at Pytho they bore me away lifeless." Burckhardt adds: "but the Greeks would have it so and would not be contented with less."
- **Cheating and civic pride.** Hieron of Syracuse induced Astylus of Croton to compete as a Syracusan, and the Crotoniates turned his house into a prison. The Thessalians denied that their champion Polydamas had ever lost. A lyre-player was murdered at Sybaris by partisans.
- **Losers and old age.** In Pindar, the defeated creep home by back alleys. The pankratiast Timanthes, finding he could no longer draw his great bow, burned himself alive in imitation of Heracles. Burckhardt suggests counting "the Olympic champions who came to a sad end".
- **Oafs.** Miller adds the comic side. When the victor Dioxippus blushed at a pretty girl in the crowd, Diogenes said: "Look at your great big athlete, throttled by a little girl." Charmus finished seventh in a field of six. "The image of athlete as hero was balanced by the image of athlete as oaf."

### 8. Athletes as Heroes

Miller corrects a modern assumption: ancient athletes became heroes "not because of their athletic accomplishments". A hero had to be dead and to receive cult, as a healer and as a channel to the underworld.

- **Milo**, the greatest wrestler, who could hold a pomegranate so that no one could take it yet leave it unbruised, "never became a hero". He died with his hands trapped in a split tree trunk, and wolves ate him.
- **Cleomedes of Astypalaea** killed an opponent at Olympia in 492 and was stripped of the victory. Back home he pulled down a school roof on about sixty children, then vanished from a locked chest. Delphi pronounced: "Kleomedes of Astypalaia is the last of heroes. Honor with sacrifices him who is no longer mortal."
- **Theagenes of Thasos.** An enemy flogged his statue every night until it fell on him and killed him. The statue was tried for murder and thrown into the sea, but brought back when famine struck and the Pythia complained, "You do not remember your great Theagenes." His cult as a healer was still being kept up five centuries later.
- **Euthymus of Locri.** His statue base still shows where its inscription was recut so that the statue would be "admired by the mortals", as if he were no longer one of them.

### 9. Pindar: Arete Without Hubris

- **The indispensable witness.** Burckhardt calls Pindar "our indispensable informant on the competitive spirit". He warns that Pindar's clients "will have been among the last aristocrats to take part", for "soon afterwards democracy was to make the agon dangerous or impossible for this class". Pindar shows us the champion families: the Oligaithidai of Corinth, and the Diagoridai of Rhodes, whose father was carried round Olympia by his victorious sons.
- **Miller's reading of *Olympian* 7.** In this ode for the boxer Diagoras (464 BC), Pindar asks Zeus to honour "his now famous arete in boxing" and praises him because "he travels the straight path which despises hubris". In Miller's words, "the athletic excellence of striving for arete and the moral virtue of avoiding hubris are balanced". But "Pindar was hired to write for Diagoras, and he was not about to add the warts". Pindar himself admits his Muse sold "sugared soft-voiced songs silver-plated for sale".
- **Donlan's reading.** Donlan treats the same odes as "a triumph of propaganda", linking god, hero, family, victor and city in "an unbreakable circularity". The victory proves the inborn excellence that caused the victory. (The fuller dispute is in [[Is Culture the Aristocratic Ideal Intellectualized? Jaeger, Donlan and the Greek Nobility]].)

### 10. Contest Everywhere

Burckhardt's broadest claim is that the agon spread from the stadium to everything.

- **Religion and art.** "In the whole ancient world the Greeks stand alone by the fact that the agon took possession of the cult and drew the artistic elements of religion into its own sphere." Tragedy and comedy were competitive from the start. Choruses competed tribe against tribe, and Plato complains of the number of rival choirs.
- **The rest of life.** Symposium songs, cock-fights and eating feats were all contests. The suitors of Agariste were tested for a year in Cleisthenes' stadium and palaestra (Herodotus 6.126–30). Theocritus' shepherds "cannot hold their singing competition when there is no judge at hand". "Agon" became the ordinary word for a lawsuit, and the dialogue itself became a contest.
- **The verdict.** "The existence that resulted from all this was of a kind never known before or since anywhere on earth - all of it saturated and dominated by the agon." Burckhardt adds a modern contrast: our "longing for fame" has been replaced by "business competition". He ends with a judgment of his own: "we may well believe that the Greeks sought the value of life too much in the opinions of others, and the agon declined until it came to consist in men staking everything to win the favour of the crowd."
- **Contempt for work.** The agonal age, for Burckhardt, also fixed the Greek "contempt for physical work". Its ideal was "combat - not so much in the military sense as in that of equals pitted against each other. The whole nation was convinced that this was the highest thing on earth", and "the agon occupied the whole of existence".

### 11. The Critics

- **Xenophanes.** Xenophanes would not rank a champion above his own wisdom. An Olympic victor does not put the city in better order (*eunomia*), and "these things do not fatten the treasury of the polis" (fr. 2, as translated by Donlan). Tyrtaeus had already refused to praise speed, strength or beauty without "savage valor" in the line.
- **Aristotle.** Miller quotes the *Politics*. Since there is no agreement about what *arete* is, there is none about how to train for it. Any pursuit that "renders the body, soul, or mind of free men useless for the practice of arete" is vulgar, and the Spartans "turn their children into little animals". "Neither a wolf nor any other animal will risk a noble danger, but only a good man," and "those who train their children in athletics to the exclusion of other necessities make their children truly vulgar". Hardly any boy champions went on to win as men.
- **Arete as individual.** Miller explains why ball games barely appear in the sources: *arete* "is an individual characteristic... a function of self-fulfillment, of testing oneself against individual capacities and limits. Arete cannot be best displayed on a team". Yet "arete was not the exclusive possession of the winner": anyone who did better than could reasonably be expected of him showed it.

### 12. Assessment

- **Aristocratic or democratic?** This is the main disagreement among the sources.
  - *Burckhardt* makes the agon an aristocratic creation, which democracy made "dangerous or impossible" for the nobility.
  - *Miller* argues the opposite for the running and fighting events (the gymnikos agon). Results were objective, foul play was flogged regardless of status, and athletes competed naked, so "social position cannot be easily discerned in the locker room". He traces the idea of equality before the law (*isonomia*) to these contests. Kroton, he notes, was both an early democracy and a power in these events, with no recorded chariot victories. Alcibiades despised the gymnic games because some athletes were "lowborn".
  - *Donlan* shows how the nobles responded to the hoplite equality of the phalanx: they turned to chariots, display and the inborn excellence Pindar celebrates.

  The three can be reconciled. The chariot race was the nobles' contest. Running and combat became everyone's. The agon itself, as Hesiod's "good Eris" already shows, was never the property of one class.
- **Burckhardt's strengths.** No one has better described how contest saturated Greek life, nor its costs. His dark side (the injuries, bribery, the ruined old champions, "the favour of the crowd") keeps the picture from becoming a celebration. Murray is right that the idea "has proved continually fruitful".
- **His weaknesses.** His "Middle Ages" frame and his talk of breeding and "quality... rather than size of population" show nineteenth-century assumptions, and archaeology has since changed much of his colonial and archaic picture. Miller's evidence (money prizes, professional careers, the hero cults) supplies the material history that Burckhardt's literary sources lack.
- **Bowra's contribution.** Bowra explains why the agon could survive inside the polis. Honour could be attached to the city, as at Thermopylae and in Pericles' speech, and the Greeks' anxious praise of the Mean was a curb on, not a denial of, the urge to excel. His observation that the desire to excel "feeds on the humiliation of others" names what Burckhardt's phrase "noble victory without enmity" leaves out.

### Conclusion

Burckhardt saw that the Greeks made competition among free equals before judges into a way of life. It grew out of Homer's funeral games and Hesiod's good Strife. It was organised in the Panhellenic festivals that taught scattered cities they were one people, and it spread into drama, law, philosophy and the symposium. Bowra supplies the ethic behind it, honour pursued through action and completed in death, and shows how the city took it over. Miller supplies the reality: big money, bribed champions, broken bodies, athletes worshipped not for winning but for strange deaths, and a Pindar paid to leave out the warts. Contest was the Greek road to excellence and to its excesses. Aristotle's warning, that a man trained only to win is "truly vulgar", is itself a judgment from inside the culture he criticises.
