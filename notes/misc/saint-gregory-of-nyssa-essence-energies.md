Saint Gregory of Nyssa spoke about the ascent toward God through His energies in his doctrine of **epektasis**. This concept describes the soul's continuous and infinite journey toward God, who is limitless and beyond full comprehension. Gregory teaches that while the soul can never fully attain or understand God's essence, it constantly grows and moves toward Him, participating in His divine energies.

In **"Life of Moses"**, Gregory uses Moses' ascent of Mount Sinai as a metaphor for this eternal journey toward God, saying:

**"This truly is the vision of God: never to be satisfied in the desire to see him."**

This teaching emphasizes that the process of becoming united with God is an ongoing, dynamic journey, where believers experience God's energies ever more deeply, but without fully comprehending His infinite essence.

## Moses' Ascent

The *Life of Moses* (full title *On the Life of Moses, or On Perfection in Virtue*) was written for a monk asking about the perfect life, usually dated late (c. 390) because Gregory mentions his "grey hair" (I.2). Meredith notes he already called himself grey at about 40, so that's weak evidence. Two unequal parts: the short **history** (Exodus retold) and the long **contemplation** (*theoria*), reading each episode as a stage of the soul's ascent, from Moses' birth (II.1) to the "final discovery" (II.305).

- The prologue states the thesis: "since this good has no limit, the participant's desire itself necessarily has no stopping place but stretches out with the limitless" (I.7).
- Already in the history (I.46) Moses "makes a bold attempt upon the darkness": whoever would have fellowship with God must "stretch his intelligence to what can be neither seen nor grasped and believe that the divine is there where the understanding does not come".
- **Virtue** here is wider than morals: it's knowing God and living rightly together. "Religious virtue" has two parts, what concerns God and what concerns conduct (II.166). Meredith: Gregory won't divorce mystical experience from moral life, unlike Evagrius, who seems to think a stage comes where virtue no longer needs attention.

**Three theophanies, three stages**

| Event | Stage |
|---|---|
| **The burning bush** (Exod 3) | **light**: the first knowledge of God, turning away from error. God reveals Himself as "He who is" (*ho ōn*): only God truly is. See [[The Name of God]] |
| **The cloud** (Exod 19) | the mind goes beyond the senses, from appearances toward the invisible |
| **The darkness** on Sinai (Exod 20:21, "Moses drew near unto the thick darkness where God was") | the highest: knowing God by **unknowing**: "the true vision of the One sought consists in this: in seeing that He is not seen" |

**The burning bush in detail** (II.19–30)
- The light comes not from the stars but from an earthly **thorn bush**: the light of the Word shining "through the thorn bush of the flesh" (John 1:9).
- The bush that burns and isn't consumed is **Mary**: "the flower of virginity was not corrupted by the birth". Meredith: probably the first time the bush is applied to Mary's virginity after the birth.
- **Take off your shoes**: remove "the dead and earthly covering of skins" from the feet of the soul, the "coats of skins" we got after the Fall.
- Truth is "being free from error about the nature of reality". What Moses learnt is that nothing grasped by sense or mind really exists in itself; only what's above them, which is "participated in by all, yet... in no way thereby diminished": **the Really Real** (*to ontos on*).
- Only after this does Moses go back to free others: you have to see the light before you can lead anyone out of slavery.
- The **hand** that turns leprous outside his bosom and is restored inside it is the Incarnation: "the change of the right hand of the most High" (Ps 77:10). The Only-begotten came out of the Father's bosom, took our colour, and took it back with Him, turning "our changeable and passible nature into impassibility". The rod becoming a serpent is the same mystery.

**The darkness in detail** (II.162–166)
- It looks like "the very opposite of the first theophany": then God was seen in light, now in darkness. But it isn't a contradiction: leaving error is moving into light, and then the further the mind advances, the more it sees that the divine nature is unavailable to knowledge.
- The mind leaves behind what the senses grasp and "whatever the intelligence seems to behold". "This is the seeing that consists in not seeing." John, who also entered the bright cloud, says "no man hath seen God at any time" (John 1:18), not even any rational creature.
- "Every thought and every defining conception which aims to encompass and grasp the divine nature is only forming an **idol** of God."

**The back of God** (Exod 33:18-23)
- Moses asks to see God's glory; God shows only His **back parts**.
- Gregory: this means **following** God. "To follow God wherever He might lead is to behold God." You see the back of someone you walk behind.
- And: Moses, after all his ascents, still asks to see more. Desire for God is never satisfied, because God is infinite (the line quoted above).
- In more detail (II.231–244):
    - The lover of beauty takes "what is seen as an image of what he desires" and yearns for the archetype. God "concedes what is demanded by actually refusing it".
    - "No man shall see me, and live" (Exod 33:20) isn't saying the vision kills. Since God is life and beyond definition, "whoever supposes that God is one of the things he knows" has turned from the Real to a concept, and so has no life. "Moses' desire is satisfied precisely in so far as his desire remains unsatisfied."
    - **The argument for infinity** (II.236–238): whatever has a limit is bounded by something other than itself, as fish by water and birds by air. God is the beautiful; what's other than the beautiful is evil. So a limited God would be bounded by evil, and the container is greater than the contained. So the beautiful has no limit, and desire for it never ends.
    - "There is a **place by me**" (Exod 33:21): not a measured place but the infinite, so "whoever runs therein never comes to an end of his running".
    - "I will put thee in a clift of the **rock**": the rock is **Christ** (1 Cor 10:4). The paradox: the running is also a standing still. "The more firm and immoveable a person is in the good, so much the more does he accomplish the race of virtue." The unstable are like people climbing up sand: big strides, no progress. Stability becomes "a sort of wing".

## Infinity and Epektasis

- **Against Eunomius**: Gregory is the first Father to argue at length that God is **infinite** (*apeiros*). For Greek philosophy, infinite meant indefinite, imperfect. Gregory makes it a perfection: no limit to God's goodness.
- So **perfection for creatures is endless growth**: "the one limit of perfection is that it has no limit".
- *Epektasis*, from Phil 3:13: "**reaching forth** (*epekteinomenos*) unto those things which are before". Not a restless lack but joy that always increases.
- Contrasts with Origen's satiety (*koros*): souls got bored of God and fell. For Gregory, you can't get bored of the infinite. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]] sec. 5.
    - Meredith: for Origen, temptation is always possible, even necessary ("no virtue seems... possible without them"), and relapse is always open. In the *Life of Moses* from II.219 on, relapse doesn't come up at all: Moses reaches a stage where only upward movement is possible. Gregory sits between Origen (change and sin possible even after death) and late Augustine (no sinning only in heaven, by grace).
    - For Gregory sin comes not from boredom but from the devil's envy and the mixing of evil into our free will (*Catechetical Oration* 6).

**Before and after Gregory** (Meredith)
- **Philo** had already read "my face shall not be seen" apophatically. **Origen** read the "back parts" as "the things that are done in latter days" (with 2 Cor 3, the veil removed), and never comments on the darkness of Exod 20:21 at all. Gregory seems to be the first Christian to read it as God's inaccessibility, partly under pressure from Eunomius.
- **Dionysius** draws on the *Life of Moses* in *Mystical Theology* 1 but goes further, in three ways that are more Proclus than Gregory:
    - God "beyond being", where Gregory calls God "He who is"
    - a creator–creature line blurred by outflow and return
    - purgation, illumination, union as stages, and "union" itself, which is foreign to Gregory
- Meredith's criticism: the three theophanies are incarnational (the bush above all), yet there's little warm devotion to the person of Christ, and little about grace or the sacraments.

## Essence and Energies

- In *Against Eunomius*: God's **essence** (*ousia*) is beyond names; we name God from His **energies** (*energeiai*) and powers (*dynameis*) seen in creation (Bradshaw).
- Eunomius said "unbegotten" names God's essence, so the begotten Son must be another essence. Gregory: no name grasps the essence; "unbegotten" is a property of the Father's person.
- "**Dunamis**" (power) in Gregory: God's power is His activity going out; the creature knows the power, not the essence. See [[Differences Between Gregory’s Dunamis and Thomistic Pure Act]].
