## What the nous is

- **Nous** (adjective: *noetic*) is, in Orthodox Christianity, the eye of the soul.
- The human soul is created by God as intelligent and noetic. St. Thalassios is cited: God made beings able to receive the Spirit and to know Him, and gave the senses to serve them.
- Angels have intelligence and nous; humans have reason, nous and sensory perception.
- Man is a microcosm, an expression of the whole creation. It is through the healed and corrected nous and intelligence that man knows and experiences God.

Nous and reason compared:

| | *nous* | *dianoia* / *logos* (reason) |
|---|---|---|
| knows by | direct apprehension, spiritual perception | discursive steps, concepts, inference |
| object | God, the logoi of things, spiritual realities | the world, reasoning about it |
| image | the **eye** of the soul | the soul's **reasoning faculty** |
| when healthy | sees God and is "the king" of the soul | serves the nous |
| when sick | darkened, scattered, identified with reason | becomes the only thing we know; rationalism |

Western languages lose the distinction: *nous* is usually translated "mind" or "intellect", which makes it sound like reason. The Philokalia translators use "intellect" in a special sense.

**Nepsis and *theoria*** (*The Watchful Mind*). The book's Greek title is *Neptike Theoria*, joining the two words. The translator's introduction defines *nepsis* as the state where "the intellect is awake and alert to what is happening in one's inner world, carefully watching and analyzing all thoughts and visions". It's been translated as watchfulness, wakefulness, attentiveness, vigilance, sobriety, and means all of them. Noetic prayer is said not with the tongue but with "the inner voice", the *endiathetos logos*.

- "Man's intellect is the adornment of the soul, and the mind is its splendor" (Discourse 17).
- "The intellect sees God": the more it's cleansed "by ceaseless tears", the more it sees God's glory (Discourse 17).

## The soul as an image of the Trinity

- The soul is made in the image of God. Since God is Trinitarian, mankind is nous, word and spirit, and the same holds for the soul (or heart).
- This follows St. Gregory Palamas: the Trinity is not to be understood from man, but man is understood on the basis of the Trinitarian God.
- That understanding is revelatory, not merely psychological. It is grasped fully only from within the revelation, as the saints lived it (see *theoria*).
- Man's nous, word and spirit are not hypostases, as the Persons of the Trinity are. They are activities or energies of the soul, inseparable from one another but without personal character.

## Where knowledge is validated

- The nous as the eye of the soul, which some Fathers also call the heart, is the centre of man.
    - The nous's proper place is the **heart** (not the physical organ only, but the spiritual centre of the person).
    - "Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life" (Prov 4:23). "Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God" (Matt 5:8).
- It is where true spiritual knowledge is validated, knowledge described as implanted in the nous and always co-existing with it.
- Fr. Ephraim's thesis: the Church's **dogma** comes from the experience of the illumined and deified saints. The councils' Fathers were theologians because they had noetic prayer (Evagrius: "if you pray truly, you are a theologian").
- **Heresy**, then, is theology done by a **darkened nous**: reasoning about God without purification and illumination. The answer to heresy isn't only better arguments but healing.
    - Fr. Ephraim's picture: an unclean spirit secretly pollutes the nous, like a spring, and the nous "gives whatever it has to the heart". The heretic's thirst for learning turns into the pleasure of reading and of "being someone special", and that intoxication is mistaken for revelation. "Raw knowledge is united with the rational intellect through the imagination, giving birth to theological apparitions."
    - He reads the lives of heresiarchs (Mani, Arius, Macedonius, Nestorius) as showing a disordered soul: Mani's "visionary idiosyncrasy", Nestorius's fanaticism. And he warns that repression doesn't heal anything; the heart has to be cleansed of pride and delusion before it can be the Spirit's dwelling.
    - Prayer and attention go together "like twin brothers". The aim is for the nous, the heart and the attention to "become one". Oral prayer, kept up, draws the nous into attention.
    - Even **Photios**, not a monk but running the patriarchate, practised noetic prayer and (Symeon of Thessalonica says) shone with grace "as a second Moses".
    - Critics call it "continual obsessive repetition", "mechanical", fit for simple people. Fr. Ephraim says a mind that "seeks wisdom with the passions" will find noetic prayer foolish. He also holds that the Latin teaching of **created grace** has lost the key to this experience. See [[God's Uncreated Energies]].
    - **Mission**: Porphyry of Gaza had "all his passions dead, save only that indignation which he stirred up against the enemies of the faith": anger as a natural power of the soul, once the passions are dead. **Patience** marks the true shepherd. With Elder Paisios, he rejects a "love" that won't tell heretics they're wrong. Gregory the Theologian: "it is more necessary to be cleansed than to cleanse." Put our own house in order and the heterodox will find peace "without any particular effort on our part".
    - He sees modern habits as small heresies: splitting "spiritual" from "worldly" conversation has a "Nestorianizing hue", like the Manichean and Gnostic dualism.

## The Fall of the Nous

- Before the Fall, Adam's nous was turned toward God, enjoying unceasing remembrance of Him (*The Watchful Mind*).
- After it, the nous turned outward and was **scattered** among the senses and created things. It "fell" into the passions.
- Fallen man identifies himself with his reasoning and imagination; the nous is darkened (Rom 1:21, "their foolish heart was darkened"; Eph 4:18, "having the understanding darkened").
- This is the real meaning of the Fall for hesychasm: not mainly guilt but the **sickness of the nous**. See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]].

## Healing the Nous

Three stages (the standard scheme, Romanides and Hierotheos following the Philokalia):

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Purification** (*katharsis*) | of the passions, by repentance, ascesis, the commandments |
| **Illumination** (*photismos*) | the nous freed and praying unceasingly in the heart: **noetic prayer** |
| **Deification** (*theosis*) / vision (*theoria*) | the vision of the uncreated light |

**Watchfulness** (*nepsis*) is the main tool: guarding the nous against thoughts (*logismoi*) before they become passions. St. Hesychios: "watchfulness is a spiritual method which... frees us from impassioned thoughts". See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]], [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].

Elder Joseph the Cave Dweller's image for the three stages (quoted by Fr. Ephraim): the grace of *praxis* is like starlight, illumination like the full moon, and *theoria* "like the midday sun".

**Noetic prayer** = the prayer of the nous in the heart, unceasing (1 Thess 5:17).

*The Watchful Mind* (an anonymous Athonite hieromonk, c. 1851, written under the influence of the 1782 *Philokalia*; twenty discourses) is almost entirely about this prayer:

- **Three names** (Discourse 10): **noetic prayer** generally; **prayer of the heart** when said "from the depths of your heart with great tension and inner force"; **watchful prayer** when grace touches the heart or a vision is given and "your mind's eye becomes watchful and fixed".
- **Preparation first**: fasting above all, mourning and tears, bodily hardship, self-control, humility, obedience to a spiritual father.
- **The method**, as the translator summarises it:
    1. withdraw from outward distractions
    2. clear the mind of outside matters
    3. sit on a stool, or stand bending forward a little
    4. incline the head toward the chest
    5. gather all attention into the heart, at the centre of the chest
    6. say the words "with the intellect, not with the tongue", slowly but forcefully, once per breath, holding the breath a little
    7. when that place grows warm, say it with more force
- The author's image: say it about ten times quietly and then more strongly, "just as the cicada ends its song with a more pronounced and melodic voice".
- The tone runs from the "turbulent agony" of the first discourses (the prayer as a sword fighting demons in the chest, pain, even spitting blood) to the "joyful brightness" of the end: sweetness in the mouth while fasting, visits of the Holy Trinity with tears and compunction, a vision of Christ celebrating the Liturgy.
- **Discernment**: the mind cleansed by ceaseless prayer, "the mother of tears", can tell which thoughts come from God and which from demons (Discourse 14). A demonic fantasy prints itself at once on a relaxed heart but "has no place in a crushed heart" (Discourse 16): "a sacrifice to God is a crushed spirit" (Ps 50:19 LXX).
- The *startsi* in other books (*The Art of Prayer*) warn against the physical method without a guide; this author teaches it freely but inside a life of fasting and obedience.

- The hesychast "brings the nous down into the heart": attention gathered in, prayer in the heart.
- Palamas (*Triads* I.2) defends this against Barlaam: the body isn't evil, and the nous can be "inside" the body as in its instrument.
- **Mission** too comes from noetic prayer: the saints who converted peoples were men of prayer (Fr. Ephraim).
