> "'Behold,' she said, 'I am that which must always overcome itself.'" (Life to Zarathustra, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* II.12)

> "Let us at least talk of this, you who are wisest, grim though it may be. Remaining silent is grimmer; all truths that are kept silent become toxic." (II.12)

The phrase "will to power" occurs only a handful of times in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*. It appears in passing in Part I ("On the Thousand Goals and One"), it returns briefly in "On Redemption", and in between it is the subject of one speech, "On Self-Overcoming" (Part II, chapter 12). This is the first place in Nietzsche's published work where the will to power is stated as a teaching about all living things, and its readers have treated it as the philosophical core of the book. Lampert calls it "the decisive speech" (p. 111). Strauss calls it "the most explicitly philosophic speech hitherto" (p. 103). Yet the speech is strange. It is addressed only to "you who are wisest", it argues by means of a story about what Life whispered to Zarathustra, and it ends by asking the wise to "at least talk" about something grim. This note follows the speech closely, then sets six commentators beside it: Laurence Lampert, Leo Strauss, Stanley Rosen, Heinrich Meier, Matthew Meyer, and Douglas Burnham with Martin Jesinghausen. They disagree on three questions. Who is the speech for? Is the will to power a claim about the world, or an interpretation? And whose "self" overcomes itself? Quotations from the text follow Graham Parkes's Oxford translation. The doctrine in general is treated in [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]].

### 1. Where the Speech Stands

Part II opens with Zarathustra returning to his disciples on the Blessed Isles, because a dream has shown him that his teaching is being distorted. He gives a run of speeches against pity, priests, the virtuous, the rabble, the tarantulas (preachers of equality) and "the famous wise ones" (II.8), who serve the people and their superstitions. Then come three songs: the Night Song, the Dance Song and the Tomb Song. "On Self-Overcoming" follows directly. Meier counts the addresses: "the famous wise ones" are named six times in II.8, while II.12 says "you who are wisest" seven times (p. 57). The audience has changed. II.8 attacked the hired intellectuals of an age. II.12 speaks to the philosophers who made the values that the age lives by.

The three songs prepare for this. Meier reads them as a set: "The night-song makes God audible; the tomb-song, man. The dance-song in the middle makes the philosopher visible" (p. 57). Strauss reads them as expressing "the fullness and depth of life", and draws a conclusion: the philosopher of the future "will be a synthesis of the philosopher and the poet, especially the lyric poet" (p. 103). Lampert calls the speech the fruit of the songs (p. 111). In the Tomb Song Zarathustra says he is invulnerable "only in my heel". Parkes notes that this makes him "a kind of anti-type to the hero Achilles", and Rosen adds that Zarathustra is closer to Odysseus, "the paradigm for the philosopher, who can walk among the dead" (p. 158). The man who sings that his will cannot be buried is the man who now explains what the will is.

### 2. The Speech Step by Step

**Thinkability.** The speech opens with a question and a correction. "'Will to truth' you call it, you who are wisest, that which drives you and puts you in heat? Will to the thinkability of all beings: thus I call your will!" The wise doubt "with healthy suspicion" that beings are thinkable, so they want to make them so: "they shall fit and bend themselves to you! ... Smooth shall they become and subject to the spirit, as its mirror and reflected image." That is "your entire will, you who are wisest, as a will to power". It holds "even when you talk of good and evil and of valuations". Their hidden hope is this: "You still want to create the world before which you can kneel."

**The bark on the river.** The people are "like a river on which a bark drifts along: and in the bark, solemn and disguised, sit the valuations". The wise put those passengers in the boat "and gave them grandeur and proud names". The river carries the boat on. The danger to their values is not the river. It is "that will itself, the will to power–the unexhausted procreative life-will".

**Three things heard.** Zarathustra then says how he came to know this. He followed the living along "the greatest and the smallest paths", and "with a hundredfold mirror" caught Life's look when its mouth was closed. He heard three things. "All that is living is something that obeys." Second, "whoever cannot obey himself will be commanded." Third, "commanding is harder than obeying", not only because the commander carries the weight of all who obey, but because "always when it commands, the living puts its own self at risk. ... For its own law it must become judge and avenger and sacrificial victim."

**The weaker in the fortress.** Why does the living obey and command? "Where I found the living, there I found will to power; and even in the will of one who serves I found a will to be master." The weaker serve the stronger to master those weaker still. The greatest yields as well, and risks "life itself", in "a dice-playing for death". "By secret paths the weaker slinks into the fortress and into the very heart of the more powerful–and there steals power."

**Life's secret.** Life then tells Zarathustra her secret: "I am that which must always overcome itself." Whatever she creates and loves, she must soon oppose. Even "you, who understand, are only a path and footstep of my will: verily, my will to power even walks on the feet of your will to truth!" The rival formula is rejected: "He surely missed the mark who shot at the truth with the words 'will to existence': this will–does not exist! ... Only, where Life is, there too is will: though not will to life, but–thus I teach you–will to power!" The phrase "will to existence" points at Spinoza's *conatus* and Schopenhauer's will to life.

**The highest evil.** Zarathustra draws the moral for the wise. "Good and evil that are not transitory–there is no such thing! From out of themselves they must overcome themselves again and again." Whoever must be a creator "must first be an annihilator and shatter values. Thus does the highest evil belong to the highest good: but this latter is the creative." Parkes notes that the word for creator here is *Schöpfer*, "as in 'God the creator'", not the usual *Schaffender*. The speech ends: "There is many a house still to build!"

Parkes adds three details. Life's "Behold" is in the singular, addressed to Zarathustra alone, and then shifts to the plural "you call it". Zarathustra's position as he goes on "to solve the riddle of your hearts" is like that of Socrates in the *Symposium* (212b), reporting to the other guests what the priestess Diotima taught him about love. And Parkes, like Lampert, translates *Art* as "way" ("the way of all the living"), not "kind" or "species".

### 3. Lampert: The Decisive Speech

For Lampert the speech is the turning point in Zarathustra's education. In his introduction he says that II.12 "elaborates his fundamental discovery of will to power into a comprehensive teaching about beings as a whole. This new teaching discloses that the highest human beings are philosophers who exercise the most spiritual will to power over all beings. Consequently, this chapter is addressed only to philosophers, 'you who are wisest,' for the new teaching threatens to terminate the dominion that their most spiritual will to power has enabled them to exert over mankind" (p. 6).

- **A conversation among rivals.** Quoting the second *Untimely Meditation*, Lampert describes a "republic of geniuses" in which "one giant calls to the other across the bleak interval of the ages". The speech "provides privileged access to a conversation among philosophers from which others are excluded ... a contest among mighty rivals" (p. 111).
- **Plato behind the fable.** Zarathustra avoids the words "philosophy", "being" and "nature". But "the hidden reason for the moral metaphysics of the wisest is their desire for a world before which they could kneel", and the demand was first made by Socrates and Plato (p. 112). The speech "translates Nietzsche's understanding of the Socratic-Platonic roots of Western civilization into Zarathustra's idiom of fable" (p. 113). The philosophers became "the secret spiritual rulers" of their age, loading the boat of values. Even the modern protest against Plato "is itself a species of Platonism". Zarathustra's insight "is a celebration of them and their power, and precisely in that celebration he announces his rivalry" (p. 113).
- **The commander.** Of the three things heard, the third matters most: the commander "experiments and risks himself". "The commander is the ungrounded ground-giver" (p. 114). Lampert distinguishes this from Hegel's struggle for recognition. And the weaker creeping into the fortress describes Zarathustra himself, who has crept into Life's fortress and stolen her secret (pp. 114–15).
- **From scepticism to a teaching.** Earlier Zarathustra had said that "the belly of being does not speak to man, unless as man" (I.3). Now he drops that "virtuous skepticism" and speaks about beings as a whole (p. 116). Lampert links the speech to *Beyond Good and Evil* 259: will to power, "as theory, a novelty – as reality, the primordial fact of all history", and to the "tyrannical" act of founding a philosophy in BGE 9 (p. 117).
- **Interpretation, and the highest natures.** The doctrine is also, by its own account, "only interpretation" (BGE 22). But "the nature of nature comes to light in the highest natures. Their commanding is an obeying" (p. 118). Lampert's conclusion is that "philosophy, imperial philosophy, is surrender to life's passion" (p. 119).
- **Against the moralizing reading.** The speech "might seem to encourage a limited interpretation of will to power as simply an injunction to human beings to overcome themselves, to practice an honorable self-mastery ... But 'On Self-Overcoming' is not characterized by exhortation, nor is it addressed to everyone" (p. 120).

### 4. Strauss: The Intelligibility of the Whole

Strauss taught the speech in a 1959 Chicago seminar and called it "of particular importance, from our point of view perhaps the most important" (p. 95). His reading turns on the word "thinkability".

- **Thinkability, not truth.** "They did not will the truth, they willed thinkability" (p. 95). Philosophy aims to know everything, and that implies that everything can be known. "How do we know that everything is knowable? Maybe this is only a dogmatic assumption made by the Greeks and inherited by us. This is what Nietzsche is driving at" (p. 96). Strauss states it in a sentence: "The intelligibility of the whole is the basic premise of philosophy and is an unsupported dogmatic premise" (p. 96).
- **The biblical alternative.** The Bible makes the highest being "radically mysterious". But that does not help, from Nietzsche's side, "because the biblical God is perfectly intelligible to himself", so men will try to imitate him (p. 96). Against both, "Nietzsche asserts that being as being is elusive". Strauss thinks this is "today much more credible" than the classical view, because it is implied in the endless progress of modern science (p. 96).
- **Creativity and elusiveness.** The deeper reason is the self. "Creativity implies elusiveness ... since the self is the origin of all meaning, it implies that the ground of everything is elusive" (p. 97). Life, so understood, is not self-preservation but will to power (p. 97).
- **True or created?** This is Strauss's sharpest question. "Is the doctrine of the will to power itself an expression of the self-consciousness of the will to power? ... Is the doctrine of the will to power meant to be objectively true or is it a creation?" His answer is that Nietzsche's philosophy differs from earlier ones "not only in its content or substance, but in its mode as well. It is not simply a doctrine which is meant to be objectively true" (p. 104).
- **Self-consciousness.** What sets Nietzsche apart is that he knows something his predecessors did not: "all earlier philosophers did not know that the will to power was at the root to their will to truth. Nietzsche knows it" (p. 106). The Greek founders contemplated "what is always and unchanging". For Nietzsche, "the seeming sempiternal or eternal is only the decayed temporal" (p. 105).
- **Subjectivity.** Strauss puts the epistemological stakes plainly: "the question is not objectivity versus subjectivity, but narrow and poor subjectivity against broad and rich subjectivity" (p. 95).

### 5. Rosen: Being as Evaluation

Rosen reads the speech as the point where Nietzsche merges theory and practice. Zarathustra "passes directly and without justification from thinkability to good and evil. In other words, he recognizes no split between theory and practico-production in the history of philosophy. Being is an evaluation, not an essence or a formal structure" (p. 158).

- **The boat is no safer than the river.** If values are set by the will to power, "each evaluation will be overthrown in its turn". "The boat carrying the values on the river of Becoming is hardly more substantial than the river itself" (p. 159).
- **The mirror.** The hundredfold mirror recalls the *Phaedo*. Like Socrates there, Zarathustra "does not look directly at the sun, or at beings, but neither does he look into logoi, as does Socrates" (p. 159). The mirror protects, as Perseus' shield did against Medusa, and lets him "see without being seen".
- **Order within the will.** Rosen draws a corrective from the three things heard: "the will to power contains an inner limitation, or a structure of command and obedience. It is not simply an undisciplined and chaotic outpouring of force. Chaos is at the heart of all things, but domination requires organization and rank-ordering" (p. 159).
- **Self-destruction.** The commander must suit his regime to those who obey, and is "threatened with destruction by the act of commanding itself". He becomes "judge and avenger, and victim of its own law", like the pale criminal of Part I. Creation needs destruction: "Creation is possible only because of the pervasiveness of the transitory" (p. 160).
- **Grace.** The next speech, "On the Sublime Ones" (II.13), says: "When power becomes gracious and descends into the visible, I call such descent beauty." Rosen calls this "a necessary corrective to the rhetoric of violence that is so often associated by Nietzsche with the doctrine of the will to power" (p. 160).

### 6. Meier: Not the Solution but the Problem

Meier asks what the speech does to the philosophers it addresses. Zarathustra does not accuse them of lying. He "wants merely ... to show them that they do not understand themselves adequately" (p. 58).

- **Life on Sinai.** "Life makes itself known to Zarathustra, as God made Himself known to Moses. But in contrast to the theologoumenon of Sinai, life's word points to the necessity that inheres in it and makes knowledge possible: 'Behold, I am that which must always overcome itself'" (p. 60). The echo is Exodus 3:14, "I am that I am". Zarathustra, like the prophets, honours the gods with his own wisdom, "except that he does not elevate life into a Goddess, and that he does not gain from his teaching a commandment for the unwise, but a critique of the wisest" (p. 60).
- **The danger is the teaching itself.** "Not the river is your danger ... but that will itself." Meier takes this further: Zarathustra's own insight is the danger, to the values of the wise but also "to the teaching of the overman as the meaning of the earth" (p. 59).
- **Did the wisest know?** The closing appeal ("Let us at least talk of this") raises a question. "Did they merely not talk about it? Did they veil their insight under edifying teachings?" (p. 61). Perhaps the great philosophers already knew, and kept silent.
- **Turning the will against itself.** Meier's central claim: "When Zarathustra exposes the will to power that is effective in the will to truth, he indicates to the wisest ones the necessity of turning the will to power against itself ... his will to get over and beyond the will to power designates its highest possibility. Both times the will to power is not the last word" (p. 61). And: "The will to power is not the solution, but the core of the problem for the philosopher" (p. 62).
- **To stand, not kneel.** II.11 (the invulnerable will), II.12 (the will to power in the will to truth) and II.13 (the "unharnessed" will) form a triad. The goal is "to stand, not kneel before the world, to pause before it with unharnessed will" (p. 62). The concept "serves the critique and self-knowledge of the philosopher" (p. 62). Meier counts seven uses of "will to power" in II.12 and nine in the whole book (p. 60, n. 77).

### 7. Meyer: A Cosmological Principle

Meyer reads the will to power less as a confession to philosophers than as a thesis about nature. "The will to power plays a lesser role in *Zarathustra*. The view appears in three passages" (I "Goals", II "Self-Overcoming", II "Redemption") (p. 80).

- **Widening scope.** In Part I it explains the origin of values, so it is "largely limited to the psychological realm". In II.12 it seems to widen "to the biological realm". But "once we interpret the concept in context, we can see that Nietzsche always thought of the will to power as a cosmological concept" (p. 80).
- **Schopenhauer.** The key is the target named in the speech. A notebook entry reads: "Will to live? I have always found only will to power in its place" (p. 80). For Schopenhauer "life" means the whole visible world, organic and inorganic. So if the will to power replaces the will to life, it too is cosmic (p. 81).
- **Naturalism, not metaphysics.** That does not make it a thing-in-itself. Schopenhauer had argued that natural science leaves two gaps: forces that are themselves inexplicable, and an infinite chain of causes. Nietzsche fills the first with the will to power and the second with eternal recurrence. "Unlike the will to life, the will to power is not a unitary entity behind or beyond appearances. Instead, there are multiple, interrelated wills to power" (pp. 82–83).
- **A postulate.** Meyer accepts that "Nietzsche openly embraces the idea that the will to power is a self-conscious interpretation (BGE 22)". It is "how the world looks to him in his mirror". So the doctrines are "either unverified hypotheses or unverifiable postulates that we should accept because they most promote life" (p. 84).
- **No exit.** Their effect is to close every door Schopenhauer opened. Any attempt to deny the will "is itself a manifestation ... of the will to power (GM III:28)". The upshot: "there is just this world and nothing besides" (p. 85).

### 8. Burnham and Jesinghausen: Whose Self?

The Edinburgh guide calls II.12 the "first sustained and general treatment of the will to power" (p. 96). Its addressees are not disciples but those who create values, "perhaps even without being aware of it". The guide reads the speech's rejection of "will to existence" and "will to life" as a rejection of Spinozism and of quasi-evolutionary biology (p. 96).

- **Two senses of "bend".** Making beings thinkable means making them "bend", "both in the sense of being malleable to the will, and also bending in the sense of reverence" (p. 97). This is not something special to philosophers: "it is what all will to power does". The weaker stealing power recalls "the (infamous) notion of a 'slave revolution' in morality" (BGE 46) (p. 97).
- **The self is not a person.** The guide's most distinctive claim is about the title. "We should note that the 'self' in the title of the section does not refer to a particular human being struggling with his or her inherited set of drives ... Rather, this section is much more straightforwardly 'ontological' in character. The 'self' being referred to is the mode of life of a people, or the existence of a system of good and evil" (pp. 97–98).
- **The overhuman.** "The overhuman is not a mode of life (a species), but the situation where life spiritualised in human activity participates in its own continual development" (p. 98). The guide links this to the "great health" of GS 382, which one "gives up again and again", and finds in it the book's narrative rhythm of advance and retreat (pp. 98–99).

The readings divide on whether the speech states a doctrine or exposes a problem. Meyer gives it the most doctrinal sense: a cosmology that completes naturalism. Lampert gives it a political sense: a new founding by a philosopher who knows that philosophers rule. Strauss, Rosen and Meier all find a difficulty inside it. If the will to truth is a will to power, so is the doctrine that says so, and it cannot simply claim to be true. Strauss puts this as a question about the mode of Nietzsche's philosophy. Rosen sees it as instability: no value can outlast the will that made it. Meier sees it as a task: the philosopher must overcome even the will to power. The text supports the second group. Life's own word is that she "must always overcome" herself, and the speech ends not with a law but with "let us at least talk". Burnham and Jesinghausen add a needed correction to popular readings. The "self" of self-overcoming is first of all a people's table of values, not a person's character, and the moralizing reading, which Lampert also rejects, has little basis in the text.

### Conclusion

"On Self-Overcoming" introduces the will to power by an argument about philosophy, not about biology. The wise say they want truth. Zarathustra says they want the world to be thinkable, smooth, a mirror of their spirit, and so a world before which they can kneel. What drives that wish is a will that commands and obeys, risks itself, slips into the fortress of the stronger, and creates only by destroying what it made. Life's word to Zarathustra, "I am that which must always overcome itself", turns this into a claim about all living things. The commentators agree that the speech marks a turning point in the book. Lampert sees the moment Zarathustra becomes a rival of Plato; Strauss, the moment the intelligibility of the whole becomes doubtful; Rosen, the merging of being and value; Meier, a critique that the philosopher must finally turn on himself; Meyer, the first statement of a cosmology; Burnham and Jesinghausen, an ontology of peoples and their values. They disagree about what the speech establishes, and that disagreement reflects its own closing gesture. Zarathustra does not issue a commandment. He asks the wisest to talk about a grim truth, because "all truths that are kept silent become toxic", and he ends with "many a house still to build".
