> "And it came to pass after these things, that God did tempt Abraham, and said unto him, Abraham: and he said, Behold, here I am." (Gen 22:1)

Erich Auerbach (1892–1957) was a German Jewish philologist, removed from his Marburg chair by the Nazis. He wrote *Mimesis* in Istanbul during the war, without libraries or journals, so the book has no footnotes. As he says in the Epilogue, "it is quite possible that the book owes its existence to just this lack of a rich and specialized library." The question is how Western literature came to represent **everyday reality seriously**. His answer: the classical world kept the high style for kings and tragedy and the low style for comedy. It was **the story of Christ**, "with its ruthless mixture of everyday reality and the highest and most sublime tragedy", that broke that rule.

## 1. Odysseus' Scar against the Binding of Isaac (ch. 1)

**Homer: everything in the foreground**
- In *Odyssey* 19 the old nurse Euryclea washes the disguised Odysseus' feet and recognises a scar. At the moment of crisis, Homer breaks off for about seventy lines: the boar hunt in which Odysseus got the scar as a boy, his grandfather Autolycus, the banquet, the wound, the recovery.
- This is **not suspense**. Goethe and Schiller called it "the retarding element". The digression makes us forget the foot-washing. Homer "knows no background"; whatever he narrates "is for the time being the only present, and fills both the stage and the reader's mind completely".
- The real reason is that the Homeric style needs "to leave nothing which it mentions half in darkness". It has "externalized, uniformly illuminated phenomena". Feelings are spoken out in orderly syntax. Even in the moment of recognition Homer tells us it is with his **right** hand that Odysseus grips her throat.
- **No second meaning.** "Homer can be analyzed… but he cannot be interpreted." Later allegorists tried, but "the interpretations are forced and foreign". Compare [[How Philosophers Saved Homer: Allegory from the Stoics to the Byzantines]].

**Genesis 22: "fraught with background"**
- "Where are the two speakers? We are not told." God comes from no stated place, gives no reasons, holds no council like Zeus, and "utters nothing but a name… without a descriptive epithet".
- "Here am I" (*hinneni*) does not tell us where Abraham is. It states "a moral position in respect to God".
- The servants, the ass, the wood and the knife have no adjectives. The three-day journey is "a silent progress through the indeterminate", "a holding of the breath". "Early in the morning" is given "for the sake of its ethical significance", to show prompt obedience. Isaac gets a single descriptor, "thine only son, whom thou lovest", only to show how terrible the test is.
- Speech hides rather than reveals. "So they went both of them together", twice. "Everything remains unexpressed."
- **The summary contrast.** Homer has foreground, light, full expression and little suspense. Genesis has "certain parts brought into high relief, others left obscure, abruptness, suggestive influence of the unexpressed… multiplicity of meanings and the need for interpretation, universal-historical claims… preoccupation with the problematic".

**Depth of character**
- Homer's heroes "wake every morning as if it were the first day of their lives". Odysseus comes home "exactly the same as he was when he left".
- Biblical people have **layers and a history**. Abraham's soul "is torn between desperate rebellion and hopeful expectation". "What a road, what a fate, lie between the Jacob who cheated his father… and the old man whose favorite son has been torn to pieces". David the harper becomes the old king warmed by Abishag, "and he knew her not".
- **Humiliation and exaltation.** "The poor beggar Odysseus is only masquerading, but Adam is really cast down, Jacob really a refugee, Joseph really in the pit". God "bends them and kneads them" without destroying them.

**History, not legend**
- Legend "runs far too smoothly". It has stiff-necked persecutors facing stiff-necked martyrs. History is "contradictory… confused".
- The David narratives have the cross-currents of real history: Absalom's revolt, Joab, the two messengers. "It is impossible to doubt the historicity of the information conveyed."

**The "tyrannical" claim to truth**
- Homer "lied to give pleasure". The biblical writer "had to believe in the objective truth of the story… passionately", or else be "a political liar". Auerbach finds the rationalist alternative "psychologically absurd".
- The Bible "insists that it is the only real world, is destined for autocracy". "They seek to subject us, and if we refuse to be subjected we are rebels."
- So the Bible both *needs* and *demands* interpretation. It is universal history, from creation to the last days, and everything else has to be fitted into it. Paul and the Fathers did this by reading the whole Jewish tradition "as a succession of figures prognosticating the appearance of Christ".

**Sublime in the everyday.** Homer keeps domestic life idyllic and keeps tragedy among aristocrats. In Genesis "the sublime, tragic, and problematic take shape precisely in the domestic and commonplace": Cain and Abel, Abraham, Sarah and Hagar, Rebekah, Jacob and Esau.

## 2. Petronius, Tacitus and Peter's Denial (ch. 2)

**Petronius**
- At Trimalchio's dinner a freedman gossips about Fortunata ("she measures money by the bushel… you wouldn't have accepted a piece of bread from her hands").
- It is brilliant, subjective, even proto-Proustian realism. But it is **comic**, and it is written "from above", for a cultivated elite who will savour every vulgarity.

**Tacitus**
- In the Pannonian mutiny (*Annals* 1.16–17), the ringleader Percennius lists the soldiers' real grievances: thirty years' service, ten *asses* a day, swamp land for veterans.
- But Tacitus dismisses them in advance in moral terms: licence, hope of plunder, "the most depraved". Percennius "speaks Tacitean", not soldiers' slang.
- Ancient historiography "does not see forces, it sees vices and virtues". It is "ethical and rhetorical". No ancient author could have asked Rostovtzeff's question, how to account for the proletariat in Italy. Thucydides too offers considerations "statically aprioristic and ethical in content, on such matters as human nature or fate". Compare [[Thucydides on Human Nature and Power]].

**Peter in the High Priest's courtyard (Mark 14:66–72)**
- Peter, the only disciple who dared follow, stands by the fire. A servant girl challenges him. His Galilean accent gives him away. Mark does not even say how he got out.
- Here "the rule of differentiated styles cannot possibly apply". The scene is low (servants, soldiers, a fisherman) and yet it is "replete with problem and tragedy". Peter is "the image of man in the highest and deepest and most tragic sense".
- **Pendulation.** Harnack's term, *Pendelausschlag*, the pendulum's swing. Peter fell deeper than the rest *because* he followed further: "Because his faith was deep, but not deep enough". Then remorse swings him back further still. "A hero of such weakness, who yet derives the highest force from his very weakness".
- **It fits no ancient genre.** It is "too serious for comedy, too contemporary and everyday for tragedy, politically too insignificant for history". Its short, direct exchange ("And thou also wast with Jesus of Nazareth"; "Surely thou art one of them: for thou art a Galilean") has no parallel in any ancient historian.
- **A movement from the depths.** The Gospels show "the birth of a spiritual movement in the depths of the common people". Random fishermen, publicans, Samaritans and adulteresses meet Jesus, and each response is "a matter of profound seriousness, and very often tragic". The Gospel "speaks to everybody… Even ignoring it implies taking sides."
- New categories appear: the era of law and the era of grace, love, power, spirit. Even justice becomes dynamic (Rom 3:21–26). This is movement "upward", toward the end of time, not along history's horizontal plane.
- **Written from within.** Tacitus and Petronius "look down from above". Mark writes "from within the emergent growths and directly for everyman".
- **The price.** Figural exegesis sometimes swallows the sensory event. In Adam's sleep and the pierced side of Christ, the Church is born from the wound as Eve from the rib, and "the sensory occurrence pales before the power of the figural meaning".

## 3. Alypius at the Games and the *Sermo Humilis* (ch. 3)

**Late antiquity.** Auerbach first shows its dark and frozen style: Ammianus' gruesome splendour, and Jerome's ascetic propaganda, "somberly suicidal… hostility to life".

**Augustine, *Confessions* VI.8**
- Alypius, a law student in Rome, detests the gladiatorial shows. Friends drag him in "with a friendly violence". He boasts: "Though you drag my body… can you force me to give my mind and lend my eyes?" He shuts his eyes.
- At a great shout he opens them, "and was struck with a deeper wound in his soul than the other… in his body". "He drank in madness unconsciously." "Nor was he now the same he came in, but was one of the throng he came unto." He returned "not only with those who first enticed him, but also before them… and to draw in others". Until God "taughtest him not to repose confidence in himself, but in Thee".
- **The fall of ancient self-reliance.** Alypius stands for "the entire rational individualistic culture of classical antiquity: Plato and Aristotle, the Stoa and Epicurus", swept away "in one powerful assault". Like Peter, he falls deeper the higher he stood, and like Peter he will rise. Christianity can fight "magical intoxication" because it too is "a movement from the depths", with "a more ordered, a more human magic, filled with more hope".
- **Biblical parataxis.** "Aperuit oculos, et percussus est". Clauses are joined with "and", as in "fiat lux, et facta est lux", not subordinated classically. The drama of an inner about-face is told in the Bible's syntax.

**The *sermo humilis***
- "Christ had not come as a hero and king but as a human being of the lowest social station." "That the King of Kings was treated as a low criminal… completely destroys the aesthetics of the separation of styles."
- A new style is born, a low style that "encroaches upon the deepest and the highest, the sublime and the eternal". Augustine, at home in both worlds, saw the problem clearly. In *De doctrina christiana* IV he discusses the cup of cold water of Matt 10:42.
- He also says Scripture "invites all with humble speech" while hiding mysteries for the patient. It is to be read as a child grows (*Confessions* III.5).

**Figura**
- Figural interpretation "establishes a connection between two events or persons in such a way that the first signifies not only itself but also the second, while the second involves or fulfills the first". "Both, being real events or persons, are within temporality."
- Isaac's sacrifice and Christ's are linked "neither temporally nor causally" but "vertically" through Providence. "In the eyes of God" the event is "something eternal, something omni-temporal".
- This "annihilated" the classical mind "down to the very structure of its language". Its subtle subordinating syntax became superfluous once the only important connection ran up to God.
- In *City of God* XV–XVIII Augustine tries to supply a continuous horizontal history as well, filling the Bible's gaps "to give the highest measure of rational plausibility". Compare [[Augustine's City of God]] and [[Types of Christ in the OT]].

## 4. Adam and Eve on a Twelfth-Century Stage (ch. 7)

**The Anglo-Norman *Mystère d'Adam***
- Adam is "a good man, a French peasant or burgher", sure of himself and of God's command, who has "his wife under his thumb". The Devil cannot move him.
- So the serpent teaches Eve to stop arguing and present a *fait accompli*. She picks the apple and presses him, "Manjue, Adam!", four times. His confidence collapses. He takes it with a touching line: "io t'en crerrai, tu es ma per" ("I will trust you, you are my equal"). Bernard's phrase fits: *perniciosa misericors*, a ruinous compassion.
- The first man–woman dialogue of world history becomes a scene "of simplest everyday reality". It is low in style and sublime in subject.
- **The figural frame.** God appears as *Salvator* and then as *Figura*. After the Fall, Adam already knows that "no one will help me now except the Son who will come forth from Mary". In God there is no distinction of times. Augustine says God has not foreknowledge but simply knowledge. The play ends with a procession of prophets foretelling Christ. "There is but one place, the world; and but one action, man's fall and redemption." Compare [[Adam as a Type of Christ and Eve as a Type of the Church]] and [[Adam and Eve Sin]].

**Sublimitas and humilitas**
- Bernard: "O humilitas, O sublimitas!" The Bride is black but comely (Song 1:5). Christ, with nowhere to ascend because "beyond God there is nothing", "found by descending a way to grow".
- Suger of Saint-Denis: *mens hebes ad verum per materialia surgit*, the dull mind rises to truth through material things.

**St Francis.** His life is a series of public scenes: giving his clothes back to his father, dying naked on the earth, Christmas at Greccio, sitting in the ashes at Easter "like a Minorite". His letter to a minister is all *et*… *et*… parataxis: love the sinning brother "if afterwards he appears a thousand times". Jacopone da Todi's lament *Donna del paradiso* ("O son, son, son!… why do you hide from the breast which gave you milk?") makes the Passion a dramatic human scene.

## 5. Dante: When the Figure Eclipses Its Fulfilment (ch. 8)

- In *Inferno* 10, Farinata, proud and unbroken in his flaming tomb, and Cavalcante, weeping for his son and "the sweet light", show the paradox of Dante's realism. Hegel's phrase for it is a "changeless existence" into which Dante plunges "the living world of human action".
- **Figural fulfilment.** In the beyond, souls attain "the true reality of their being". Their earthly careers were "only the figure of this fulfillment". God's judgment consists in "the absolute realization of a particular earthly personality in the place definitively assigned to it". Cato of Utica, guardian of earthly liberty, becomes the guardian of Purgatory's liberty. The Roman Empire is the figure of the Kingdom (*quella Roma onde Cristo è Romano*). See [[The Case for One Ruler: Aquinas, Dante, Maistre, Kuehnelt-Leddihn]].
- The **style** is mixed, comic (*Commedia*) and Christian. Yet Dante, taught by Virgil, keeps the *gravitas* of the high style even for the grotesque.
- **The turn.** "It was precisely the Christian idea of the indestructibility of the entire human individual which made this possible." But Dante's power is such that "figure surpasses fulfillment". "What actually moves us is not that God has damned them, but that the one is unbroken and the other mourns." **"The image of man eclipses the image of God."** "Dante's work made man's Christian-figural being a reality, and destroyed it in the very process of realizing it." The beyond becomes "a stage for human beings and human passions". This is the seed of modern secular realism.

## 6. The Epilogue: Two Breaks with the Rule of Styles

- The first break came through **Christ**. The second came with Stendhal and Balzac, who made "random individuals from daily life in their dependence upon current historical circumstances" the subject of tragedy.
- Between them, the classicists of the 16th and 17th centuries re-erected the barriers.
- The two breaks yield opposite pictures. The medieval one is **figural**: "an occurrence on earth signifies not only itself but at the same time another… a oneness within the divine plan". The modern one is immanent and historical.

## 8. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What Auerbach saw rightly**
- His deepest observation is theological, though he puts it in terms of style. The **Incarnation** is the reason a servant girl's question can be the hinge of world history. "He hath put down the mighty… and exalted them of low degree" (Luke 1:52). God "hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise" (1 Cor 1:27). Athanasius makes the same point in *On the Incarnation*: the Word took a body "of no different sort from ours", so the humble and bodily became the place of salvation. See [[On the Incarnation: Main Argument]].
- **"Fraught with background"** is a fine description of how Scripture speaks of God. He is present but not comprehended, "something of him appears, he always extends into depths". This is the biblical root of **apophatic theology**. God is not Zeus in a council chamber but the One who "made darkness his secret place" (Ps 18:11). See [[Apophatic and Cataphatic Theology]].
- His account of **figura** is the Fathers' own. Typology keeps both poles real. Isaac really went up Moriah, and Christ really went up Golgotha. That is exactly what distinguishes patristic typology from pagan allegory, which dissolves the story into ideas. See [[Types of Christ in the OT]] and [[The Eucharist in Type and Fulfilment]].
- **Peter's denial and Alypius' fall** are the anthropology of repentance. The fall happens through self-confidence: "so much the weaker in that it presumed on itself". The rising comes by trusting God. "Let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall" (1 Cor 10:12). See [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]].

**Where I part from him**
- Auerbach treats Genesis as "legendary" and Peter's Easter experience as "visions" produced by remorse. That is a methodological agnosticism the text itself resists. His own observation that the narratives are too rough-edged for legend is better evidence than his conclusion. It is an argument for eyewitness testimony, as the Gospels claim (Luke 1:1–4; 2 Pet 1:16). See [[The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus]].
- His word "tyrannical" for Scripture's claim is a modern's reaction. The Fathers would say the claim is total because **reality is one**: Christ is "the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world" (Rev 13:8). A true account of the world cannot be one story among many.
- **Dante's "eclipse".** Auerbach is right that Western Christian art, from Dante through the Renaissance, increasingly made the human figure the centre and the divine order a backdrop. Orthodox iconography resists exactly that drift. In the icon the saint is *fulfilled* humanity, transfigured, frontal and lit from within, never a human drama against a divine setting. In Auerbach's terms the icon keeps the fulfilment above the figure. See [[Theology of the Icon]] and [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- **Figural reading of history** is not only medieval. It is how the Orthodox Liturgy still reads time: "Today is the beginning of our salvation", "Today he who hung the earth upon the waters is hung upon the Cross". The feasts make past events present, the same "omni-temporal" figure Auerbach describes, kept alive.
