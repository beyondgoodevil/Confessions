Ancient readers didn't just admire Homer, they **interrogated** him. Why does Zeus send Agamemnon a lying dream? How can fate be "unalterable" in the *Iliad* but broken by Aegisthus in the *Odyssey*? Why does Homer bother saying "white milk"? Problems like these were called *zētēmata* or *problēmata*, and the answers *luseis*, "solutions". The fullest surviving collection is by **Porphyry of Tyre**, the philosopher later famous as Plotinus' editor and the Church's sharpest pagan critic. His guiding rule was to **explain Homer from Homer**. Here's how it works:

### 1. Who Porphyry Was

- Born in **Tyre in 234 AD**; his Syrian name meant "King", and his teacher **Longinus** turned it into *Porphyrios*, after the royal purple (Eunapius).
- Studied in Athens with the grammarian Apollonius, the mathematician Demetrius and above all Cassius Longinus, "the preeminent critic of his age".
- In **263** he joined **Plotinus' school in Rome**. After five and a half years he became severely depressed and, on Plotinus' advice, went to Sicily. Eunapius says that, overcome by philosophy, "he hated his own body and the fact that he was human". Late in life he married a friend's widow (the *Letter to Marcella*). His last datable work is the *Life of Plotinus* (301).
- Scholars usually date the *Homeric Questions* **before** he met Plotinus, because there's no Neoplatonic allegory in it. In the dedication Porphyry calls it a sort of "preliminary exercise" and says he's "deferring greater studies on Homer". His later Homer works, *On the Cave of the Nymphs* and *On the Styx*, read the *Odyssey* as an allegory of the soul escaping the body (see [[How Philosophers Saved Homer: Allegory from the Stoics to the Byzantines]]). MacPhail notes, following Lamberton, that this dating is only probable.

### 2. The Genre: Problems and Solutions

- **The critics.** The questions focus on "inconsistencies, contradictions, illogicalities, improbabilities, and violations of propriety". They range from the moral attacks of **Xenophanes and Plato** to the "pettifoggery" of **Zoilus of Amphipolis**, "the so-called Scourge of Homer". Porphyry explains that Zoilus, a pupil of Isocrates, "wrote Against Homer for practice, as public speakers were also accustomed to practice on the poets" (HQ ad Il. 10.276).
- **Aristotle** is the theorist. *Poetics* 25 lists five kinds of criticism and twelve kinds of solution, the basis of his lost six books of *Homeric Problems*. Porphyry quotes him repeatedly; the scribe of one manuscript even wrote "of Aristotle" in the margin whenever Porphyry cited him.
- **The Museum at Alexandria** made a parlour game of it: "it was a custom to propound questions and record the solutions that were being given" (HQ ad Il. 9.682–3).
- **Porphyry's own approach**, from a question that may have opened another book: "The collection of questions is already in the works of others. But we... consider the solutions that they proposed... Some of them we approve, some we deny, others we invent ourselves, and others we attempt to revise and work out" (HQ ad Il. 10.252–3). So it's partly an anthology of 600 years of Homer criticism, which is why it preserves fragments of Aristotle, Chrysippus, Metrodorus, Zenodotus, Aristarchus, Apion and others.

### 3. "Homer From Homer"

- In the dedicatory letter of Book One, addressed to his friend **Anatolius**, Porphyry quotes the maxim "**Homer explains himself**" (*autos heauton ta polla Homēros exēgeitai*), adding that "many [subtleties] of speech are not recognized and escape the notice of the majority".
- The famous wording is in Question 11 of Book One: "**Seeing fit to elucidate Homer from Homer** (*Homēron ex Homērou saphēnizein*), I was pointing out..." (HQ I 56.3–6). What he points out is that Homer explains himself "sometimes... immediately (*parakeimenōs*), but at other times elsewhere (*en allois*)".
- **Is it Aristarchus'?** It's usually credited to **Aristarchus of Samothrace** (2nd century BC), the great Alexandrian editor. MacPhail is careful: "there is no proof that Aristarchus said this dictum", but "the idea is uncontestably Aristarchan". A scholium (on Il. 5.385) reports that Aristarchus thought the poet's more fabulous statements should be taken in line with poetic licence, without adding anything from outside what the poet says. Porphyry's passage is the earliest surviving statement of the principle (Sententiae Antiquae).
- **A worked example** (Book One, via Sententiae Antiquae): the one-off word *eirokomos* for the old woman Aphrodite disguises herself as (Il. 3.386). Porphyry explains *-komos* from *komeō*, "to tend, adorn", and confirms the sense of "adorn" from Od. 3.437, where cow's horns are adorned with gold. So a "wool-*komos*" is a wool-dresser. The blogger isn't convinced, but notes that this is the method still used for Homeric hapaxes: explain the strange from the system's own usage.
- Book One is careful philology, conversational in tone ("We were inquiring into the sense of this", "Reading this you were puzzled"). It often ends by noting a knock-on effect: "From this you will also solve..." It includes close readings of the siege scene on the shield of Achilles (Il. 18.509–34; see [[The Shield of Achilles: A World on a War Shield]]) and of the fish that will eat Lycaon's corpse.

### 4. How the Text Survives

- **Book One** survives whole in one manuscript, **Vaticanus Graecus 305** (14th century), headed "Book One of the Homeric Questions of the Philosopher Porphyry". The same codex has part of Heraclitus' *Homeric Allegories* and Porphyry's *Cave of the Nymphs*. It stops after the twentieth question with the note "here end Porphyry's *zētēmata*".
- **The rest** survives only as **extracts copied into the margins of Homer manuscripts**, chopped up and attached to the lines they discuss. The main ones:
  - **Venetus B** (11th century, Venice). A later hand filled the empty margins with Porphyry and Heraclitus.
  - **Escorialensis 509** (11th century), which labels every extract "of Porphyry".
  - Two later manuscripts, at Leiden and Leipzig.
- The marginal extracts are in a different style from Book One: they begin with a direct question ("Why...?"), call the problem "illogical", "impossible", "unfitting" or "contradictory", and answer "one must say that..."; they cite anonymous views ("some say... others..."), then Porphyry's own suggestion introduced by "perhaps" (*mēpote*). MacPhail thinks the extracts on the *Iliad* would fill roughly five more books.
- Editors since Valckenaer (1807) have argued over what is really Porphyry. Schrader (1880) gave him every marginal note shaped as a question; Erbse showed that was too generous. MacPhail's edition (2011) gives Greek text, English translation and commentary for the *Iliad* questions; Book One had been translated by Robin Schlunk (1993).

### 5. Types of Solution

Following Aristotle, the solutions are classified by where they come from (MacPhail, pp. 7–8):

| Solution | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **From diction** (*ek tēs lexeōs*) | the word means something else here | allegory: the battle of the gods is a battle of elements (HQ ad Il. 20.67–75) |
| **From custom** (*ek tou ethous*) | that's how people did things then | Aristotle: "even now in Thessaly they drag [corpses] around tombs" |
| **From time / occasion** (*ek tou kairou*) | the circumstances of the time explain it | the gods behave like kings because Homer lived "when Hellas was ruled by kings" (HQ ad Il. 20.67–75) |
| **From the character** (*ek tou prosōpou*) | a character said it, not the poet | Hector's "no one escapes *moira*" vs Zeus on Aegisthus |

Allegory counts too: Porphyry files it as a "solution from diction", the oldest of all, "from Theagenes of Rhegium, who first wrote about Homer" (HQ ad Il. 20.67–75).

### 6. Examples

- **Fate (*moira*)** (HQ ad Il. 6.488–9). Hector says no man escapes his *moira*, but in the *Odyssey* Aegisthus marries Clytemnestra "in violation of fate". The solution is **from diction**: *moira* has three meanings in Homer, "destiny", "portion" (as in dividing up the feast) and "propriety". Aegisthus acted against propriety, not destiny. Also, the speakers differ (Hector there, Zeus here), and "it is not at all unreasonable that different characters are inconsistent". Then Porphyry lines up a whole catalogue of passages to show that for Homer the destiny of death really is unalterable even for gods: Ares, Poseidon, Zeus weeping "bloody drops" for Sarpedon, Heracles, Hephaistos unable to hide Achilles from death. This is "Homer from Homer" in action.
- **"White milk"** (HQ ad Il. 4.434). Critics mocked such epithets as redundant: what milk is black? What trench isn't dug? Porphyry answers each one. A "dug" trench is deliberately excavated, not a natural gully. Milk is white "because it does not admit a shadow". Oil is "wet" because it stays moist when other liquids dry. "Seeing with eyes" is opposed to seeing in imagination or dreams, and "hearing with ears" means hearing first-hand, "from a living voice". His conclusion: "any one inquiring on all such [words] will find a reasonable cause for the epithet". (Benardete makes the same bet 1,700 years later; see [[Achilles and Hector: The Homeric Hero]].)
- **The lying dream** (HQ ad Il. 2.8–12). Why does Zeus promise Agamemnon he'll take Troy "now" and then not deliver? Agamemnon took "now" to mean "today", "foolish man". Zenodotus defends Zeus by saying Troy was doomed that very day when the Trojans broke the oath.
- **Agamemnon's test** (HQ ad Il. 2.73). Aristotle had complained that the rescue (Athena stopping the flight) is a "contrivance". Porphyry argues the test made sense: with plague, a long war and Achilles' mutiny, Agamemnon had to find out the army's mood before giving orders.
- **Achilles' insults** (HQ ad Il. 1.225). Why does Achilles return again and again to "dog-eyed" but not "wine-heavy" or "deer-hearted"? Because Agamemnon's glare at Calchas ("his eyes looked like a shining fire") was there for all to see. Then a second answer: the three insults hit the three virtues of a general that Isocrates lists, care, loyalty and courage, so Homer is "divinely inspired".
- **Achilles' lyre** (HQ ad Il. 9.186). Is it decent for Achilles to play music while the Greeks are dying? It calms his anger; he sings "glories of men", not effeminate songs. Or he's putting on a show for the envoys. Or "though idle in body, he was unwilling to be idle also in spirit... In time of peace, he practices warfare."
- **Odysseus' report** (HQ ad Il. 9.682–3, the Museum's question). Odysseus tells Agamemnon only that Achilles "threatened" to sail at dawn. He leaves out the softer answer to Ajax. Is this a "false embassy"? The solver says Odysseus reports only what was said to him, calls it a "threat" because of what Achilles told the others, and leaves Ajax to report his own part, out of courtesy.
- **Dragging Hector** (HQ ad Il. 24.15–16). Isn't it against all custom? First solution: Hector started it. He wanted to cut off Patroclus' head and stick it on the palisade (18.175–7), and the Trojans stretched Patroclus' corpse "like the hide of a large ox". "Not those who defend themselves in kind, but their aggressors act unlawfully". Then **Aristotle's** solution from custom: "even now in Thessaly they drag [corpses] around tombs."
- **Achilles' grief** (HQ ad Il. 18.22). Plato (*Republic* 3) objected to heroes lamenting. Zoilus mocked Achilles' grief as "woman-like", worse than "a barbarian nurse". Zenodorus answers that the grief matched the size of the loss, and that it was the custom: "to cut one's hair and shed a tear" (Od. 4.197–8). See [[Plato's Quarrel With the Poets: Republic X Read Three Ways]].
- **The night council** (HQ ad Il. 10.194–7). Aristotle asks why the chiefs meet outside the wall, among the corpses. His answers: the Trojans weren't likely to attack; serious matters need quiet; generals should be near the guards; a meeting inside would have caused alarm.
- **The dancing floor** (HQ ad Il. 18.590–3). How can a god (Hephaistos) imitate a man (Daedalus)? Perhaps the floor is "like" Daedalus' because both are copies of the same dance, the mixed dance of the youths Theseus saved from the labyrinth. Or perhaps "the poet teaches to imitate the good, although it is out of cheap [materials]".
- **Zoilus nitpicking** (HQ ad Il. 5.7). Athena makes fire blaze from Diomedes' head and shoulders. Wouldn't he burn? Answers: "fire" means "lustre"; it's "from the helmet and shield" by metonymy; "as" is understood, as in "they fought like a body of fire".
- **The king between gods and men** (HQ ad Il. 1.340). Achilles swears by "the gods, mortal men and the king". The Pythagoreans posited a third class between gods and men, the king or wise man, "though Homer first placed the king in between". He adds that in India kings bow to the Brahmans, "the philosophers among them".
- **The battle of the gods** (HQ ad Il. 20.67–75). The general charge is that the stories about the gods are "unseemly". Three defences:
  - **Allegory** ("from diction"): it's a war of the elements, with dry against wet and hot against cold. Apollo, Helios and Hephaistos are fire, Poseidon and Scamander water, Artemis the moon, Hera the air. Sometimes the names are dispositions: Athena wisdom, Ares stupidity, Aphrodite desire, Hermes speech.
  - **Custom**: the cities and lawgivers themselves put these stories in the mysteries and wove the gigantomachy into Athena's robe.
  - **The time**: "with Hellas then acting as king", Homer shows human nature as "not altogether far off from the divine", inventing about gods "what we see around men".

### 7. Assessment

- **What it's good for.** It's a museum of ancient criticism: fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems*, Chrysippus, Metrodorus, Alexander of Myndos on birds, Zoilus, Aristarchus. It also shows that ancient readers asked the same questions we do. And the core principle, read a text by its own usage before importing outside meanings, is still the first rule of philology.
- **Its limits.**
  - Many solutions are special pleading: if Homer says something odd, find another meaning for the word. The *moira* solution is good; "milk is white because it does not admit a shadow" is a stretch.
  - "Homer from Homer" assumes a single consistent author who never nods. That's exactly what the analysts (Wolf onwards) later denied, and the oral-formulaic school explains "white milk" as metre, not meaning.
  - Porphyry is also happy to stack incompatible solutions ("or... or...") rather than decide.
- **Philology and allegory.** It's striking that the same man wrote the most philological work on Homer to survive and the most allegorical (*Cave of the Nymphs*). MacPhail reports Gildersleeve's view that the two genres couldn't have sat together, and rejects it. One passage in the *Homeric Questions* itself (HQ ad Il. 19.221–4) mixes them. "Homer from Homer" and allegory both assume that Homer's text is unified and wise, so that apparent nonsense hides sense.
- **Orthodox angle.**
  - **"Scripture interprets Scripture."** The Christian version of the same rule is older than Porphyry's book. **Irenaeus** (c. 180) attacks the Valentinians for reading the Bible like people who make **Homeric centos**: they cut lines out of Homer and stitch them into a new poem that looks Homeric but tells a story Homer never told (*Against Heresies* 1.9.4). They're like someone who takes a mosaic of a king and rearranges the stones into a dog or a fox (1.8.1). Irenaeus' answer is a "Scripture from Scripture" principle governed by the **rule of faith**: read the parts in light of the whole, as the Church received it. The Fathers interpret obscure passages by clear ones.
  - **Porphyry turned the method against the Church.** In his *Against the Christians* he accused **Origen** of taking the Greek allegorical method he had learned (from Stoic books by Chaeremon and Cornutus) and applying it to the Jewish Scriptures (Eusebius, *Church History* 6.19). He used historical-philological criticism to argue that **Daniel** was written in the Maccabean period, not in the Exile (Jerome's commentary on Daniel). So the method of the *Homeric Questions* became the first serious biblical criticism, aimed at Christianity. His book was later burned by imperial order.
  - **The difference.** For Porphyry, Homer's unity is a literary assumption, and his gods need rescuing by allegory. For the Church, Scripture's unity rests on one Spirit speaking through many authors and on **Christ** as the key to the whole (Luke 24:27, "beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself"). So typology isn't an escape from an embarrassing text but a reading of history as prophecy (see [[Types of Christ in the OT]]).
  - **A lesson anyway.** Porphyry's insistence that "many subtleties escape the notice of the majority" and his patience with single words ("white milk", *moira*) are the same virtues the Fathers show with Scripture: Basil on the six days, Gregory of Nyssa on the titles of the Psalms, John Chrysostom on every particle of St Paul.

### Conclusion

Porphyry's *Homeric Questions* is where the ancient argument over Homer becomes a method. The critics ask "Why?", "How can it be?", "Isn't it unseemly?". The defenders answer from diction, custom, occasion and character. Porphyry adds the rule that would outlast them all: **explain Homer from Homer**, sometimes from the next line and sometimes from the other end of the poem. The book survives only in fragments in the margins of Homer, which is a fitting place for it. The same man later read the *Odyssey* as a Platonic allegory and attacked the Christians' Scriptures with the critic's tools, while the Church had already worked out its own version of the rule: Scripture explains Scripture, read as one book about Christ.
