## 1. Early Christian Writings and Canons

### A. Old Testament (OT)

1. **Septuagint (LXX)** (3rd–1st Century BC):
    - Greek translation of Hebrew Scriptures.
    - Includes deuterocanonical books like **Tobit**, **Judith**, **Wisdom of Solomon**, and **Maccabees**.
    - Widely used by Greek-speaking Jews and the early Church.
2. **Hebrew Bible (Tanakh)**:
    - Used by Palestinian Jews.
    - Contains 24 books (equivalent to 39 books in the Protestant OT).
    - Excludes deuterocanonical books.

### B. New Testament (NT)

1. **Proto-canon (1st Century)**:
    - Circulated writings among early Christian communities:
        - The four Gospels: **Matthew**, **Mark**, **Luke**, **John**.
        - **Pauline epistles**.
        - **Acts of the Apostles** and some General Epistles.
2. **Other Early Texts**:
    - Writings like the **Epistle of Barnabas**, **Shepherd of Hermas**, and **1 Clement** were considered Scripture in some communities.
    - Apocryphal gospels (e.g., **Gospel of Thomas**, **Gospel of Peter**) circulated but were later rejected.

## 2. Competing Canons in Early Christianity

### A. Marcion's Canon (ca. 140 AD)

- Marcion (a Gnostic heretic) rejected the OT entirely.
- His NT included only parts of Luke and 10 Pauline epistles, purged of Jewish references.
- This prompted the Church to formalize a more comprehensive canon.

### B. Muratorian Fragment (ca. 170 AD)

- Early NT canon list with 22 of the 27 NT books.
- Omits **Hebrews**, **James**, **1 Peter**, and **2 Peter**.
- Mentions the **Shepherd of Hermas** as suitable for private reading but not for liturgical use.

### C. Other Regional Canons

- **Alexandria**: Emphasized the Septuagint and additional books like **Wisdom of Solomon** and **Sirach**.
- **Antioch**: Preferred the proto-canon but accepted many deuterocanonical books.
- **Rome and Carthage**: Gradually formed a canon similar to today's Eastern Orthodox canon.

### D. Codex Variations

1. **Codex Sinaiticus (4th Century)**:
    - Contains the LXX OT (with deuterocanonical books) and NT.
    - Includes **Shepherd of Hermas** and **Epistle of Barnabas**.
2. **Codex Vaticanus (4th Century)**:
    - Similar to Sinaiticus but lacks some apocryphal NT texts.
3. **Codex Alexandrinus (5th Century)**:
    - Contains the deuterocanonical OT books and all 27 NT books.

## 3. Councils and Synods: Establishing the Canon

### A. Old Testament

1. **Septuagint (LXX)**: Universally accepted in the Eastern Church, including the deuterocanonical books.
2. **Council of Jamnia (ca. 90 AD)**:
    - Jewish leaders excluded deuterocanonical books, finalizing the Hebrew canon (24 books).
    - Christians continued using the LXX.

### B. New Testament

1. **Council of Laodicea (ca. 363 AD)**:
    - Affirmed 26 NT books, omitting Revelation.
2. **Council of Hippo (393 AD)** and **Carthage (397 AD)**:
    - Affirmed all 27 NT books and the deuterocanonical OT books.
3. **Athanasius' Festal Letter (367 AD)**:
    - First exact listing of 27 NT books.
    - Affirmed deuterocanonical books for devotional use.
4. **Synod of Jerusalem (1672 AD)**:
    - Finalized the Eastern Orthodox canon:
        - **49 OT books** (LXX, including deuterocanon).
        - **27 NT books**, identical to other Christian traditions.

## 4. The Gospel of Thomas and Non-Canonical Texts

Reasons for Exclusion:

1. **Lack of Apostolic Origin**: Not authored by or linked to Apostles.
2. **Contradictory Theology**: Contains Gnostic elements (e.g., denial of bodily resurrection).
3. **Late Composition**: Written in the 2nd–4th centuries, long after the Apostolic era.
4. **Rejection by Early Church Fathers**: Irenaeus and Eusebius dismissed such writings as heretical.

## 5. Oldest Complete Bible

- **Codex Sinaiticus (ca. 330–360 AD)**:
    - Oldest surviving complete NT and nearly complete OT (Septuagint).
    - Includes additional texts like **Shepherd of Hermas**.
