The *Cambridge History of Greek and Roman Political Thought* opens with four chapters on the period before Plato. Their shared claim is that Greek political thought did not begin with philosophy. It began in the polis itself, and in the poems, laws, plays and histories the polis produced. Systematic theory came late and grew out of that older reflection.

Each chapter has its own angle. Paul Cartledge sets out what "the political" meant to Greeks and how far it was from what it means now. Kurt Raaflaub traces political thinking in the Archaic poets, lawgivers and philosophers, and compares it with the Near East. Simon Goldhill asks how drama, and above all tragedy, counts as political thought. Richard Winton covers the sophists and the first two historians. The note gives each chapter equal space. Sections 1–2 are Cartledge, 3–7 Raaflaub, 8–11 Goldhill and 12–14 Winton.

### 1. What "the political" meant

Cartledge starts from vocabulary. Aristocracy, democracy, monarchy, oligarchy, tyranny and politics itself are Greek words, and the Greeks are credited with inventing "politics in the strong sense: that is, communal decision-making effected in public after substantive discussion by or before voters deemed relevantly equal" (p. 11). Yet their politics differed sharply from ours. The average polis had perhaps 500 to 2,000 adult male citizens, and Athens with 40,000 or more was hugely exceptional. More important, they joined politics to practical ethics.

Public affairs were put *es meson*, "in the middle", as a prize to be contested (p. 12). Several features follow.

- **Public and private.** The Romans opposed *res publica* to *res privata*. The Greek counterpart was not "the public" but *ta pragmata*, "affairs", and the word for revolution was *neotera pragmata*, "newer affairs" (p. 13). For us "The personal is the political" is a radical slogan. For a Greek "it would have been just a banal statement of the obvious" (p. 13). There was no State to stand apart from the citizens, and no word for the individual in our anti-social sense. The private man, the *idiotes*, eventually gave English its "idiot".
- **Household and city.** Women had no public political status, and war, with its virtue of *andreia*, "manliness", belonged to men. But household and polis were interwoven. The city needed women's public religious roles, as in the women-only Thesmophoria, and it policed marriage because citizenship depended on it, as in Pericles' citizenship law of 451/0 (p. 14).
- **Religion.** The Greek city was a city of gods as well as men, and to a Greek everything was "full of gods" (p. 14). Agora and acropolis were the twin centres of political life. "The polis, however, was no theocracy" (p. 14). Men chose which gods to worship and how, "without benefit of clergy, dogma or sacred scripture" (p. 15).
- **Nomos.** The word meant custom and law. Once laws were written down in the seventh century, a distinction grew between the unwritten laws, mostly religious and all the more binding, and the written laws, which varied and could be changed (p. 15). *Nomos* probably comes from a verb meaning "to distribute", and what the polis distributed was honour (*time*) and offices (*timai*). Even unequal regimes assumed some basic equality, and by about 500 this produced *isonomia*, an exactly equal distribution among those deemed equal. The word appears first among the elite and was later taken over by democrats (p. 15).
- **Freedom.** Greek freedom was defined against slavery. Aristotle combined strong citizen freedom with a doctrine of natural slavery, and the Greeks set their liberty against the despotism they saw among "barbarians" (p. 16). Cartledge cites Benjamin Constant's 1819 lecture on ancient and modern liberty. Ancient liberty was public and subjected the individual wholly to the community. Modern liberty is private and is "little more than freedom from politics as the Greeks understood it" (p. 16).

### 2. The stateless polis, stasis and the birth of theory

The typical polis was a republic, and for practical purposes "a more or less fully stateless political community" (p. 17). The positive side was direct participation: "The citizens were the polis", with no line between "us" and "them". The negative side is a list of absences (p. 18). There was no civil society distinct from government, no separation of powers, no clear sovereignty, no parties and so no loyal opposition, almost no police, no toleration of dissent (the trial of Socrates shows it), and no natural rights. Since the polis could not restrain its citizens, it stressed self-control, and violent loss of it, *hubris*, was "the ultimate civic crime" (p. 18).

The same statelessness made *stasis*, civil strife, a constant worry (p. 19). Cartledge names two causes. One was the clash between the ideal of equality and men of exceptional charisma who felt denied their honour; politics was a zero-sum contest for prestige, and democratic Athens was unusual in channelling it for long. The other was the gap between rich and poor, defined by leisure, which thinkers and activists alike saw as permanent "class struggle on the political plane" (p. 19). Against this the Greek ideal was *homonoia*, literally "same-mindedness". From Thucydides on, thinkers also praised a mixed constitution that would give every group something (pp. 19–20). Modern thought from Hobbes to Madison treated faction as an evil to be removed. The Greeks expected it and planned around it.

Greek political theory was never only about institutions. *Politeia* meant both "constitution" and "citizenship", and it was called the city's "life and soul". Where modern theory thinks in images of machines and buildings, Greek theory thought in organic terms, of sharing (*methexis*) and rule (*arche*), not sovereignty or force (p. 20).

The decisive step was the idea that every constitution is rule by one, by some or by all, each with a good and a corrupt form. It appears first in Herodotus' Persian debate (3.80–2), "a beautiful hypothesis distinguished by its combination of scope and economy" (p. 21). Plato had little interest in such comparison. Aristotle studied over 150 of the more than 1,000 Greek cities, which sat "like frogs or ants round a pond" in Plato's phrase. Cartledge suggests that the invention of democracy at Athens around 500 may have been what turned thinking about politics, which goes back to *Iliad* 2, into political theory proper (p. 21). The polis itself arose in the eighth century from land, war and religion. Hesiod gave the first account of the just city, and the hoplite farmers who could afford armour made the later revolutions possible (p. 22). Simonides said that "the polis teaches a man". The theorists who followed held that this was necessary but not enough for political virtue (p. 22).

### 3. Homer

Raaflaub begins with a chain of quotations from Simonides back to Homer, all centred on the polis (pp. 23–24). Xenophanes complains that athletes get public honours though they do nothing for the city's good order. Phocylides thinks "A small polis on a high cliff that is well run is better than foolish Nineveh". Theognis fears his city "is pregnant" with a tyrant. In the *Iliad* Hector says, "One bird-omen is best, to fight defending the fatherland." Raaflaub defines the polis as a community of citizens, territory, cults, customs and laws, and prefers the label "citizen-state" (p. 24).

He argues, against Cartledge and others, that Homer already pictures a world of poleis, and that the poet thinks about what a polis is (pp. 25–26). The Cyclopes have a fine island they never settle. They "have no shared laws (*themistes*), no meetings for counsels", and "each one sets the law for his own wives and children, and cares nothing about the others" (*Od.* 9.112–15). They are the opposite of human society. The Phaeacians, by contrast, are an ideal polis. "In the epics, the polis represents civilization, communication, community and justice; not to live in a polis means primitiveness, isolation, lack of community and lawlessness" (pp. 25–26).

Can oral epic be used as evidence? Raaflaub thinks so. The singer had to keep his audience, so the poems mixed heroic fantasy with conditions familiar to listeners, roughly those of the late ninth and eighth centuries (pp. 26–27). A poet was "a teacher and educator of his people" (p. 27), and audiences were used to catching political points inside stories.

In Homer the *basileis*, the leading nobles, hold power with a paramount leader at their head, under an ethic of "always to be the best and to excel above the others" (*Il.* 6.208). The masses seem negligible but are not. They fight the battles, and the assembly witnesses and legitimises decisions. "The polis was built from the beginning on a foundation of considerable equality" (p. 29). Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus shows the bargain: honours and land in return for fighting in the front line (*Il.* 12.310–16). The tension between a leader's ambition and his duty is the subject of the *Iliad* itself (pp. 30–32).

- **Agamemnon and Achilles.** The Achaean camp is a makeshift polis. Agamemnon knows the code — "I myself desire that my people be safe, not perish" (*Il.* 1.117) — and breaks it by shaming Achilles, so "the primary fault lies with Agamemnon" (p. 31).
- **Thersites.** In Book 2 the army runs for the ships. Thersites, with no authority, says what everyone feels: "It is not right for you, their leader, to lead in sorrow the sons of the Achaeans!" Odysseus has to argue against "leadership by many" (p. 31).
- **Troy.** Hector's son is called Astyanax "because Hector alone saved Ilion", yet Hector ignores Poulydamas and his men and dies fearing their blame. The Trojan assembly, bribed by Paris, refused to return Helen, so all Trojans share the guilt. Hector tells Paris he should have been "stoned to death for the wrong you did us" (pp. 31–32).
- **Ithaca.** Mentor tells the silent assembly: "It is the rest of you I am indignant with ... you are many; the suitors are few" (*Od.* 2.239–41). The people are responsible for the common good (pp. 32–33).

The sceptre, made by Hephaestus for Zeus, is held by "the judges, when they administer the norms from Zeus" (*Il.* 1.237–9). Unlike a Near Eastern king, the Homeric leader is not identified with a god, so he is himself under the norms he applies, and can be judged by them (p. 33). Every stage of the quarrel happens in assembly, and when Agamemnon makes amends he becomes "more just" (pp. 33–34). By building positive and negative models, the poet "forces them to think, he educates them" (p. 34).

### 4. Hesiod and the lyric poets

Hesiod also blames the *basileis*, but as judges, not warriors (pp. 34–37). Dike, Justice, "howls when she is dragged about by bribe-devouring men whose verdicts are crooked", and goes to her father Zeus. One man's injustice brings famine, plague and war on the whole city, while straight verdicts make it flourish (*Works and Days* 220–1, 235–47, 259–62). The Prometheus myth makes man responsible for evil, and so for its cure. The first part of the *Works and Days* is an "instruction to princes". The *Theogony* shows the model ruler: Zeus wins by force but keeps power by wise and generous measures, giving the gods their shares justly. His wife Themis bears Eunomia, Dike and Eirene, "good order", "justice" and "peace" (*Th.* 901–3). Hesiod does not appeal to the assembly. He distrusts the agora and tells the small farmer to tend his work and neighbours, advice Solon would reject (pp. 36–37).

The lyric fragments show the strains of the seventh and sixth centuries (pp. 37–39).

- **Tyrtaeus**, writing for Sparta during the Messenian revolt around 650, makes civic excellence the duty of every citizen in the line: "It is a beautiful thing when a good man falls and dies fighting for his country."
- **Archilochus** throws away his shield to save himself — "I can buy another one equally good" — mocks the tall, groomed captain, and lives by his spear. Raaflaub calls such independence "essential for the emancipation of political thinking" from tradition (p. 38).
- **Theognis** speaks for an aristocracy under pressure from tyrants, upstarts and impoverishment. He insists that nobility is inborn: "By teaching one will never make the bad man good" (429–38). The old social words *agathos* (noble) and *kakos* (base) became moral words, good and bad, with "long-lasting impact on political and constitutional thinking" (p. 39). The elite drew together in clubs, the symposium and pederasty.

### 5. Solon

Athens in Solon's time was on the edge of civil war between the rich and the *demos*. He was made archon in 594 with full powers, and both sides ended up dissatisfied (pp. 39–40).

His programmatic poem starts with human responsibility. The gods do not want the city destroyed; "the citizens themselves in their ruthlessness are bent on destruction of their great city" (4W). The blame falls on the greed and *hubris* of the rich. Dike now stands on her own, and her revenge comes "certainly" (*pantos*) on the whole city (p. 40). Raaflaub asks: "Why does Solon know where Hesiod believed? Because his thinking is empirical and political" (p. 40). Solon had seen in many cities that elite abuse leads to strife and tyranny. He states it as a law of nature: "Out of the cloud comes the heavy snow and the hailstorm ... So through great men is a polis destroyed" (9W). This is the perception of "political laws, comparable to laws of nature" (p. 41).

The public ruin "overleaps the lofty wall" and finds each man in his inner room (4W.26–9). So Hesiod's advice to mind one's farm no longer works. Every citizen must take part, and the rich cannot afford injustice because it will ruin them too. The choice is between *dusnomia*, bad order, and *eunomia*, good order (p. 41).

Solon could act. He freed the mortgaged land and the debt-bondsmen, "fitting together force and law in true harmony", and from then on "personal freedom was an inalienable right of the Athenian citizen" (p. 41). He wrote laws "for the lowborn and noble alike". He replaced birth with wealth as the test for office, probably created a new council, let anyone who wished (*ho boulomenos*) prosecute on behalf of a wronged person, and set up a court of appeal. His policy was integrative: give the *demos* a share of power without destroying aristocratic leadership (p. 42).

### 6. Lawgivers, eunomia and isonomia

Other cities also gave a single lawgiver unlimited power to settle their quarrels (pp. 42–48). That presupposes that a constitution was seen "not as ordained by divine sanction or fixed by tradition, but as changeable by human insight and decision" (p. 42). The many cities made comparison easy, colonies invited experiment, and Delphi may have served as a clearing-house of advice. The Seven Sages held what Christian Meier calls a "third position" above the parties. Thales proposed that the Ionians form one state with a council at Teos (Herodotus 1.170).

Raaflaub follows Hölkeskamp in denying that whole law codes were common; they are attested only at Athens and Gortyn. Most legislation was single laws aimed at particular crises. Even so, written law cut the freedom of officials and of self-help, extended the polis's power and helped create the idea of citizenship (pp. 43–44).

- **Draco's homicide law** (probably 622), after the failed coup of Cylon, distinguished intentional from unintentional killing, made revenge depend on a court, and allowed the unintentional killer safe exile (p. 44).
- **Dreros** in Crete decreed: "This has been decided by the polis: when a man has been *kosmos*, the same man shall not be *kosmos* again for ten years", on pain of losing office for life (p. 44).
- **Sparta** made its citizens a professional warrior class of *homoioi*, "similars", living off helot labour. The Great Rhetra fixed a council of thirty elders over sixty, including the two kings, and an assembly that held the final power (*kratos*) but could be checked by the elders' right to reject "crooked choices" (pp. 45–46).

*Eunomia* was the ideal behind all this. Tyrtaeus linked the Rhetra to it, Solon contrasted it with *dusnomia*, Hesiod made it Zeus's daughter, and Alcman called it the sister of Persuasion. It stood for the political resolution of crisis and "encapsulates the main concern of early Greek political thinking" (p. 46).

Then came *isonomia*. Aristocrats fighting tyrants discovered the value of their equal share in power. The physician Alcmaeon used the word for health: "The bond of health is the 'equal rights' (*isonomia*) of the powers ... while the *monarchia* of one of them is the cause of disease" (p. 47). Equality was later extended to all hoplite farmers: in Sparta they became *homoioi*, in Athens *isoi*, "equals". Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7, after the fall of the Peisistratid tyranny, divided Attica into more than a hundred demes, grouped into thirds and tribes, each tribe mixing citizens from different regions, with a Council of Five Hundred drawn from them. Raaflaub calls Athens' later power and full democracy "unthinkable without" these changes (p. 48).

### 7. Philosophers, the Near East, and why Greece was different

The Ionian thinkers studied nature, but for them physics, ethics and politics were not yet separate, and they read nature through social ideas (pp. 48–50). Anaximander's one fragment says that things "pay penalty (*dike*) and retribution (*tisis*) to each other for their injustice (*adikia*) according to the assessment of Time". A cosmos governed by a balance of equals presupposes the political idea of *isonomia* (p. 49). Heraclitus said "they would not know the name of *dike* if these things did not exist", that "war is togetherness (*xunon*) and *dike* is strife", and that "the sun will not transgress his measures; otherwise the Furies, ministers of Justice, will find him out." In politics he urged that "we must base our strength on that which is common (*xunon*) to all, as the city on the law (*nomos*)" and that "the people should fight for the *nomos* as if for their city-wall", while despising the *demos* (p. 50).

Raaflaub then weighs Near Eastern influence (pp. 50–57). It was real in myth, science, crafts, coinage and writing. The Archaic Greeks were open to the older civilisations, and the sharp Greek–barbarian opposition came only with the Persian Wars. Everywhere in the Near East the ruler's first duty was justice. In Egypt, society left to itself was chaos and injustice (*isfet*), and the king upheld *ma'at*, an order in which the strong protect the weak and the weak obey, which Jan Assmann calls "vertical solidarity" (pp. 52–53). Hammurabi said he was appointed "to make justice appear in the land ... so that the strong might not oppress the weak" (p. 53). The Hebrew prophets denounced even the powerful in God's name. Hesiod and Solon have clear parallels, including debt relief and an order like *ma'at*, and Hesiod may share an intellectual world with his near-contemporary Amos (p. 54).

The differences matter more (pp. 55–57). In the Near East the king decides, and debt cancellation is his occasional favour. In Greece justice was demanded by the powerless, as with Hesiod, and enacted by an elected mediator under popular pressure, as with Solon. The obligation was "founded not in divine law outside or above society but in communal values and norms" (p. 55). The Greek gods punish wrongdoers, but they "neither cause nor resolve such a crisis"; men cause it and men must fix it (p. 56). Mesopotamia saw the whole cosmos as a hierarchical state in which, as Thorkild Jacobsen put it, "the 'good life' was the 'obedient life'". "It is difficult to think of a starker contrast to Greek society" (p. 57).

Raaflaub's conclusion lists the conditions that made Greek political thinking possible (pp. 57–59). There was no sacred kingship. No large state formed, because no great outside threat demanded one. Leaders were first among equals, and the gap between nobles and farmers was small. Rapid change, colonisation and comparison bred a respected culture of political mediation. And the immediate cause was "the dissatisfaction with the shortcomings of elite leadership and the discrepancy between the interests of community and individual" (p. 59).

### 8. The theatre as a political institution

"Tragedy compromises political theory" is Goldhill's first sentence (p. 60). Plato, "the first policeman of political thought", banned drama from his city, though legend says he wrote tragedies first. Aristotle kept drama for education but helped push it out of theory. Nietzsche said Socratic reason killed tragedy. Hegel, by contrast, used tragedy to explore the citizen's life, and Bernard Williams holds that "Philosophy ... has to make demands on literature" (pp. 60–61). Scholars split. Winton and Garnsey draw "a clear distinction" between tragedy and the analytic approach whose emergence "marks the beginning of Greek political theory as such". Peter Euben replies that "tragedy was as close as one could come to a theoretical institution" (p. 61). Goldhill's question is how tragedy contributes to political thought, and "what does it mean when political theory tries to proceed without tragedy?"

He starts with the institution (pp. 61–65). The theatre stood beside the law-court and the assembly as a place where citizens gathered to hear and judge competing speech. "To be in an audience is to play the role of the democratic citizen" (p. 62). The Great Dionysia drew perhaps 14,000 to 17,000, more than twice an assembly. The seating mapped the city: places for the Council, for foreign ambassadors, for ephebes, and probably a wedge for each tribe. It was "the city on display" (pp. 62–63).

Four ceremonies came before the plays (p. 63):

1. The ten generals poured the opening libation together, one of the few times they acted as a body.
2. A herald named citizens crowned for service to the city.
3. The tribute of the empire was paraded, and allied ambassadors had to watch.
4. Sons of men killed in war, raised at public expense, appeared in full armour and swore to fight and die as their fathers had.

The organisation was democratic throughout (p. 64). The archon "granted a chorus". Rich citizens paid for productions as a public duty and competed for honour. The judges were drawn by lot from each tribe, and only five of ten votes, again chosen at random, counted. A Theoric Fund paid citizens to attend, and it was illegal even to propose changing its law. A special assembly afterwards reviewed the festival. The spectator's activity was *theoria*, from which "theory" comes, meaning "official viewing by participation in a formal ceremonial event" (p. 65). The poets were "the teachers of the people", which is exactly why Plato wanted to replace them with philosophy.

### 9. How tragedy has been read politically

Modern political thought has used tragedy heavily (pp. 65–67). Hegel read the *Antigone* as a clash between individual and State, "of Right and Right", a reading central to his philosophy of history even if it has "rather slender ties to Sophocles' drama". Goldhill speaks of "the tyranny of Greece" over German thought and says "Antigone is in this sense a text of nineteenth-century political thought" (p. 66). Feminists such as Luce Irigaray took up Hegel's Antigone to rethink family, state and gender.

Critics of the ancient plays have followed three strategies (pp. 67–70).

- **A specific message.** This produced allegories such as *Oedipus Tyrannus* as a play about Pericles. Euripides' *Trojan Women* came the year after the Athenians destroyed Melos and as the Sicilian expedition was being prepared, but its generality resists tying it to one policy. Fewer critics now look for such messages in tragedy (pp. 68–69).
- **The political process.** Christian Meier says what Aeschylus thought of the Areopagus reforms "is not only a moot point, but one with little bearing on our interpretation." Jean-Pierre Vernant locates "the tragic moment" in the clash between the heroic world of divine causes and the city's demand for human responsibility; tragedy is "an expression of torn consciousness" (p. 69).
- **Myth, ritual and gender.** Tragedy reworks myths like the defeat of the Amazons or Athenian birth from the soil, and so builds the city's picture of its own order (p. 70).

Goldhill then names three themes that go to the heart of democracy (pp. 70–74):

1. **Individual and community.** Homer made Achilles "a problem of integration" and the *Odyssey* a story of reintegration. The *Oresteia*'s move from the palace to the court means "The household cannot be a sufficient locus of order (*dike*) any more." Sophocles' heroes, such as Ajax and Philoctetes, put self against community (pp. 71–72).
2. **The autonomous judge.** Democratic institutions assume a responsible citizen who weighs a case and decides. Tragedy's archetypal cry is "Alas, what should I do?", but the gods undercut the confident agent. Creon tells Oedipus: "do not seek for control in all things" (*OT* 1522–3) (p. 72).
3. **Language.** Democracy runs on public speech, and tragedy shows it failing. Vernant notes that characters use the same words with opposite meanings, so that "there are zones of opacity and incommunicability in the words men exchange" (p. 73). Phaedra says "with the tongue, there is nothing to be trusted."

So tragedy shows "the developing city putting its developing structures of thought at risk and under scrutiny in the public arena" (p. 74).

### 10. The Areopagus and Creon

The *Oresteia* has polarised scholars most (pp. 74–81). For Winton and Garnsey "it would be absurd" to call it political theory, and Farrar calls its thinking "figurative, indirect and allusive". For Meier "the transition to democracy in Attica was never perceived as clearly as it was by Aeschylus" (p. 75). Homer's Orestes was a simple model of a son's revenge. Aeschylus makes him kill his mother, and so "Aeschylus has, precisely, politicized Orestes' story" (p. 75).

Goldhill focuses on the founding of the Areopagus (pp. 76–78). Apollo's purification is necessary but not enough. Athena sets up a court because "the issue is too great for some one human to judge" (*Eum.* 470–1), an appeal to collective judgment. Her foundation speech makes the court permanent, ties its name to Theseus' war with the Amazons, and charges it with "citizen respect / And inborn fear", warning the citizens to uphold "Neither anarchy nor tyranny" and asking "who among mortals is just who fears nothing?" (690–9). The line "do not revolutionize the laws" and the image of fouling clear water have been read both ways. In 462 Ephialtes had stripped the Areopagus of most of its powers, and was murdered soon after. Conservatives read Athena as opposing the reform. But the reformers claimed to be removing later accretions, and the court kept murder trials, so others read her as a democrat. Goldhill thinks the generalising language rules out a party line (p. 77). The play's end, with its procession like the Panathenaea, makes the *Oresteia* "a charter for the polis" (p. 78), though critics divide over whether its gender settlement is harmony or the founding of patriarchy. The site's note [[Justice in the Oresteia: From Vendetta to the Court?]] takes up the trilogy's *dike* at length.

The *Antigone* scene Goldhill picks is the argument between Creon and his son Haemon (pp. 81–84). Creon builds an analogy of household and city: "whomsoever the city may appoint, that man must be obeyed, in little things and in great, in just things and unjust ... disobedience is the worst of evils. This it is that ruins cities" (661–73). Goldhill notes there was "scarcely any tradition and no valorization of civic disobedience in the classical polis" (p. 82), and that Creon uses the ephebe's oath to stand by his comrade. Haemon answers that trees that bend in a flood survive. As the quarrel heats, Creon slides into a tyrant's lines — "Is not the city held to be its ruler's?" — and Haemon's plea for flexibility ends in his suicide.

Goldhill draws two lessons. "The expression of political theory in tragedy is always part of a scene of persuasion": Creon's theory is a move in a struggle for power. And "The figures' arguments are not the play's argument" (pp. 83–84). The play frames theory inside a narrative that tests it. To separate theory from "particular issues and individuals", as Winton and Garnsey do, hides the fact that theory meets practice only in cases. "The exemplary case of Creon indeed encapsulates the tragic misprision and misuse of theoretical positions in politics" (p. 84).

### 11. Comedy

Philosophers from Plato to Hegel to Martha Nussbaum have ignored comedy (pp. 84–85). Plato let citizens watch it only to learn what is vulgar. Plutarch advised against reading Aristophanes at dinner. Yet comedy is set in the present city and has an evident political agenda. In the *Acharnians* a citizen called Dikaiopolis, "Just City", makes a private peace with Sparta while the general Lamachus, probably in the audience, prepares for war. The *parabasis*, where the chorus addresses the audience directly, works like a speech in the assembly. The *Wasps*, *Knights*, *Birds*, *Ecclesiazusae* and *Clouds* together give "a carnivalized repertoire of the city's political operations" (p. 86).

Critics disagree about how serious it is (p. 86). Some take Aristophanes as a fearless conservative critic, pointing to his attacks on Cleon and the tradition that the *Frogs* was restaged "because of its parabasis". Others say the fantasy and obscenity cancel any point. Most take a middle line. Goldhill's own view is that comedy, like tragedy, approaches politics "obliquely, via detours". Its effect depends on the audience: "Who you laugh with and at, defines you" (p. 87). Comedy is "a space in which citizens negotiate the boundaries of the acceptable", where lines are drawn and crossed between licence and abuse. He quotes Nicole Loraux: "Laughter ... maker of its own space, producer of distances, allows a better negotiation of the real" (p. 87).

His conclusion is that if political theory means abstract analytic argument, tragedy falls outside it. But tragedy has repeatedly been made part of political philosophy, and its stories both examine democratic principles and show how theory becomes a move in practice. "Tragedy provokes the question of what happens to political theory when its seclusion as theory is compromised by the narrative dramas of practice, example and conflict" (p. 88).

### 12. The sophists and nomos against phusis

Winton opens with three texts (pp. 89–91).

- **The Sisyphus fragment**, by Euripides or by Plato's uncle Critias, says human life began like that of animals. Men made laws so that "justice (*dike*) would be ruler (*turannos*)". When people kept doing wrong in secret, "some shrewd and clever (*sophos*) individual invented fear of the gods" and so "quenched lawlessness (*anomia*) by means of laws (*nomoi*)" (pp. 89–90). Winton notes that this clever speaker reverses the usual picture of the orator: he deceives, but for the common good.
- **Aristophanes' *Clouds*** has Wrong defeat Right by rejecting the *nomoi* of men for the "compelling forces" of nature. It offers "a Utopia of unrestrained immorality" (p. 90).
- **Prodicus' "Choice of Heracles"**, retold in Xenophon's *Memorabilia*, has the young hero choose between Virtue and a woman whose friends call her Happiness and whose enemies call her Vice (p. 91).

Rhetoric, education and morality: these are the themes of the sophists, the professional teachers of rhetoric who prepared young men for citizen life, especially in democratic and imperial Athens (p. 91). Protagoras of Abdera, Gorgias of Leontini, Hippias of Elis and Prodicus of Ceos mostly survive through Plato, who is "at once hostile and elusive" and treats them as belonging "rather to the history of publicity than to that of ideas" (p. 92). The word *sophistes* was wholly negative by 400. Winton warns that the evidence makes a real history of these ideas "an impossibility" (p. 94). What is clear is that this period saw "systematic reflection and argument on broad political issues, conducted in purely human terms", prompted by Athens' twin revolution of democracy at home and empire abroad. Aristotle's survey in *Politics* II ignores the sophists but discusses the town-planner Hippodamus, "the first individual not himself engaged in politics to speak on the nature of the best constitution" (p. 94).

The traditional polis was made of citizens and gods, and both became problems (pp. 95–96). The fifth century invented the category of myth and doubted the gods themselves. Protagoras wrote: "Concerning the gods, I am not able to know either that they exist or that they do not ... much impedes our knowing, the obscurity of the matter and the brevity of human life." Prodicus explained the gods as deified fruits and benefactors. Hesiod's myth of decline was reversed into a story of progress through *techne*, craft (p. 96).

Protagoras' "Great Speech" in Plato gives the most positive version (p. 97). Men had fire and the crafts from Prometheus but lacked *politike techne*, the political art, including war, and so could not form cities. Zeus sent Hermes with justice (*dike*) and respect (*aidos*), to be given not to a few experts but to all, "for the polis cannot endure unless all its members possess at least basic competence". Winton calls it "one of the few systematic rationales of democracy" in Greek (p. 92).

Antiphon's *Truth* gives the negative version (pp. 97–99). Justice is obeying the city's laws, so a man will profit most if he obeys "the laws (*nomoi*) when in the presence of witnesses, but ... the demands of nature (*phusis*) when not". "The demands of the law are adventitious, those of nature inescapable." Antiphon also turns the distinction against Greek pride: "by nature we are at birth in all respects equally capable of being either *barbaroi* or Greeks", since we all breathe through mouth and nostrils. And he argues that even truthful testimony harms someone who has done the witness no harm, so courts themselves are unjust. Thrasymachus and Callicles push similar lines in Plato.

Winton calls the *nomos*/*phusis* contrast "the single most fertile and most influential idea to emerge in fifth-century Greece" (p. 98). It covered nature against culture, convention, nurture and art, appearance against reality, the local against the universal, fact against value, and choice against necessity. "Its deployment in political discussion effectively marks the beginning of Western political theory" (p. 98).

The pamphlet known as the Old Oligarch, from the 430s or 420s, uses these tools for a critic of democracy (pp. 99–101). It is "just for the poor and the demos there to have more than the well-born", because "it is the common people who man the ships and confer power on the city" (1.2). He pardons the poor for being democrats, but a man of standing who chooses to live under democracy "is a man preparing to do wrong" (2.20). "The Old Oligarch turns Protagoras upside down" (p. 100). Against Pericles' claim that "we alone regard the man who takes no part in politics ... as someone who has nothing to contribute", an ideology of withdrawal grew up, voiced in Plato's *Theaetetus*. In the same years Sparta began to be idealised "as a polis that transcends politics" (p. 101).

### 13. Herodotus

Herodotus and Thucydides belong in a history of political thought because "the polis is itself centrally at issue in their works", as norm or as problem (p. 102). Herodotus' work was new: the critical inquiry, on a large scale, into the recent past, built on human testimony. "My duty is to report the things reported ... it is not to believe them all alike" (7.152) (p. 103). He presents the Persian War as a clash between the free, law-ruled polis and a despotic empire, and his sympathies are clear, but "Herodotus is no chauvinist" (pp. 103–104).

His key text is the story of Darius and the Callatians (3.38). Darius asked some Greeks what it would take for them to eat their dead fathers, and some Indians who did eat their fathers what it would take to burn them. Both refused in horror. "Pindar was right, in my judgment, when he said: 'Nomos is king (*basileus*) of all.'" (p. 104). Winton stresses that for Herodotus the variety of customs does not undermine custom. It is a fact, not a threat. Callicles later quoted the same line of Pindar to argue for the right of the strong.

Other stories carry the same themes (pp. 104–106). Two Spartans travelling to Xerxes to atone for murdered heralds tell the Persian governor Hydarnes: "A slave's life you understand; but, never having tasted liberty, you cannot tell whether it be sweet or no" (7.133–7). The exiled king Demaratus warns Xerxes that the Spartans "are not altogether free: law (*nomos*) is for them a master (*despotes*) whom they fear far more than your subjects do you" (7.104). Herodotus takes the polis as a norm of moderation against the excesses of barbarian kings and Greek tyrants, who "privatize what properly belongs to the community" (p. 106). Some read the whole work as a veiled warning to imperial Athens; Winton leaves the question open (p. 107).

Two passages engage directly with abstract politics.

- **Deioces** (1.96–100). Among the Medes, newly free of Assyria, a clever man earns a name for just dealing as a judge, then withdraws. Disorder returns, and the Medes choose a king so they can get on with their private affairs. They pick Deioces, who demands a bodyguard and a fortified palace, hides from view, forbids laughing or spitting in his presence, judges by written petitions and keeps spies everywhere. He has invented himself, like the gods of the Sisyphus fragment, as ruler of "an anti-polis", with a palace at its centre instead of an agora. The word *nomos* never appears in the story (pp. 107–108).
- **The Constitutional Debate** (3.80–2). After a usurper is overthrown, three Persian nobles argue. Otanes says absolute power corrupts even the best man with *hubris* and envy, so that he "subverts established usages ..., violates women, and executes men without trial". Rule by the many has "the fairest of names, *isonomia*", fills offices by lot, holds officials to account and debates in public. Megabyzus agrees about tyranny but says the mob is worse, rushing on mindlessly "like a torrent in winter spate", and proposes rule by the best. Darius argues that oligarchy breeds faction, faction murder, and murder monarchy, while democracy breeds cabals until a champion of the people becomes a monarch anyway. One man freed Persia, and good ancestral laws should not be changed (pp. 108–109).

There is no appeal to religion, and the debate is almost wholly abstract (pp. 109–110). It surely reflects Greek arguments of the fifth century, whatever Herodotus says. The *nomos*/*phusis* contrast runs under it, since each speaker claims that Persia has no real choice. All three judge by stability. And the settlement foreshadows the mixed constitution: Otanes withdraws, saying he wants "neither to rule nor to be ruled", and his house alone stays free, while the conspirators become a privileged nobility around the king. The Persians keep monarchy, where the Athenians in a similar moment chose democracy and rose to power (pp. 110–111). Herodotus ends his whole work with Cyrus warning the Persians against moving to richer land: "soft countries gave birth to soft men" (9.122).

### 14. Thucydides

Thucydides has no dreams, and gods and women barely appear (p. 111). He wants his work judged useful as "a possession for all time" (1.22), because events "in the nature of human affairs" will recur in similar forms (p. 112). Winton's question is how one war acquires universal meaning. Detailed treatment of the Melian Dialogue, the Mytilene debate, Corcyra and Sicily is in the site's note [[Thucydides on Human Nature and Power]]. What Winton adds is an argument about form.

First, the actors themselves interpret events in universal terms. At Melos the Athenians set justice aside and argue from power: "of the gods we believe, and of men we observe, that where they can rule nature (*phusis*) constrains them to do so. This law (*nomos*) was not made by us ... we did but inherit it, and shall bequeath it to all time" (5.105). Here the Athenians sound like Callicles quoting Pindar (pp. 112–113).

Second, Thucydides joins two kinds of writing (pp. 114–116). The narrative is annalistic and self-effacing, with almost no comment. The speeches, a fifth to a quarter of the text and often in opposed pairs, give argument of unlimited range. By his own admission at 1.22 they partly give what the situation required. His world is one of cities deciding policy in debate, where speech is "at once of enlightenment and obfuscation, of candour and humbug", and men lean on probability and self-interest to see through it. "Political debate is thus at the same time indispensable and problematic", and the pairing of speech and narrative lets Thucydides both show this and rise above it (p. 115). His narrative "realizes between author and reader an ideal relation all too rare between Thucydidean orator and audience" (p. 116).

Third, the structure carries the verdict (pp. 116–121). Athens lost the war; Sparta did not win it. Pericles had said "I am more afraid of our own mistakes than I am of the plans of our enemies" (1.144). Athens was destroyed by the very energy and boldness he praised. Under him it was "in name a democracy, in fact ... rule by the leading man" (2.65), and his successors competed for popular favour. Alcibiades was "a Pericles without principle or judgment" (p. 117). Melos is placed straight before Sicily, and the reversal speaks for itself: at Syracuse the Athenians become "sailors become landsmen", and the survivors end in the stone-quarries (pp. 117–118). The Hermae affair of 415 "opened up a fatal breach of mistrust ... between the demos and its traditional aristocratic leaders" (p. 120). Thucydides admired the oligarch Antiphon but condemned the coup of 411. He praised the regime of the Five Thousand that followed as "a moderate mixing-together (*xunkrasis*) in respect of the few and the many", because it averted civil war (8.97) (p. 121).

### Conclusion

The four chapters build one picture from different sides. Cartledge shows a political world unlike ours: small, stateless, run directly by its citizens, steeped in religion but without a clergy, committed to an equality it could never secure, and always threatened by faction. Raaflaub shows that the poets were already reflecting on that world long before anyone wrote a treatise. Homer judged leaders by their service to the community. Hesiod made justice the condition of a city's prosperity. Solon turned that faith into knowledge drawn from experience and acted on it. The Near East shared the concern for justice but placed it in the king and the gods, while the Greeks made men responsible for their own order.

Goldhill argues that the theatre was itself a democratic institution and that tragedy tested the city's ideas of community, agency and speech in public. His Creon shows a political theory stated clearly and then broken by the story around it. Winton follows the sophists' discovery that law is human-made, the *nomos*/*phusis* contrast that he calls the start of Western political theory, and the two historians who put the polis at the centre of their work. Herodotus found in the variety of customs no reason to doubt custom. Thucydides showed a great democracy undone by its own debates. Together the chapters suggest that political theory, when it came, was a late and narrower outgrowth of a culture that had been thinking about politics in other forms for three centuries.
