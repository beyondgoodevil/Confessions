In Homer, how you treat a stranger at your door shows what kind of person, and what kind of society, you are. The relationship is called *xenia*, "guest-friendship". The *Odyssey* is basically a string of visits: Athena at Ithaca, Telemachus at Pylos and Sparta, Odysseus among the Phaeacians, in the Cyclops' cave, at Eumaeus' hut, and finally as a beggar in his own house. Steve Reece's *The Stranger's Welcome* argues that these scenes follow a fixed traditional script. The poet's art lies in how he follows it, stretches it, cuts it or turns it upside down, and an audience that knew the script would catch every variation.

### 1. The Word and the God

- **One word, three meanings.** *Xenos* means "stranger", "guest" and sometimes "host". Finley calls this range "symbolic of the ambivalence which characterized all dealings with the stranger in that archaic world" (*World of Odysseus*, ch. 4). Reece adds the Latin pair *hostis* ("enemy") and *hospes* ("guest"), from the same root.
- **The two poles.** Finley's picture:
  - One pole is "fear, suspicion, distrust of the stranger", who has no kin to protect or avenge him.
  - The other pole is the duty of hospitality under **Zeus Xenios**, the god of strangers.
  - The Phaeacians show both. Athena warns Odysseus that "they do not readily bear with strangers" (7.32), yet they become proverbial for lavish entertainment.
  - The Cyclops stands at one pole only: "we give no heed to aegis-bearing Zeus" (9.275).
- **"Every stranger and beggar comes from Zeus."** Nausicaa says it to her maids (6.207–8), and Eumaeus says it to the disguised Odysseus. In McCrorie's version: "it's wrong to mistreat them. Every stranger and beggar / comes from Zeus. Our gifts are small but they're friendly" (14.56–8). Finley reads this as a moral advance: the gods' *themis* set "a new ideal" over the older reality of permanent war against the outsider.
- **The gods in disguise.** A suitor warns Antinous after he throws a footstool at the beggar: "The Gods may look like strangers from far off. / They take on every shape and move through our cities", watching "both the proud and the lawful" (17.485–7, McCrorie). Reece, translating the lines himself ("observing both the violence and the orderliness of men"), uses them as the epigraph of his Ithaca chapter. You never know who is at the door.
- **Outside Homer, the same rule:**
  - **Hesiod** puts the mistreatment of strangers among the great crimes: "It is equally bad to mistreat suppliants and strangers, or to sleep with your brother's wife" (*Works and Days* 327–8). One of the signs of the degenerate Iron Age is that "there will be no affection between guest and host" (182–3).
  - **Plato's *Laws*** explains why the stranger is specially protected: "being bereft of comrades and relatives", he "evokes more pity on the part of both human beings and gods", so "almost all the wrongs committed... against strangers are linked more closely to an avenging god" (729e–730a).
  - **Aeschylus** makes the Trojan War itself a punishment for a crime against xenia. Zeus, "guardian of host and guest", sends the Atreidae against Paris (*Agamemnon* 60–2), and Troy pays "for the dishonoring of the guest-table / and of hospitable Zeus" (701–2). Martin's introduction to McCrorie puts it plainly: the whole Trojan saga is "a mythic example of the catastrophes brought on by improper guest-host relations".

### 2. Gift and Counter-gift

- **Finley's rule.** "It may be stated as a flat rule of both primitive and archaic society that no one ever gave anything... without proper recompense, real or wishful, immediate or years away" (p. 64). A gift is always "the first half of a reciprocal action". Guest-gifts are the core of xenia.
- **Athena's dilemma** (1.311–18). Telemachus offers "Mentes" a parting treasure, "such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends". A god can't accept a human gift under a false identity, and refusing a gift was unthinkable: it would mean refusing the relationship. So she **postpones** it ("give it to me on my return") and tells him to choose one "worthy of a reciprocal exchange". Neither had mentioned a counter-gift, but "they understood each other perfectly" (Finley). Reece adds the theological point: the relationship between gods and men is "vertical", so gods get sacrifices and give blessings rather than swapping gifts.
- **Gifts given "in vain."** The disguised Odysseus tells Laertes he once entertained Odysseus and gave him seven talents of gold, a silver bowl, cloaks, carpets and four women. Laertes weeps: "The countless gifts which you gave, you bestowed in vain" (24.283), since his son isn't alive to repay them. A gift that can't be returned is wasted.
- **Inherited friendship.** In *Iliad* 6, Glaucus and Diomedes meet on the battlefield and find their grandfathers were guest-friends. Diomedes: "I am your friend and host in the heart of Argos; you are mine in Lykia". They swap armour, and Glaucus gives "gold for bronze, for nine oxen's worth the worth of a hundred" (6.224–36, Lattimore). Finley insists this is "not comedy": guest-friendship was "the alternative to marriage in forging bonds" between households, and "as formal and as evocative of rights and duties as marriage". It overrides even the war.
- **What makes a good gift.**
  - **Storable treasure** (*keimēlion*): metal cups, bowls, tripods, weapons, fine clothes. Telemachus refuses Menelaus' horses, since Ithaca has no plains: "let it be treasure" (4.600).
  - **Gifts with a history** are worth most: Menelaus' krater came from the king of Sidon (4.613–19), and the **bow that kills the suitors** was a guest-gift from Iphitus (21.31–3).
- **Into politics.** Finley traces the institution forward: Herodotus' Croesus sends gifts to Sparta, and the Spartans "took the oaths of guest-friendship and alliance" (Hdt. 1.69). In Homer it is still a tie between individuals, "Diomedes and Glaucus, 'Mentes' and Telemachus", not between states. But for powerful lords it is already "the Homeric version, or forerunner, of political and military alliances".

### 3. The Script: Reece's Grid

Reece abstracts the conventional elements of 18 hospitality scenes (in the *Iliad*, *Odyssey* and *Homeric Hymns*) into a grid of 25 headings and 38 sub-elements. No scene has them all and no two scenes are identical, but the **order** underlies every scene: it's the "syntax" of hospitality. Grouped:

| Stage | Elements |
|---|---|
| **Approach** | I. maiden at the well / youth on the road; II. arrival; III. description of the house and its people; IV. dog at the door; V. waiting at the threshold; VI. supplication |
| **Reception** | VII. host sees the visitor, (hesitates), rises, approaches, (sees to the horses), takes his hand, says welcome, takes his spear, leads him in; VIII. seat |
| **Meal** | IX. feast (preparation, eating, end); X. after-dinner drink |
| **Exchange** | XI. identification (host asks, guest answers); XII. exchange of information; XIII. entertainment; XIV. guest blesses host; XV. guest shares in libation or sacrifice |
| **Night** | XVI. guest asks to sleep; XVII. bed; XVIII. bath |
| **Departure** | XIX. host detains guest; XX. guest-gifts; XXI. departure meal; XXII. departure libation; XXIII. farewell blessing; XXIV. departure omen; XXV. escort to the next destination |

Some conventions are worth knowing because the poem plays with them so much:

- **Maiden at the well.** Odysseus meets Nausicaa at the river, Athena disguised as a girl with a water jar, and the Laestrygonian king's daughter at a spring (with a less fortunate outcome). In the *Hymn to Demeter*, the goddess meets the daughters of Celeus at a well. The male version is a youth on the road: Hermes before Circe's house, and Athena as a young shepherd on Ithaca.
- **The dog at the door.** The gold and silver dogs of Alcinous, the fawning wolves and lions around Circe's house, Eumaeus' dogs that nearly maul Odysseus "even as he arrives at his own steading", and finally **Argus**, the neglected dog on the dung heap who is "a sympathetic representation of his master" (Reece).
- **The threshold.** Crossing it puts the visitor under the master's protection. Equals **stand** at the door; beggars and suppliants **sit**. Odysseus enters the Cyclops' cave uninvited, and Polyphemus seals that same threshold with a rock. Back home, Odysseus takes up a permanent place on his own threshold, "whether master or beggar, insider or outsider".
- **Feed first, ask later.** The good host gives food before asking the stranger's name or business. Telemachus: "Greetings, stranger, you will be welcomed by us; but when you have partaken of the meal, you will tell us what you need" (1.123–4, Reece's translation). Nestor, Menelaus, Arete and Eumaeus observe the rule. Calypso asks too early, Alcinous presses Odysseus before his belly is full, and the Cyclops demands names on sight (9.252).
- **Seats encode status.** The *thronos* (upright chair with arms) is for gods, nobles and honoured guests; the *klismos* (reclining chair) for men at ease and women; the *diphros* (stool) for servants and humble folk. Telemachus gives Athena the throne and takes the *klismos* himself, as Achilles does for Priam. Odysseus at home moves from a stool to a throne as he goes from beggar to master (19.97, 23.164).
- **The bath transforms.** The guest often rises from it "looking like a god". That's why Odysseus, who mustn't be recognised, asks only for a **footbath**, and the footbath reveals his scar (19.386ff.; see [[Auerbach's Mimesis: Odysseus' Scar, Peter's Denial and Figural Realism]]).
- **The bed's geography.** The guest sleeps in the **portico**, the host in the inner room. Eumaeus reverses this: he gives the guest the bed by the fire and sleeps outside himself. Odysseus moves from the beggars' lounge (Melantho's suggestion) to the portico (Penelope's offer) to the bedroom (23.295): "beggar to guest to master".
- **Detention.** Menelaus states the ideal: "It is as blameworthy to urge a guest to leave who does not want to as it is to detain a guest who is eager to leave. One must grant hospitality to a guest who is present and grant conveyance to a guest who wants to leave" (15.72–4, Reece's translation). "All things are better in due measure." Yet he himself detains Telemachus.

### 4. The Scenes One by One

- **Ithaca: Athena-Mentes (Book 1) as a theoxeny.**
  - The scene is a disguised god testing mortals' hospitality, a worldwide folktale motif. Telemachus passes and the suitors fail.
  - Telemachus is "by far the first" to see the stranger, is indignant she's been kept waiting, takes her spear and puts it "in his father's own spear stand", seats her on a throne apart from the suitors, and feeds her before asking questions.
  - Reece's best point: the meal for Telemachus and Athena uses the full **five-line formula** for serving a guest (water from a golden pitcher, a polished table, a respected housekeeper with bread). The suitors' meal gets a hurried jumble of lines borrowed from a libation scene. Homer marks the moral difference "on the level of form as well as content".
  - The suitors force the bard to sing "under compulsion" (*anankēi*, 1.154).
- **Pylos and Sparta (Books 3–4, 15): two good hosts, both too good.**
  - **Nestor** is pious and personal: sacrifices everywhere, his own sons serve, his youngest daughter Polycaste bathes the guest, and Pisistratus becomes Telemachus' friend. But the provisions are simple.
  - **Menelaus** is lavish and impersonal: a herald meets them, a hot bath is always ready, there are rich gifts and fine beds, but no sacrifices.
  - Both **detain** the guest, so Telemachus' journey mirrors his father's. On the way home Telemachus asks Pisistratus to drop him at his ship rather than face another round of Nestor's hospitality (15.195–214).
- **The Phaeacians (Books 5–13): the full script, with hesitation.**
  - Odysseus sits as a suppliant in the ashes of the hearth, and Alcinous and Arete sit silent until old Echeneus rebukes them (7.153–66). The Spartans and Phaeacians both hesitate, and Reece suggests a reason in each case: Sparta's last famous guest was Paris, and the Phaeacians are fated to be punished for escorting strangers (13.170–83).
  - Once the hesitation is over, they perform **every element**: the longest feasts, games, songs, the longest interrogation (answered by the four-book *Apologoi*), mountains of gifts, and finally the escort home (*pompē*).
- **The Cyclops (Book 9): the anti-host.** Polyphemus perverts every element:
  - He demands names before food.
  - Instead of serving a feast, he makes a feast of his guests; Homer applies the ordinary banquet formula to cannibalism.
  - His **guest-gift** is to eat "No-one" last. In McCrorie's version: "I'll dine on No-one myself the last of his war-friends, / the rest go first—there's a gift for a stranger!" (9.369–70).
  - His farewell **blessing** becomes a **curse**: that Odysseus come home "late and badly off" and find "troubles at home" (9.534–5).
  - Reece compared the 200-plus folktale versions of the blinded ogre. Only in Homer's is the hero motivated by curiosity about whether the ogre is "friendly to guests" and by "desire to acquire guest-gifts", and only in Homer's do hero and ogre exchange gifts (wine for the "privilege" of being eaten last).
  - Odysseus is not a model guest either. He walks in uninvited and eats the cheese. His departure sacrifice of the stolen ram is refused by Zeus (9.553), "apparently because the stolen ram is symbolic of Odysseus' violations of hospitality as a guest".
- **The *Apologoi*: hospitality as danger.** Reece notes that every host in Odysseus' wanderings is either murderous or obstructive: "There is no middle ground."
  - The guest becomes food (Cyclops, Laestrygonians).
  - The food makes you forget home (the Lotus-eaters, Circe's drugged meal), and so does song (the Sirens).
  - The guest-gift drives you away from home (Aeolus' bag of winds).
  - The bed detains you for years (Circe, Calypso).
- **Eumaeus (Books 14–16): humble perfection.**
  - Almost every element is present, scaled down to a swineherd's hut. He seats the guest on a goatskin from his own bed, says he won't ask questions until they've eaten (14.45–7), gives him the chine (the portion of honour), refills his own cup for him, and lends his own cloak as a blanket.
  - Reece's linguistic point: where Homer had to adapt the palace formulae to a hut, there's "a high concentration of late linguistic forms". So tales of a swineherd's hospitality may not have been part of the inherited epic tradition, and Homer improvised them in his own vernacular.
- **The suitors (Books 17–23): everything inverted.**
  - Guests have made themselves hosts in someone else's house, and the real master is the guest.
  - Footstools and the meat basket, the tools of welcome, become **missiles**: Antinous' stool, Eurymachus' stool, Ctesippus' ox-hoof "guest-gift" (20.296).
  - "Escort" (*pompē*) becomes being thrown out of the house, or **sold as a slave** to Sicily or to King Echetus, who cuts off noses and ears.
  - The guest supplies the entertainment: the boxing match with Irus.
  - The punishments answer in kind. The cowherd Philoitius kills Ctesippus with "My spear is your guest-gift, matching the ox-hoof you / offered godlike Odysseus before" (22.290–1, McCrorie). Odysseus kills the suitors with Iphitus' guest-gift bow, just as he got the Cyclops drunk with Maron's guest-gift wine. And the "song and the lyre" of the last feast (21.430) is the bow itself (see [[The Bow and the Lyre: A Platonic Reading of the Odyssey]]).
  - Antinous' stool draws the guest's **curse** instead of a blessing: "If there are gods and Furies for beggars, may death come upon Antinous before marriage" (17.475–6).

### 5. The Architecture of the Poem

Reece's last chapter argues that the hospitality scenes are the **signposts** by which an oral poet remembered "the course" (*oimē*) of his song. Odysseus' own question is "What, then, shall I narrate first, and what last?" (9.14). The scenes form a ring:

> Athena in Ithaca → Pylos → Pherae → Sparta → Phaeacians → [embedded: the *Apologoi*] → Phaeacians → Eumaeus → Sparta → Pherae → Pylos (aborted) → Odysseus in Ithaca

They interact in four ways:
- **Elaboration.** Long scenes carry weight and short ones are skeletons, but the audience fills the gaps. Diocles' three-line hospitality at Pherae (3.488–90) still implies the whole ritual.
- **Crescendo.** In the first four scenes the guest arrives **later and later** in the meal (preparations, roasting, the feast under way, libations before bed), and each scene carries the ritual **further** (meal only; bath and bed; gifts and farewells; finally the escort). Phaeacia combines Pylos and Sparta: beach in the morning, palace in the evening, a prompt young greeter, a hesitant host. Reece compares the arming scenes in the *Iliad*: Paris, Agamemnon, Patroclus, Achilles.
- **Doublets.** Telemachus at Sparta and Odysseus at Scheria happen at the same time and mirror each other: hesitant hosts, awe at the palace (the same simile), a powerful queen, stories of the returns, and the guest weeping and hiding his face in a purple cloak, which gives him away. Phaeacia also previews Ithaca: the stranger wakes on a strange shore, Athena hides him, a young person on the edge of adulthood meets him, there's a contest among suitors and a "wedding" feast. The Cyclops and the suitors also form a pair, and Odysseus links them himself: "Endure, my heart! You once endured something even more shameful... when the Cyclops... was devouring my mighty companions" (20.18–21).
- **Foils.** Pylos (pious and personal) is set against Sparta (secular and lavish). The Cyclopes and the Phaeacians are polar opposites, though once neighbours and both descended from Poseidon. When Odysseus tells the Cyclops story to the Phaeacians, it's a warning to his hosts, while the Circe and Calypso stories warn them against the opposite extreme of keeping him too long.
- **The frame is a theoxeny.** The poem opens with a disguised goddess testing a house, and closes with a disguised hero who is repeatedly taken for a god (by Telemachus at 16.178–85, by the suitors at 17.485–7). This "places Odysseus' actions against the suitors within the realm of divine justice rather than human vengeance."

### 6. The Theoxeny After Homer

The tale of the disguised god who tests a household never went away. Its classic form is Ovid's **Baucis and Philemon** (*Met.* 8.611–724). Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as mortals, wander Phrygia: "A thousand homes they came to, seeking rest; / a thousand doors were bolted fast against them; / one home received them, humble, just a hut" (Martin's translation).
- The old couple give them a bench with a rough blanket, warm water for their feet, a table steadied with a potsherd, and a little bacon from the rafter, but "more than these were beaming looks". It's Eumaeus' hut in Latin.
- The wine bowl refills itself, the gods reveal themselves, the inhospitable neighbourhood is flooded, and the hut becomes a temple.
- Reece lists other examples in Ovid, Nonnus and Apollodorus: Jupiter at Lycaon's table (*Met.* 1.211–41), and Hyrieus (*Fasti* 5.495–536).

### 7. Assessment

- **What Reece does well.**
  - He makes the repeated scenes **readable**. Once you know the script, Telemachus taking Athena's spear, the suitors' sloppy formula, Eumaeus sleeping outside and the Cyclops' "gift" all register as deliberate.
  - His opening idea is right: the formulas and type-scenes are not filler but "dynamic ingredients" that have "accrued deep and significant meaning" through use.
  - He is also careful about the text. His section on **concordance interpolations** shows that copyists tended to "complete" short type-scenes with lines from fuller ones. The whole of Circe's feast formula (10.368–72) is missing from the oldest manuscripts. He warns that theories about oral composition built on the vulgate can rest on "late scribal additions".
- **Limits.**
  - The grid is, as he says, "a highly artificial abstraction". Some elements occur only once (horses, the departure omen), and he has to argue they were conventional anyway.
  - The method assumes a single poet with "good control over the architectonics of his entire epic", which oralists like Parry and Lord would query. Reece answers with the Serbo-Croatian singer Avdo Međedović, who expanded a song he'd just heard to three times its length while keeping the order of its themes.
  - The idea that Eumaeus' hospitality was improvised in "late" language is suggestive but hard to prove.
- **Finley and Reece together.** Finley reads xenia as **history**: a real archaic institution, the forerunner of alliances and a substitute for kinship abroad. Reece reads it as **poetics**: the "syntax" of a type-scene. The two readings need each other. The script only means something because the institution was real, and the institution is known to us mainly through the script.
- **Xenia and the poem's justice.** Hospitality gives the *Odyssey* its moral measure. The suitors are guilty because they abuse a guest, so their death becomes "divine justice rather than human vengeance". That's how the poem presents it: Penelope says a god killed them because they "honored no one... neither an evil nor a good man, whoever approached them" (23.65–6, Reece's translation). Readers like Benardete doubt that the suitors' offence matches their punishment; Reece's reading shows how much the poem relies on xenia to make that punishment look just.

### Conclusion

Xenia is the Homeric world's answer to the stranger. He might be an enemy, a beggar or a god, so he must be fed, seated, bathed, questioned, given gifts and sent home, in that order, and the relationship binds the families for generations. Reece shows that Homer built the *Odyssey* out of variations on this one ritual. It's done perfectly at Pylos and in the swineherd's hut, extravagantly in Sparta and Phaeacia, monstrously in the Cyclops' cave, and backwards by the suitors in Odysseus' own house. The poem opens and closes with a disguised visitor testing the household, and ends by punishing the hosts who failed the test.
