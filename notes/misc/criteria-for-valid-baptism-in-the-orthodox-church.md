## The Four Criteria

1. **Proper Form**:
    - The baptism must be performed using the **Trinitarian formula**, as commanded by Christ:  
      *"In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit"* (Matthew 28:19).
    - Baptisms conducted without this formula, such as those performed solely "in the name of Jesus," are not considered valid.
2. **Proper Matter**:
    - Baptism must involve **water**, which symbolizes both cleansing from sin and the new birth in Christ. The preferred method is **triple immersion** in water, symbolizing the death, burial, and resurrection of Christ.
    - Pouring or sprinkling is generally not accepted unless immersion was impossible due to extraordinary circumstances (e.g., illness).
3. **Proper Intention**:
    - The intent must be to perform a baptism as understood by the **Orthodox Church**. If the intention is to perform a symbolic act or a ritual devoid of sacramental grace, it may be deemed invalid.
4. **Canonical Standing of the Church or Minister**:
    - Baptism must be performed within a Church that maintains **apostolic succession** and adheres to the **Orthodox faith**.
    - Baptisms performed by heretical or schismatic groups may be subject to review. If the group holds an erroneous understanding of the Trinity or the sacraments, their baptisms are often considered invalid.

## How the Orthodox Church Determines Validity

### 1. Evaluation by Canon Law and Tradition

- The Church applies the principles outlined in the **Canons of the Ecumenical Councils** and **patristic writings**. For example, the **Seventh Canon of the Second Ecumenical Council (381 AD)** and the **Nineteenth Canon of the Council of Carthage** address the reception of heretics and their sacraments.
    - **St. Basil the Great** distinguished between three categories of groups:
        1. **Schismatics**: Those who have broken from the Church but retain valid sacraments.
        2. **Heretics**: Those who deny fundamental dogmas of the faith (e.g., the Trinity) and whose baptisms are invalid.
        3. **Catholic-minded separated groups**: Groups with valid baptisms that still adhere to core Trinitarian beliefs.

### 2. Reception of Converts

Depending on the specific case, converts to Orthodoxy may be received by:

- **Baptism**: If the prior baptism was invalid (e.g., improper form or performed by a non-Trinitarian group).
- **Chrismation**: If the baptism is deemed valid but lacks the seal of the Holy Spirit, as in certain schismatic communities.
- **Confession of Faith**: If the baptism is fully valid, only a profession of Orthodox faith is required.

### 3. Economia (Dispensation)

- The Orthodox Church sometimes applies **economia**, a pastoral approach that allows flexibility without compromising doctrine. For example, baptisms performed in Roman Catholic or some Protestant contexts may be **accepted as valid** under economia, even if they did not follow the ideal triple immersion, provided the Trinitarian formula and proper intent were present.

## Examples of Invalid Baptisms

1. **Non-Trinitarian Baptisms**:
    - Groups like **Jehovah's Witnesses** or **Mormons**, which reject the orthodox understanding of the Trinity, perform invalid baptisms.
2. **Symbolic Baptisms**:
    - Some Protestant groups view baptism as purely symbolic and do not intend it as a sacrament conveying grace. Such baptisms may be invalid.
3. **Improper Form or Intent**:
    - Baptisms performed "in Jesus' name only" (common in Oneness Pentecostalism) are not valid since they lack the Trinitarian formula.

## Patristic and Conciliar Authority

1. **Council of Carthage (258 AD)**:
    - Reaffirmed the invalidity of heretical baptisms.
2. **St. Cyprian of Carthage**:
    - Argued that baptisms outside the Church are invalid. However, this was nuanced by later councils and Fathers like St. Basil, who allowed flexibility.
3. **Second Ecumenical Council (381 AD)**:
    - Accepted the baptisms of certain schismatic groups (e.g., Arians) under specific circumstances.

## Summary

The Orthodox Church evaluates baptism based on its **form, matter, intent, and context**. The sacraments' validity depends on their alignment with the faith and practice of the Church. Through canonical precision and pastoral flexibility (**economia**), Orthodoxy ensures that each case is treated faithfully yet pastorally.
