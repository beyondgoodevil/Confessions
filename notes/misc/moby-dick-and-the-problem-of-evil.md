> "Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook?… Upon earth there is not his like, who is made without fear. He beholdeth all high things: he is a king over all the children of pride." (Job 41:1, 33–34)

> "And I only am escaped alone to tell thee." (Job 1:15, the epigraph to Melville's Epilogue)

*Moby-Dick* begins with a list of "Extracts" about whales, Job's Leviathan among them, and ends with a line from Job's messengers. In between, a captain who has lost his leg to a white whale drives a ship of thirty men round the world to kill it, and all but one drown. Hawthorne wrote of his friend that Melville "can neither believe, nor be comfortable in his unbelief". The book is that discomfort in 135 chapters. Melville wrote to Hawthorne when he finished: "I have written a wicked book, and feel spotless as the lamb."

## 1. The Names

- **Ishmael** is Abraham's son by the slave Hagar, cast out into the wilderness: "his hand will be against every man" (Gen 16:12). "Call me Ishmael" introduces a man who is choosing an outcast's name.
- **Ahab** is the wicked king of Israel who "did more to provoke the LORD God of Israel to anger than all the kings… before him" (1 Kgs 16:33). He married Jezebel, served Baal and was killed in battle, where "the dogs licked up his blood" (1 Kgs 22:38). In chapter 19 a ragged prophet called **Elijah**, Ahab's ancient enemy, warns Ishmael off the ship.
- The ship that rescues Ishmael is the **Rachel**, searching for her lost boys: "Rachel weeping for her children… because they were not" (Jer 31:15).

## 2. Ishmael's Despair and Father Mapple (chs. 1, 7–9)

- Ishmael goes to sea when it is "a damp, drizzly November in my soul", when he finds himself following funerals and has to stop himself "knocking people's hats off". It is "my substitute for pistol and ball."
- Anderson's thesis: the novel is Ishmael's transformation from despairing philosopher to the cheerful narrator who tells the tale, "from melancholy philosopher to joyful sage". He reads it as a Zarathustran *amor fati* and as Melville's "intellectual-spiritual autobiography".
- In the Whalemen's Chapel, among memorial tablets to the drowned, **Father Mapple** climbs a rope ladder into a ship's-prow pulpit and preaches on **Jonah**. Jonah tried to flee God's command, was swallowed, prayed from "the belly of hell" (Jonah 2:2) and was delivered. The lesson: "if we obey God, we must disobey ourselves; and it is in this disobeying ourselves, wherein the hardness of obeying God consists." It ends in "Delight": "Delight is to him… who against the proud gods and commodores of this earth, ever stands forth his own inexorable self".
- Anderson reads this as Phaedo-Platonism, the soul imprisoned and seeking "deliverance". It is also the novel's one fully Christian voice, and it sets the terms: obey God against yourself, or obey yourself against God. Ahab will choose the second.

## 3. Ahab's Theology (chs. 36, 41)

**The Quarter-Deck**
- Ahab nails a gold doubloon to the mast for whoever first sights the white whale, and swears the crew to "hunt him… round perdition's flames".
- To Starbuck, who protests that taking vengeance on "a dumb brute" is "blasphemous":
  > "All visible objects, man, are but as pasteboard masks. But in each event… some unknown but still reasoning thing puts forth the mouldings of its features from behind the unreasoning mask. If man will strike, strike through the mask! How can the prisoner reach outside except by thrusting through the wall? To me, the white whale is that wall, shoved near to me."
- He half-suspects "there's naught beyond", but it does not matter: "Be the white whale agent, or be the white whale principal, I will wreak that hate upon him." "Talk not to me of blasphemy, man; I'd strike the sun if it insulted me."
- **Anderson**: Ahab is "a metaphysician infected with an inchoate nihilism". He is a Platonist who feels the body as a prison but "has no use for purification", so he tries "a violent jailbreak". He is at once "an idealist and a materialist", and this contradiction kills him.

**"Moby Dick"** (ch. 41)
- The sailors' whale has the attributes of God: "ubiquitous", "immortal", acting with "supernatural agencies". He also has "unexampled, intelligent malignity", "infernal aforethought", "the great gliding demon of the seas of life".
- For Ahab the whale became the sum of "all his intellectual and spiritual exasperations… all the subtle demonisms of life and thought; all evil". He "piled upon the whale's white hump the sum of all the general rage and hate felt by his whole race from Adam down". After losing his leg, his "torn body and gashed soul bled into one another".

## 4. The Whiteness and the Void (chs. 42, 96, 99)

**"The Whiteness of the Whale"**
- White is the colour of holiness, of priests' robes and the Lamb, and yet it "strikes… panic to the soul": the polar bear, the albino, snow, the shroud. A Vermont colt panics at the smell of a buffalo robe it has never seen, by an "instinct of the knowledge of the demonism in the world".
- The deepest terror: "is it that by its indefiniteness it shadows forth the heartless voids and immensities of the universe… a colorless, all-color of atheism from which we shrink?" Whiteness is "not so much a color as the visible absence of color".
- Anderson links this to the fall of the Aristotelian cosmos and then of its God: the "invisible spheres were formed in fright".

**"The Try-Works"** (ch. 96)
- Ishmael falls asleep at the helm by the hellish glare of the furnaces boiling blubber. He wakes turned round, the ship "rushing from all havens astern". Anderson sets this beside Nietzsche's madman: "straying as through an infinite nothing."
- Ishmael's counsel: "Look not too long in the face of the fire, O man!… There is a wisdom that is woe; but there is a woe that is madness." Solomon is "the truest of all men", and Ecclesiastes "the fine hammered steel of woe".

**"The Doubloon"** (ch. 99)
- Each man reads the coin's mountains, flame and zodiac differently. Stubb: "There's another rendering now; but still one text." Ishmael: "some certain significance lurks in all things, else all things are little worth, and the round world itself but an empty cipher."
- Melville to Hawthorne (1851): "perhaps, after all, there is no secret… It turns out, at last, to consist in a triangle, a mallet, and an apron – nothing more!" Anderson's gloss: Melville "understands that nihilism follows from the death of God."

## 5. Fire, Tears and the End (chs. 119, 132, 135, Epilogue)

**"The Candles"**
- In a typhoon, St Elmo's fire burns on the masts. Ahab grips the lightning chain and addresses the fire: "Oh! thou clear spirit of clear fire… I now know thee, and I now know that thy right worship is defiance." He calls himself "darkness leaping out of light". Starbuck: "God, God is against thee, old man; forbear!"
- Anderson: this is not Camus's absurd hero but Nietzsche's **active nihilism**, "one helps to destroy". Ahab "does not believe that God is dead… Ahab inhabits a hostile universe."

**"The Symphony"**
- On a mild day Ahab drops "one tear into the sea" and admits he is "more a demon than a man". He remembers his young wife and child, and almost turns home. Then: "What is it, what nameless, inscrutable, unearthly thing is it… Is Ahab, Ahab? Is it I, God, or who, that lifts this arm?"
- Anderson: Ahab "requires God precisely because he needs to hold God responsible for his suffering". Nietzsche's "innocence of becoming", where no one is to blame, is exactly what he cannot accept.

**The last chase**
- Starbuck: "Moby Dick seeks thee not. It is thou, thou, that madly seekest him!" Ahab's last words: "to the last I grapple with thee; from hell's heart I stab at thee; for hate's sake I spit my last breath at thee." The line catches him round the neck and he is gone. The whale smashes the *Pequod*, and the sea closes "as it rolled five thousand years ago".
- **Epilogue**: "And I only am escaped alone to tell thee." Ishmael floats on **Queequeg's coffin**, carved with his tattoos and made into a life-buoy, until "the devious-cruising Rachel, that in her retracing search after her missing children, only found another orphan."
- Anderson: Ishmael rides *on* the coffin rather than lying in it, overcoming "the death-obsessed… thought-world of Phaedo-Platonism". He compares Zarathustra's dream of a coffin that "burst and spewed out a thousandfold laughter".

## 6. Ahab and Job

## 7. Assessment

*Mine.*

**Melville saw the problem truly**
- The book refuses the cheap theodicies. Ishmael's colt that smells "the demonism in the world" and Ahab's "all evil… visibly personified" are serious. Evil is real, and the world is not obviously friendly. This is the honesty of Job's own book. Its friends' pious explanations are what God rejects: "ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath" (42:7). See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]] and [[Wandering in Darkness]].
- And Melville shows where hatred of God leads. Ahab is not Prometheus but the king of Israel who sold himself "to work evil", and he drags thirty men to death for his quarrel. His "right worship is defiance" is the formula of the demonic. "More a demon than a man" is his own confession. Pride, the root passion, makes a god of one's own grievance. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]] and [[The Ladder of Divine Ascent]].

**Ahab's error: the mask and the wall**
- Ahab assumes that if there is something behind the mask, it is an enemy, and that the only way through the wall is a harpoon. Job's protest is just as fierce, but it is addressed to God *as a person*. He holds on to the relationship even while accusing ("Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him", 13:15). That is why God answers him and does not answer Ahab. Ahab wants to "strike through", not to see. Job asked to see, and was shown.
- What God shows Job is **Leviathan**: creation's wildness, beyond man's control, "made without fear… a king over all the children of pride". God's answer is not an explanation but a vision of a world larger and stranger than Job's theory, with God present in it. Melville's white whale is Leviathan seen without God, so it can only be "malignity" or "void". Read Job 41 again after *Moby-Dick*: the same beast that kills Ahab is in Job a creature God *boasts of*.
- **The Christian answer to "How can the prisoner reach outside except by thrusting through the wall?"** is that the prisoner cannot, but the One outside has come in. Christ "descended into hell" and broke the gates from within. The wall was pierced from God's side, at the cost of God's own body: "one of the soldiers with a spear pierced his side" (John 19:34). Ahab tries to pierce God; in the Gospel, God lets Himself be pierced. See [[The Descent into Hades]] and [[Why Jesus' Death and Resurrection Had to Be So Violent]].

**Whiteness and darkness**
- "The colorless, all-color of atheism" is the apophatic seen from outside. The Fathers also say God is not any colour, not any thing, "darkness" to the mind. Dionysius calls Him "beyond being". For Moses this darkness was "the thick darkness where God was" (Exod 20:21), the place of meeting. For Ishmael at the try-works it is a void "rushing from all havens". The difference is not in the darkness but in whether one enters it in prayer or in rage. See [[Apophatic and Cataphatic Theology]] and [[Nothingness East and West: Nishitani, Tanabe, Abe and the Christian Nihil]].

**Ishmael, Jonah and the coffin**
- Anderson reads Ishmael's survival as Zarathustra's laughter, and that is half right: Ishmael is the one who did not hate. But Melville's own frame is biblical. Mapple's sermon is Jonah, and Christ called His resurrection "the sign of the prophet Jonas" (Matt 12:39–40). Ishmael "escaped alone", like Job's messengers, to bear witness. He is saved by a **coffin that floats**, made by a pagan friend who loved him, and picked up by **Rachel**, the mother weeping for her children. That is baptismal imagery: "buried with him by baptism… raised up" (Rom 6:4). The tomb becomes the ark (1 Pet 3:20–21). Melville could not believe it, but he could not stop writing it. See [[1 Peter 3:20-21: Baptism Is Like the Ark]].
- The book's real moral contrast is not Plato against Nietzsche but **hatred against love**. On one side is Ahab's "for hate's sake". On the other are Queequeg's friendship ("a bosom friend"), the sailors squeezing spermaceti in "A Squeeze of the Hand" ("Come; let us squeeze hands all round"), and Starbuck's longing for his wife and child. Ishmael survives because he was taught to love by a cannibal. "He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love" (1 John 4:8).

**What I take from it**
- Hawthorne was right: Melville could "neither believe, nor be comfortable in his unbelief". *Moby-Dick* is what that state looks like when it is honest. It is better than easy faith, and far better than easy atheism. The way out is Job's: keep speaking *to* God, even in accusation, until He answers. "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee" (Job 42:5).
