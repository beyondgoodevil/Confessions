The *Phaedo* is Plato's account of the last day of **Socrates**, who spends the hours before he drinks the hemlock arguing that the **soul is immortal** and that a philosopher should welcome death. Its central claim is that philosophy itself is "**practice for dying**": the philosopher tries all his life to separate his soul from the body, and death only finishes the job. Socrates offers four **arguments for immortality**, and almost every reader, ancient and modern, has found them weak. So the real puzzle of the dialogue is why Plato put shaky arguments into the mouth of a dying man and still made this the most moving thing he wrote. This note follows the dialogue in order, in Grube's translation, with the commentaries of Gallop, Arieti, Burger, Lampert and the Neoplatonist Olympiodorus.

### 1. The Frame: Phlius, Theseus' Ship and an Absent Plato

The story is told after the event. Phaedo, one of the friends present, stops at Phlius in the Peloponnese on his way home and tells it to **Echecrates**. Echecrates belongs to a group of Pythagoreans, and so do Socrates' two main partners, **Simmias and Cebes**, Thebans who had studied with the Pythagorean Philolaus. Cooper's introduction points out that the Pythagorean themes of immortality, reincarnation and purification, and the theory of eternal Forms tied to them, are "Plato's own contribution". The Socrates of the *Apology* had been noncommittal about life after death.

Plato goes out of his way to tell us he was not there: "Plato, I believe, was ill" (59b). The execution had been delayed because of the ship the Athenians send each year to Delos. It commemorates **Theseus**, who sailed to Crete with fourteen young victims for the Minotaur and saved them (58a–c). Dorter, cited by Arieti, notes that fourteen named friends are with Socrates on his last day. The frame casts Socrates as a new Theseus, which Lampert develops: he will slay the "real Minotaur", the fear of death.

Phaedo describes the mood as "a strange feeling, an unaccustomed mixture of pleasure and pain" (59a). Socrates' first act sets the theme. Released from his chains, he rubs his leg and reflects that pleasure and pain are "like two creatures with one head", so that whoever catches one gets the other (60b–c). Burger reads this as a model of the whole dialogue: opposites that cannot be separated, like soul and body.

Two more details matter. Socrates sends his wife Xanthippe home, and he reveals that in prison he has been writing poetry, Aesop's fables in verse and a hymn to Apollo. He did it because of a recurring dream that told him to "practice and cultivate the arts". He had always taken the dream to mean philosophy, "the highest kind of art", but now he obeys it literally, just in case (60e–61b).

### 2. Why Not Suicide? Why Welcome Death?

Socrates tells Evenus, through Cebes, to "follow me as soon as possible", and then adds that he must not kill himself. Cebes catches the contradiction at once. Socrates answers with a doctrine from the Mysteries, "that we men are in a kind of prison", and with a simpler image: the gods are our guardians and we are their possessions, so we must wait until a god sends "some necessity" (62b–c).

Cebes presses harder. If the gods are good masters, a wise man should be sorry to leave their service (62d–e). Socrates is pleased with the objection, and offers to make a defence "more convincing than it was to the jury" (63b). The *Phaedo* is presented as a second *Apology*, this time before friends who are also Pythagorean sceptics.

### 3. Philosophy as Practice for Dying (64a–69e)

The core claim:

> I am afraid that other people do not realize that the one aim of those who practice philosophy in the proper manner is to practice for dying and death. (64a)

Death is defined as "the separation of the soul from the body" (64c). The argument then runs:
- The philosopher does not care for the pleasures of food, drink, sex or fine clothes (64d–e).
- The body is an obstacle to knowledge. The senses are inaccurate, and we have never seen the Just itself, the Beautiful or the Good "with your eyes" (65b–d).
- So the soul thinks best "when it is most by itself", using "pure thought alone" (65c–66a).
- The body also distracts us. It fills us "with wants, desires, fears, all sorts of illusions", and "all wars are due to the desire to acquire wealth", which is for the body's sake (66c–d).
- So "if we are ever to have pure knowledge, we must escape from the body" (66d–e). Either we never get wisdom, or we get it after death.

Philosophers are therefore "in training for dying and they fear death least of all men" (67e). A man who resents dying was "not a lover of wisdom but a lover of the body" (68b–c).

Socrates then contrasts **true and slavish virtue** (68c–69d). Ordinary people are brave out of fear of something worse, and moderate because they want other pleasures more. This is "to exchange pleasures for pleasures, pains for pains and fears for fears ... like coins". But "the only valid currency for which all these things should be exchanged is wisdom". Virtue without wisdom "is only an illusory appearance of virtue; it is in fact fit for slaves". He ends with a line from the Mysteries: "many who carry the thyrsus but the Bacchants are few" (69c–d). The real initiates, he says, are the true philosophers.

The Neoplatonists made this passage the key to the dialogue. In their school curriculum the *Phaedo* was read third, after the *Alcibiades* and the *Gorgias*, as the dialogue about the "**purificatory**" virtues (Westerink, introduction). Proclus found no fewer than six grades of virtue in this one page: spurious, natural, moral, civic, purificatory and contemplative. Westerink calls this a distortion "prompted by the necessity to adapt the text of Plato to the complicated structures of late Neoplatonism".

### 4. The First Argument: Opposites and the Cycle (70c–72e)

Cebes states the fear plainly. Most people think the soul at death "is dispersed like breath or smoke" (70a). The first argument appeals to "an ancient theory" that the living come from the dead.
- Everything that has an opposite comes to be from its opposite: the larger from the smaller, the worse from the better.
- Between each pair there are two processes, like going to sleep and waking up.
- Living and being dead are opposites. Dying is one process, so there must be the reverse, "coming to life again". Otherwise "nature" would be "lame".
- If the cycle ran only one way, everything would end up dead, like Endymion asleep forever, or Anaxagoras' original mixture (72b–c).

Arieti notes that the argument starts with "all things which come to be" and quietly narrows to things that have an opposite, "surely a great reduction from many things to just a few". It also confuses relative terms (larger, smaller) with states like being alive. Cebes does not refute it. He simply moves on.

### 5. The Second Argument: Recollection (72e–77d)

Cebes recalls the theory, familiar from the *Meno*, "that for us learning is no other than recollection" (72e). Socrates develops it with **the Equal itself**.
- Seeing a lyre makes a lover think of the boy who plays it. Being reminded of one thing by another is recollection (73d).
- Equal sticks and stones "sometimes ... appear to one to be equal and to another to be unequal", but the Equal itself never appears unequal (74b–c).
- Sensible things "strive to be like the Equal but are deficient" (74e–75a). To judge them deficient we must already know the standard.
- We have used our senses since birth, so we must have known the Equal before birth, and the same goes for "the Beautiful itself, the Good itself, the Just, the Pious" (75c–d).

Socrates is careful to say how much hangs on the Forms: "If these realities do not exist, then this argument is altogether futile" (76e). Simmias then points out that at most the soul's existence *before* birth has been shown, and "half of what needed proof has been proved" (77c). Socrates says the two arguments together finish the job, but admits that the young men have "this childish fear" that the wind will blow the soul away. Cebes answers that there is "a child in us", and Socrates tells them to "sing a charm over him every day" (77e).

Arieti points out a running joke. Nobody in the dialogue can remember the theory of recollection. Simmias asks to have it repeated (73a), and later fears that "by this time tomorrow there will be no one left" who can give an account of it (76b).

### 6. The Third Argument: Affinity (78b–84b)

The third argument asks what kind of thing is liable to be scattered.
- Composite things can be broken up; incomposite things cannot.
- What is always the same, like the Forms, is likely incomposite. What changes, like beautiful horses and clothes, is composite.
- There are two kinds of being, the visible and changing and the invisible and unchanging.
- The soul is invisible and, when it thinks by itself, "passes into the realm of what is pure, ever existing, immortal and unchanging" (79d). The soul also rules the body, as the divine rules the mortal (80a).
- So the soul is "most like the divine, deathless, intelligible, uniform, indissoluble", and the body most like the mortal (80b).

Socrates adds a picture of what happens to impure souls. They are "heavy, ponderous, earthy", hang around graves as ghosts, and are reborn according to their habits (81c–82b):
- Gluttons and drunkards become donkeys.
- Lovers of tyranny and plunder become wolves, hawks and kites.
- People of "popular and social virtue ... developed by habit and practice, without philosophy" become bees, wasps or ants, or decent people again.

Only the lover of learning joins the gods. Philosophy, he says, finds the soul in the body "as through a cage", and sees that "the prisoner himself is contributing to his own incarceration" through its desires (82e–83a). The most memorable image comes here: "every pleasure or pain provides, as it were, another nail to rivet the soul to the body" (83d).

Arieti spots the problem. If a pleasure can nail the soul to the body and make it "corporeal", the soul is not as aloof from the body as the argument needs. Then why could the body's death not affect it too? The argument also gives only likeness: the soul is "most like" the indissoluble, "or nearly so" (80b).

### 7. The Objections: Simmias' Harmony and Cebes' Weaver (84c–88b)

After "a long silence", Socrates sees Simmias and Cebes whispering and makes them speak. They were afraid to upset him "in your present misfortune". Socrates laughs and compares himself to the **swans**, who "sing most and most beautifully" before death because they are servants of Apollo and know the blessings to come (84e–85b).

Simmias then states his view of human knowledge, one of the most famous sentences in Plato:

> One should achieve one of these things: learn the truth about these things or find it for oneself, or, if that is impossible, adopt the best and most irrefutable of men's theories, and, borne upon this, sail through the dangers of life as upon a raft, unless someone should make that journey safer and less risky upon a firmer vessel of some divine doctrine. (85c–d)

His objection is that the soul might be like the **attunement** (*harmonia*) of a lyre: invisible, beautiful and divine, yet destroyed when the strings break. If the soul is the right blend of hot, cold, wet and dry in the body, it would be the first thing to perish (85e–86d).

Cebes grants that the soul is stronger than the body but compares it to a **weaver** who wears out many cloaks. The man outlasts each cloak but still dies before the last one. The soul might wear out many bodies and still perish in the end. Unless the soul is proved altogether immortal, a dying man must fear for it (87b–88b).

Phaedo stops the story to say how depressed everyone was. Echecrates admits that the harmony theory "has a remarkable hold on me, now and always" (88d). The narrative breaks off at exactly the middle of the dialogue.

### 8. The Centre: Phaedo's Hair and the Danger of Misology (88c–91c)

What Phaedo admired most was not that Socrates had an answer, but "the pleasant, kind and admiring way he received the young men's argument" and how he "healed our distress" (88e–89a). Socrates strokes Phaedo's long hair. Tomorrow, he says, you will cut this in mourning, "Not if you take my advice ... It is today ... that I shall cut my hair and you yours, if our argument dies on us". Phaedo says not even Heracles could fight two at once. Socrates offers to be his Iolaus, and Phaedo insists it is the other way round (89b–c).

Then comes the warning that Burger, Arieti and Lampert all treat as the dialogue's centre:

> That we should not become misologues, as people become misanthropes. There is no greater evil one can suffer than to hate reasonable discourse. (89d)

Misanthropy comes from trusting people without skill, being let down again and again, and concluding that no one is good. In fact most people are in the middle, as most men are neither very tall nor very short. Misology works the same way. Someone who trusts argument after argument and finds each one failing ends by blaming argument itself, and thinks that "all that exists simply fluctuates up and down as if it were in the Euripus" (90c). The right response is to believe "that it is we who are not yet sound" (90e).

Socrates admits he is himself in danger of arguing only to win, and gives the rule for the rest of the day:

> If you will take my advice, you will give but little thought to Socrates but much more to the truth. If you think that what I say is true, agree with me; if not, oppose it with every argument and take care that in my eagerness I do not deceive myself and you and, like a bee, leave my sting in you when I go. (91c)

For Burger, this is where the dialogue turns. The "most threatening danger facing them" is "not the fear of death, but the loss of trust in argumentation altogether" (introduction). Arieti calls the misology passage "more important than the arguments on immortality", because it teaches philosophical courage, which the arguments only display.

### 9. Answering Simmias (91e–95a)

Socrates gives three replies to the harmony theory:
1. **Consistency.** A harmony cannot exist before the lyre and strings that make it. But Simmias accepted recollection, which says the soul existed before the body. He must choose, and he keeps recollection, since the harmony theory was adopted "without proof" for its "plausibility" (92a–e).
2. **Degrees.** Harmonies can be more or less tuned, but one soul is not more a soul than another. If virtue is harmony and vice disharmony, then a soul that is itself a harmony could never be bad, and "all the souls of all living creatures will be equally good" (93a–94a).
3. **Rule.** A harmony follows its strings, but the soul opposes the body: it refuses drink when the body is thirsty. Socrates quotes Odysseus: "he struck his breast and rebuked his heart saying, 'Endure, my heart, you have endured worse than this'" (*Odyssey* 20.17–18). Homer clearly thought the soul rules the passions (94b–95a).

He ends with a pun. Harmonia was the wife of Cadmus, founder of Thebes, and now the other Theban, Cebes, must be dealt with (95a).

### 10. Socrates' Autobiography and the "Second Sailing" (95e–102a)

To answer Cebes, Socrates says, "requires a thorough investigation of the cause of generation and destruction" (95e–96a). He tells the story of his own intellectual life.
- **Natural science.** As a young man he was "wonderfully keen" on natural science, asking whether we think with blood or air or fire. It made him "quite blind" even to what he thought he knew. He no longer understood why one added to one makes two, or why a man is taller "by a head" (96a–97b).
- **Anaxagoras.** He heard that Anaxagoras said Mind is the cause of everything, and hoped to learn why each thing is arranged as it is best for it to be. He was disappointed to find Anaxagoras explaining things by "air and ether and water". That is like saying Socrates is sitting in prison because of his bones and sinews (97c–98d). The true cause is that "the Athenians decided it was better to condemn me" and "it seemed best to me to sit here":

> For by the dog, I think these sinews and bones could long ago have been in Megara or among the Boeotians, taken there by my belief as to the best course, if I had not thought it more right and honorable to endure whatever penalty the city ordered rather than escape and run away. (98e–99a)

  The error is "not being able to distinguish the real cause from that without which the cause would not be able to act as a cause" (99b).
- **The second sailing.** Since he could not find a teacher of that kind of cause, he took up a "second best" way (99c–d). It was like watching an eclipse in a reflection rather than staring at the sun. He would "take refuge in discussions (*logoi*) and investigate the truth of things by means of words" (99e). His method was to lay down the strongest hypothesis and test what follows from it.
- **The "safe" answer.** He posits "a Beautiful, itself by itself, of a Good and a Great and all the rest". Then the "safe" answer is that "all beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful", not by their colour or shape, and big things are big by Bigness (100b–e).

Gallop explains that the phrase "second voyage" was a sailor's term for "taking to the oars when the wind has failed". The Forms hypothesis is second-best compared with a full account of why things are as it is best for them to be (Gallop, note on 99c9–d1). Burger reads the second sailing as a "*technē* of *logoi*", an art of argument, and a turn from studying things directly to studying them through speech. This is also the cure for misology.

### 11. The Final Argument: Forms and the Deathless (102a–107b)

The last and most elaborate argument builds on the Forms.
- Opposites themselves never admit their opposites. Tallness in Simmias will never become short. When shortness approaches it "either flees and retreats ... or it is destroyed" (102d–e).
- An unnamed listener objects that earlier they agreed opposites come from opposites. Socrates explains that then they meant opposite *things*, but now they mean the opposites *themselves* (103a–c).
- Some things that are not opposites still always carry an opposite with them. Snow always brings cold, fire always brings heat, and three always brings oddness. So snow will not admit heat, and three will not admit the even (103c–105b).
- This gives a "more sophisticated" safe answer. What makes a body hot is fire, what makes it sick is fever, and what makes a number odd is oneness (105b–c).
- **What makes a body alive is soul.** The soul always brings life, and the opposite of life is death. So the soul will not admit death; it is "deathless" (*athanatos*) (105c–e).
- But can the deathless be destroyed? Snow retreats from heat but also melts. Socrates answers that "the god, and the Form of life itself, and anything that is deathless, are never destroyed" (106d). So at death "the mortal part of him dies ... but his deathless part goes away safe and indestructible" (106e).

Cebes is satisfied. Simmias still has "private misgivings" because of "the importance of our subject and my low opinion of human weakness". Socrates agrees: "our first hypotheses require clearer examination" (107b).

Modern verdicts on this argument are harsh. Arieti collects them: Hackforth said "the argument has petered out into futility", Crombie called it "a nest of confusions", and Skemp "a blatant petitio principii" (begging the question). The key slide is that "deathless" first means "not dead" and then means "indestructible". Arieti's own objection is simpler: snow destroyed by fire "does not ... get up on its legs and run away". Gallop is kinder. He suggests that the appeal to "the god" points to an argument for a necessary, eternal being, so that the medieval proofs of God's existence "can be seen as 'footnotes' to Plato's text" (note on 106d). Lampert reads the whole series the other way: "Socrates' arguments on his last day, all invalid, point to the soul being mortal" (p. 86).

### 12. The Myth of the True Earth (107c–115a)

Socrates turns from argument to practice: "if the soul is immortal, it requires our care not only for the time we call our life, but for the sake of all time". "The soul goes to the underworld possessing nothing but its education and upbringing" (107c–d). Then he tells a myth.
- **Hollows.** The earth is a sphere held in the middle of the heavens by its own balance. We live in one of its hollows, "like ants or frogs around a swamp", and mistake the murky air for the sky. We are like a creature living at the bottom of the sea who thinks the water is the heavens (109a–d).
- **The true earth.** Above us is the true surface. Seen from above it looks like a ball stitched from twelve pieces of leather in brilliant colours. Its stones are pure gems, and its people talk with the gods face to face (110b–111c).
- **The underworld.** Below are rivers of water, fire and mud, flowing in and out of Tartarus: Oceanus, Acheron, Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus with the Styx (111c–113c).
- **Judgement.** The dead are judged (113d–114c):
  - The average go to the Acherusian lake to be purified and rewarded.
  - The incurably wicked are hurled into Tartarus forever.
  - Those guilty of curable crimes, like striking a parent in anger, are thrown out after a year. They must beg their victims for forgiveness before they can leave.
  - The very pious live on the true surface. "Those who have purified themselves sufficiently by philosophy live in the future altogether without a body."

Socrates then gives the myth's status in words that matter for reading all of Plato's myths:

> No sensible man would insist that these things are as I have described them, but I think it is fitting for a man to risk the belief—for the risk is a noble one—that this, or something like this, is true about our souls and their dwelling places, since the soul is evidently immortal, and a man should repeat this to himself as if it were an incantation. (114d)

The word "incantation" picks up the "charm" for the frightened child in section 5. Arieti reads the shift to myth as "an admission that scientific knowledge is impossible" in these matters. Its job is to move the soul toward virtue "by a means which bypasses discursive reasoning". Burger notes that the dialogue's second half separates pure argument from pure myth, where the first half had mixed them.

### 13. The Death of Socrates (115a–118a)

The last pages are among the most famous in Greek literature.
- **The bath.** Socrates bathes so that the women will not have to wash a corpse (115a).
- **Instructions.** Asked for instructions, he says only: "take good care of your own selves" (115b).
- **The burial.** Asked how he wants to be buried: "In any way you like ... if you can catch me and I do not escape you." Crito still thinks Socrates is "the thing which he will soon be looking at as a corpse". Socrates adds: "to express oneself badly is not only faulty as far as the language goes, but does some harm to the soul" (115c–e).
- **The jailer.** The officer of the Eleven, who brings the order, weeps and calls him "the noblest, the gentlest and the best man who has ever come here" (116c).
- **No delay.** Crito points out that the sun is still on the hills. Socrates refuses to delay, so as not "to become ridiculous in my own eyes for clinging to life" (117a).
- **The cup.** He asks whether a libation may be poured from the cup, prays for a fortunate journey, and drinks "calmly and easily". Phaedo: "I was weeping for myself, not for him." When Apollodorus breaks down, Socrates rebukes them all: "one should die in good omened silence" (117c–e).
- **The last words.** The poison numbs him from the feet up. Before the cold reaches his heart he uncovers his face and says:

> Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius; make this offering to him and do not forget. (118a)

Phaedo's epitaph closes the dialogue: "of all those we have known the best, and also the wisest and the most upright" (118a).

### 14. What Did the Last Words Mean?

A cock was the usual offering to **Asclepius**, the god of healing, after a cure. Readers have offered at least four explanations:

| Reading | Who | What the cure is |
|---|---|---|
| Release from the disease of life | Nietzsche, *Gay Science* 340: "O Crito, life is a disease!" | Death heals Socrates of living |
| A literal debt | Gallop | Some real vow, "incurred in some connection unknown" |
| The healing of the friends and of Plato | Burger, Lampert | Socrates "healed us" of misology (89a); Plato, who was "ill", recovers through the dialogue |
| Gratitude for life itself | Lampert, after Nietzsche's *Beyond Good and Evil* 49 | Greek religion's "unrestrained fullness of gratitude" for life as it is |

**Gallop** rejects the first reading. The idea that life is a disease was Cebes' (95d), and it is "hardly compatible" with Socrates' own warning against misology. **Burger** points out that Asclepius was the son of Apollo, the god of purification, so the healing god completes the hymn to Apollo with which the day began. She also notes that Pindar told of Asclepius being bribed to save a dying man, so the offering might even be "one last affirmation of the goodness of life".

**Lampert** builds the most elaborate case.
- Socrates' first and last words are both addressed to Crito, the old friend whose loyalty refutes the charge in Aristophanes' *Clouds* that Socrates corrupted the young.
- "We owe" is plural, so the debt is shared by "a community of the gratefully indebted". It runs from Crito, through Phaedo, who will retell the tale, to the unnamed objector who understood the last argument's weakness. Lampert guesses that objector is Plato.
- "Plato pays his debt ... ensuring as far as possible that the death of Socrates will not be the death of philosophy" (p. 85).

For the full story of Nietzsche's reading, see [[Nietzsche and Socrates: The Problem of Socrates]].

### 15. Why Are the Arguments So Weak?

Readers have answered this in four main ways.
- **The Neoplatonists took them as proofs.** Iamblichus "insisted on finding five absolute proofs of immortality". Even later Neoplatonists saw this was untenable: Damascius said "the great Iamblichus" had overshot the mark (Westerink, introduction).
- **The dialogue is about a death, not a doctrine.** Arieti argues that it "is not, of course, about the immortality of the soul; it is about the death of Socrates". The arguments must fail, because "only the philosopher knows that he cannot know about the afterlife ... and he is thus the only one who can die courageously". Courage in the *Laches* sense is facing an outcome one cannot know. Socrates is "most heroic when his arguments are weakest", inviting objections that could shake his own calm.
- **The arguments test logos itself.** Burger thinks the real "practice of dying" is the separation of argument "from just that attachment to the self that the hope for immortality betrays". The final argument quietly redefines the soul as a principle of life, which contradicts the first half's picture of the soul as pure mind.
- **They are exoteric.** Lampert holds that Socrates' public words are "Odyssean": "with an exoteric ring of edifying piety, they convey the esoteric and deadly content of philosophy". The friends are given a belief they can live by, while the sharpest listeners see the soul is mortal.

Gadamer, quoted by Arieti, sums up what most readers feel: "the Phaedo's poetic power to convince is stronger than its logical power to prove". Arieti also quotes Nietzsche's observation that the dying Socrates became the new ideal for Greek youth "instead of to that older heroic ideal, Achilles". Arieti ends with a contrast from the *Crito*. Socrates dreamed he would reach "Phthia" on the third day, quoting Achilles' threat to sail home and abandon the Greeks. Achilles left his comrades to die. Socrates stayed, and "When Socrates left his prison house of Athens, the city's soul was departing".

### Conclusion

The *Phaedo* argues that philosophy is a training for death, because thinking at its best already separates the soul from the body. It gives four arguments for immortality:
- the cycle of opposites;
- recollection of the Forms;
- the soul's likeness to the divine;
- the soul as bearer of life that cannot admit death.

Each is open to objection, and the dialogue lets Simmias and Cebes make the objections themselves. At its centre it puts the warning against misology and the "second sailing" into arguments, and it ends with a myth that Socrates explicitly calls a noble risk and an incantation. What persuades is Socrates himself: laughing, stroking Phaedo's hair, refusing to delay the cup, and remembering a debt to the god of healing. The Neoplatonists read the dialogue as a set of proofs. Modern readers mostly read it as a portrait of how a philosopher dies when the proofs run out.
