St Gregory Palamas (1296–1359) holds a place in Orthodox theology like that of Aquinas in Catholic theology. He is also usually cast as Aquinas's opposite: the mystic of experience against the logician of concepts. His central teaching is that God has an unknowable **essence** and knowable, uncreated **energies**, and that the saints are deified by sharing in the energies. Its own defenders have often called that teaching an *antinomy*, a pair of contrary but equally true claims that reason cannot reconcile. This note sets out two analytic philosophers who disagree with that picture, in one volume, *Logic in Orthodox Christian Thinking* (2013). **Richard Swinburne** looks at Palamas's theory of knowledge: how he thought we come to know God, and whether those ways are reasonable. **Paweł Rojek** looks at his metaphysics: whether the essence–energies distinction and the doctrine of deification can be stated in formal logic without contradiction. I give them equal weight. They work on different questions but reach the same conclusion: Palamas is a philosopher, and a defensible one.

### 1. The Controversy

Rojek supplies the history (pp. 41–5). The **hesychasts** were Athonite monks who prayed in stillness, with the Jesus Prayer and a bodily technique, and some reported a vision of divine light. **Barlaam of Calabria** attacked them on two counts: the body had no part in prayer, and since God is "essentially unknowable and inexperienceable", the light they saw could not be divine (p. 41). Palamas answered him in the *Triads*, and he answered Akindynos and Nikephoros Gregoras in later treatises. Councils at Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 upheld Palamas, and in 1352 his teaching entered the *Synodikon* read on the Sunday of Orthodoxy. In David Bradshaw's words, "hesychasm and its Palamite interpretation became the official theology of the Orthodox world" (quoted p. 42).

The usual reading treats this as East against West: mysticism against rationalism, Palamas against an "eastern Thomist" Barlaam. Losev went so far as to say that in condemning Barlaam the Church "has condemned the whole Renaissance" (p. 42). Rojek accepts the newer historians' correction. Barlaam was no Thomist, and if any Western writer influenced him it was Augustine. The dispute was internal to the Byzantine tradition, as even Lossky later conceded (p. 43). Rojek also argues that Palamas was the better-trained philosopher of the two. When Palamas was a student, his teacher said before the emperor that "If Aristotle himself had been here in flesh and blood, he would have praised him". The quarrel was "a struggle between two different philosophies, and not simply between theology and philosophy". Indeed "it was Barlaam who really denied human cognitive power, not Palamas" (pp. 43–4).

The teaching was then largely forgotten until the 1910s. It was revived through another Athonite quarrel, over the **imiaslavie** ("name-glorifiers", also called onomatodoxy), who held that the names of God are divine energies and therefore God himself. Pavel Florensky saw the parallel with Palamas. Krivoshein, Lossky and Meyendorff then made Palamas the centre of modern Orthodox theology. Rojek notes the irony that Lossky and Meyendorff were students of Étienne Gilson, the Thomist (pp. 44–5).

### 2. Swinburne: Three Roads to Knowing God

Swinburne divides the question into the three ways Christians have traditionally claimed to know God: **natural reason**, **public revelation** (Scripture) and **direct awareness** of God (pp. 18–19). Palamas wrote almost entirely for believers and said little about the first two. For that reason he is often thought to have rejected them, and Swinburne says that view "is mistaken" (p. 19).

On natural reason, Swinburne cites the *Triads*: "knowledge of creation brought mankind to knowledge of God before the Law and the prophets; today also it is bringing men back" (*Triads* 2.3.44; p. 19). Palamas was hostile to Greek philosophy only because he thought it led to polytheism (*Triads* 1.1.18; p. 19 n. 2). The real disagreement with Barlaam was about the form of proof. Barlaam argued from Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics*: a demonstration needs premises "true, primary, immediate, better known than, prior to and causative of their conclusion" (71b20–25). No premises drawn from the created world could meet that standard for a conclusion about God. Swinburne thinks these conditions are "excessively demanding", though he agrees that a premise must at least be better known than the conclusion (p. 20). Palamas agreed with Barlaam this far: "common notions", he wrote, "depend on the intelligence of him who was last created", that is, on mere human intelligence (p. 20).

### 3. Swinburne: The Argument Is Inductive

Swinburne's main claim is that the Greek Fathers never meant their natural theology as syllogisms. "It was not however characteristic of the patristic tradition to put natural theology into the form of a syllogism" (p. 21). They pointed instead to the order of the world. Athanasius (*Against the Heathens* 35–44) argues that the four elements, contrary by nature, could not form a world fit for human beings unless "One that has made and ordered it" held them together (pp. 21–2). Palamas gives a compressed version in *Triads* 2.3.44 (p. 22). In his first letter to Akindynos he calls the ascent from good things to goodness itself "a demonstration free from deceit" that there is "one who is in all things and who is removed from and transcends all things" (p. 23). Robert Sinkewicz, the scholar of Barlaam, sees here "a notion of demonstration quite distinct from that advocated by Barlaam and ultimately by Aristotle" (p. 23).

Swinburne names that notion. These are arguments "from effect to cause. Such arguments are not deductive, but (in a wide sense) inductive" (p. 23). They are the kind scientists and detectives use, and "Neither Aristotle nor the medievals, East or West, had the slightest conception of the nature of inductive inference" (p. 23). His criteria are that an argument from evidence *E* to an explanation *H* is cogent insofar as (p. 24):

1. if *H* is true, *E* is probable;
2. if *H* is false, *E* is improbable;
3. *H* is simple.

His example is a detective. The fingerprints, the witnesses and the hidden money are best explained by one man robbing a safe. A defence theory with three separate people acting without collusion would fit the clues just as well, but it is less simple (p. 24). Applied to God, the evidence is that the universe obeys laws, that those laws produce human bodies, and that humans are conscious. God is "the simplest kind of person there could be", a person with no limits on power, knowledge or freedom. Such a God would have reason to create beings with the distinctive good of choosing between good and evil, so the evidence is what a God would probably produce (pp. 25–6). The Fathers' physics of four elements is "totally erroneous", but "My basic point is the same as that of the Fathers" (p. 26).

### 4. Swinburne: Essence and Energies Against Aquinas

The standard objection is that God is incomprehensible, so we cannot know what he is like. On a strict apophatic reading (Dionysius, and Barlaam), every predicate either denies something of God or names something he causes. Swinburne rejects this, using Aquinas's argument (*ST* Ia.13.2). If "God is good" only means that God causes goodness, why not say "God is a body", since he causes bodies? If all we knew was that God causes a world that is not bad, "He might be a powerful spider, or a being indifferent to human well-being, or some theorem of mathematics" (p. 27).

Aquinas's own answer has a problem, Swinburne says. Aquinas held that God is simple and also that God is omnipotent, omniscient and good. He reconciled the two "in a cavalier and superficially incomprehensible way, by asserting that really all these properties are the same property as each other, and the same as God himself!" (p. 28). Palamas, Swinburne argues, held the same three things: "God is simple, we can know quite a bit about him, but we cannot know his deepest nature". He stated them without the paradox because he and Aquinas meant different things by "essence". For Aquinas the essence is whatever properties a thing of its kind must have, so omnipotence belongs to God's essence. For Palamas "the essence of a thing is its deepest nature, whatever underlies its other necessary properties". On that definition God's power and goodness are not his essence but his **energies**, which are "inseparable from God". Following Basil ("The energies are various, and the essence simple"), Palamas could say the energies are many without making God composite (p. 28). On this reading the essence–energies distinction is a clearer statement of what Aquinas was trying to say. This bears directly on [[Divine Simplicity: Western and Orthodox Views]].

### 5. Swinburne: Revelation and Direct Awareness

Swinburne gives revelation a short section. Palamas held that there are sound deductive arguments from Scripture and the Fathers. But he knew from the *filioque* dispute that opposite doctrines can be drawn from verses taken in isolation. And unlike Justin, Tertullian and Irenaeus, he did little to supply public evidence that Scripture is revealed (p. 29). Swinburne offers Duns Scotus's ten reasons for the credibility of Scripture (fulfilled prophecy, miracles, the Church's stability and others) as "a cogent inductive argument" of the kind Palamas did not give (p. 30). Palamas used two of them in his debates with the Turks during his captivity. Muhammad, he said, was not foretold in the Old Testament and worked no miracles (p. 31).

Palamas's real interest was **direct awareness** of God's energies, though not of his essence. Swinburne records his hesitation over who has it. Sometimes it is "common to all who have believed in Christ". Sometimes it belongs to those who, "becoming gods, in God they know God" (*Triads* II.iii.68). In its fullness it seems open only to monks. Palamas said he himself had only smelled the honey from a distance and not yet held it (pp. 31–2 and n. 17). The light of Tabor "shines even now in the hearts of the faithful and perfect". Compared with it, Scripture is "a lamp that shines in an obscure place" (*Triads* II.iii.18). The vision is "superior to all knowledge", and is called knowledge only "in an improper and equivocal sense" (II.iii.17; p. 32).

Swinburne makes sense of this with the difference between **knowing that** and **knowing a person**. "I do not think we can know a person without knowing something about that person." Doctrine tells us what God is like, so that we can tell an experience of God from, say, a feeling of oneness with nature or a demon (pp. 32–3). He then justifies the experience by his **Principle of Credulity**: "it is rational to believe that things are as they seem to us to be—in the absence of counter-evidence". If you seem to see an elephant in an English garden, believe it unless you have reason not to. If the experience is overwhelming, it can outweigh contrary testimony. So whoever has "overwhelming experiences apparently of God of the kind which Palamas describes" is rational to trust them, "whatever the doubts expressed by others" (pp. 33–4). Principles of Memory and Testimony extend this to recalled experiences and to the reports of others (p. 34).

His conclusion is that Palamas rightly saw little need for natural theology among fourteenth-century Greeks. "We however in twenty first century Europe are surrounded by people who need these things", and the need for direct awareness of God through prayer is as great now as then (pp. 34–5).

### 6. Rojek: The Antinomy Thesis

Rojek's target is a view held by Palamas's own champions. Krivoshein called the distinction "a theological antinomy" with an "objective character". For Lossky it was "an antinomic theology which proceeds by oppositions of contrary but equally true propositions". The Catholic Spiteris said Palamas "uses concepts, which are contradictory, though consistent in God" (all p. 39). Rojek replies: "I would like to challenge this popular view", and "there is nothing especially inconsistent in Palamism" (p. 39). Palamas, he says, was asking a philosopher's question: "What should the world be like since revelation and deification are possible?" (p. 45). Scripture gives both facts. "No one has ever seen God" (John 1:18), yet God spoke to Moses "face to face" (Ex. 33:11). And Christians are to "become partakers of the divine nature" (2 Pet. 1:4; pp. 45–6). The energies are Palamas's answer: God has one essence and many energies, which are "distinct, yet not separable from essence. Being such, they are really God himself, though are not God's essence" (pp. 46–7).

### 7. Rojek: What an Energy Is

Rojek first argues that the distinction is "quite intuitive. Things manifest themselves", and a thing is never exhausted by how it appears (p. 47). Florensky put it as a being's inner side, turned toward itself, and outer side, turned toward others (pp. 47–8). Bradshaw notes that Palamas's "energies" cover three older categories, realities (the uncreated light), attributes (life, infinity) and operations (creating, providence), and unite them as "acts of self-manifestation" (p. 48).

Rojek then places energies between Aristotle's **accident** and **property** (pp. 48–50). Palamas says an energy is "neither essence, nor accident". Energies resemble accidents because they act and cease to act, but they "cannot not exist". God has wisdom "not as a property, but only as energy". Rojek explains this with the distinction between *dynamis* (a power, necessary to the essence, like a property) and *energeia* (its exercise, which can begin and end, like an accident). Unlike a property, an energy can be shared with another kind of being, as divine wisdom is given to humans. His summary: energies are "more dynamic than properties and more static than accidents". They also suit a personal God because they include acts of will (p. 50). Against Krivoshein and Spiteris, who limit the distinction to God or call it metaphor, Rojek points out that Palamas applies it to everything: "only nonbeing is deprived of this power" (*Triads* III.2.7). His method was "downward", working out the concept for God and then applying it to creatures (pp. 50–51).

The logical key is **dependence**. The energies are not identical with the essence and not separable from it. They are "enhypostatic": they exist only in a subject. "Dependent entities are exactly distinct, yet not separable entities", and dependence is "the best known 'ontological glue'" (pp. 51–2). They are not effects like creatures. "A creature is an effect of divine energies not the energies themselves" (*Treatises* III.19). Nor are they parts, since "no being is composed of its own acts" (*Treatises* III.25; pp. 52–3). Rojek then notes that this matches the Western scholastic **formal distinction**: two things differ in definition but cannot be separated in reality. Palamas says the same: "We do not treat the unity of essence and energies as if they had the same meaning, but as something inseparable" (*Treatises* II.8). Rojek concludes, "It seems there really is no gap between the eastern and the western distinction" (p. 53). Because the energies are God, though not his essence, "God is entirely present in each of the divine energies", and the hesychasts who see the light see God himself (pp. 53–4).

### 8. Rojek: Correcting Florensky's Formula

In 1922 Florensky proposed formulas for the four possible views of how energy (*A*) relates to essence (*B*). He called them immanentism, extreme positivism, Kantianism and Platonism, and identified Palamism with the last, written "A ⊃ B : B ⊃ −A" (pp. 57–8). He claimed that "The logical core of theological disputes in the 14th and 19th centuries boils down to only this epistemological formula" (p. 58).

Rojek shows that the formula as written fails. Read in modern notation as (A ⊃ B) ∧ (B ⊃ ¬A), it gives A ⊃ ¬A by transitivity, which is true only if *A* is false. So "Palamism would be true if and only if there were no energies at all" (p. 59). The negation is in the wrong place. Corrected, Palamism says energy implies essence, but it is *not the case* that essence implies energy: (A ⊃ B) ∧ ¬(B ⊃ A). Bogdan Strachowski objected that the same correction makes Kantianism self-contradictory, since (A ⊃ B) ∨ (B ⊃ A) is a law of classical logic (p. 60). Rojek's answer is **modal logic**, which "was not invented at that time yet" (p. 61):

| View | Rojek's modal version | In words |
|---|---|---|
| Palamism | □(A ⊃ B) ∧ ¬□(B ⊃ A) | Energy necessarily requires essence; essence does not necessarily require energy |
| Kantianism | ◊(A ∧ ¬B) ∧ ◊(B ∧ ¬A) | Each could exist without the other; consistent, because ◊A ∧ ◊B does not entail ◊(A ∧ B) |

He then sees two remaining problems with his own fix (p. 62). First, simple dependence does not separate energies from **effects**, since the created world also could not exist without God. He borrows Roman Ingarden's distinction: effects are merely "non-self-sufficient", needing something else, while energies are *dependent*, belonging to their subject. Second, the essence is not wholly independent of its energies, because its powers (*dynamis*) belong to it necessarily. So the formula "holds only for acts, not for the potencies" (pp. 62–3).

He also gives a second, **relational** reading, in which *x* < *y* means "*x* is an energy of *y*" (pp. 63–4). The relation is asymmetric, irreflexive and transitive, so it is a strict order. An essence is something that is not an energy of anything, and an energy is something that has no energies of its own. If the order had a root, everything would be an energy of God, but Palamas denies that creatures are God's energies, so "in Palamitic universes there would be no root". A variant (≤, "is an energy of or is identical with") is a partial order. It has the same formal properties as relations in established systems: Leśniewski's Ontology, Perzanowski's "ontologics" and Moiseev's modal ontology (p. 64). The distinction can thus be stated in known logic without contradiction.

### 9. Rojek: Deification and Identity

Before Palamas, theology knew two kinds of union: **essential**, of the three Persons in the Trinity, and **hypostatic**, of the two natures in Christ. Palamas added a third. God deifies the worthy "not hypostatically—that belonged to Christ alone—nor essentially, but through [...] energies" (*Against Akindynos* V.26; p. 55). Following Dionysius, he divides deification into **assimilation** (likeness, achieved by ascetic effort) and **unification** (a gift). Effort is necessary but not sufficient: "the mysterious union itself is effected by grace" (*Triads* III.1.27; p. 55). God "communicates to us not His nature, but His proper glory and splendor" (*Triads* I.3.23). Maximus is quoted to the same effect: the saint is by grace "everything that God is by nature, save only the identity of nature" (p. 56). See [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].

Rojek draws here on Florensky's *The Pillar and Ground of the Truth*. Florensky treated love as a real union, *Finis amoris ut duo unum fiant* ("love is completed when two become one"). He accused modern logic of reducing identity to likeness: the usual definition of identity "replaces the question of real numerical identity with the question of the formal similarity of traits" (p. 66). Rojek finds the argument "perfectly sound". If properties are real universals, so that two things can share one and the same property, and a thing is more than a bundle of its properties, then the **identity of indiscernibles** fails. Two distinct things can have every property in common (p. 67). That allows two kinds of sameness:

| Kind | What is shared | Rojek's label |
|---|---|---|
| Realistic ("homoousian") | Numerically the same properties | Identity |
| Nominalistic ("homoiousian") | Exactly similar properties | Resemblance |

Florensky's version risked **pantheism**, since he spoke as if humans could share God's *nature*. Rojek's correction uses Palamas's distinction. Sharing one nature is consubstantiality, found only in the Trinity. Sharing the same energies is **synergy**, open to the saints. "Without the Palamitic distinction it is uneasy to avoid the charge of pantheism" (pp. 68–9). Assimilation is then shared likeness of energies, and unification is shared identity of energies. Since identity entails likeness but not the reverse, "assimilation really is only a necessary but not sufficient condition of deification" (p. 70).

The disputed question is which kind of synergy deification is. Rojek's thesis is "that deification consist of strict identity of energies, and not mere resemblance" (p. 70). He compares three patristic cases (p. 70):

| Case | Essences | Energies |
|---|---|---|
| The Trinity | one | one, "not by analogy ... but truly also one in number" |
| Christ | two | two, with "no division, no change, no partition, no confusion" |
| Ordinary humans | many | "similar, but not identical" |

Meyendorff modelled synergy on Christ's two energies, with the human energy "obedient" to the divine (p. 71). Rojek objects that this leaves God and man with two distinct, merely similar energies. Deification would then be "a homoiousian imitation, not homoousian union", the external imitation Palamas rejected. In Christ the two energies could stay two because one hypostasis already united the natures. In deification there is no shared hypostasis, so only identical energies give a real union (p. 72). Florensky's language of energies that "fuse" and "knit" points the same way, and Lourié goes further: the deified man has "no human energies, only divine" (pp. 71–2). Palamas's own analogy supports Rojek. God is joined to the saints "as a soul is to its body", and soul and body have activities in common, not merely similar ones (pp. 72–3).

One question remains: does the deified person share *all* God's energies? Palamas says "The entire Divinity comes to dwell in fullness in those deemed worthy". He also says God deifies "through a small part of the uncreated energies" (pp. 73–4). Rojek reconciles the two through the claim that God is "entirely present in each" energy, so a small part brings the whole God. Deification is one-sided: every energy of the deified person is divine, but "God can have some energies which are not shared by man", as the soul has acts the body does not share (p. 74).

### 10. Rojek: Why It Looked Like Antinomy

Rojek's conclusion is that "No true antinomy was found", and that formal logic even exposes "some problems usually neglected by commentators" (pp. 74–5). The appearance of mystery came from Palamas's method. Ordinarily theology borrows its concepts from philosophy. Palamas reversed this and practised what Rojek calls **theological philosophy**: "philosophy should accept concepts and axioms from theology". He changed the categories of accident and property to fit revelation, and from the old point of view the result looked contradictory. Once the distinction is accepted as an axiom, it "would be no more explanandum but rather explanans" (p. 75), something that explains rather than something needing explanation. He also takes a position on ecumenism. Spiteris thought dialogue required stripping Palamism of "all conceptual content". Rojek holds "the opposite view": a logical reconstruction shows foundations "not so alien to western philosophy", and so helps undo the stereotypes of "the intellectual West" and "the mystical East" (pp. 75–6). Not every contributor to the volume agrees. The next chapter, by Michael Craig Rhodes, argues that Orthodox reasoning is paraconsistent, tolerating contradiction without collapsing into absurdity (p. 82), which is close to the antinomy view Rojek rejects.

### 11. Comparing the Two

The two readings support each other at several points:

- **Against the "mystical East" stereotype.** Swinburne shows that Palamas did not reject natural theology. Rojek shows that his metaphysics is consistent, and that Palamas, not Barlaam, had the higher view of human reason.
- **Toward Aquinas.** Both narrow the gap. For Swinburne the energies say what Aquinas meant, without the paradox of identical attributes. For Rojek the distinction is formally the same as the scholastic formal distinction.
- **On essence.** Swinburne's point that Palamas meant by "essence" a thing's deepest nature, not its necessary properties, explains why Rojek can treat power and wisdom as energies, inseparable from God but not identical with his essence.

They differ in method and in emphasis:

- **Method.** Swinburne works in epistemology and probability. Rojek works in modal logic and formal ontology.
- **Emphasis.** Swinburne admits he has not had the vision and justifies it from outside, by a general principle of rational belief. Rojek takes the experience of revelation and deification as given and asks what the world must be like for it to be real.
- **On reason.** Swinburne's Palamas reasons, unknowingly, by induction. Rojek's Palamas builds a new metaphysics, with theology supplying its axioms.

Read together, they argue that Palamism is neither anti-intellectual nor contradictory. Swinburne shows that its claims about how we know God can be defended. Rojek shows that its claims about what God and deified human beings are can be stated without contradiction.

### Conclusion

The essence–energies distinction has usually been defended as a sacred antinomy and attacked as an incoherence. Both philosophers reject both descriptions. Swinburne argues that Palamas's "demonstration free from deceit" is a sound inductive argument from the order of the world. His distinction then lets one say what Aquinas meant by simplicity without paradox, and the hesychast's vision is justified by the ordinary principle that we may trust how things seem. Rojek takes the doctrine itself apart with modal and relational logic. Energies are dependent, inseparable acts, neither accidents, nor parts, nor effects. Deification is the sharing of numerically identical energies, which keeps it a real union without pantheism. Florensky's attempt a century earlier failed on a misplaced negation, and once that is corrected no contradiction remains. Their shared conclusion is that Palamas's theology begins from experience but is not irrational.
