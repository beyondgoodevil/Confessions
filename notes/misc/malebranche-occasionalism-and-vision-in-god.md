> "For in him we live, and move, and have our being." (Acts 17:28)

> "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work." (John 5:17)

Malebranche was a priest of the Oratory who read Descartes' *Treatise on Man* in 1664 and, his biographer says, had to stop because his heart was pounding. He spent the rest of his life fusing Descartes with Augustine. Two doctrines came out of it. In knowledge, **we see all things in God**. In causation, **God alone acts**, and creatures are only the "occasions" of His acting. Both rest on one conviction: the creature is so wholly dependent that to give it any power or light of its own is a kind of paganism. Hume told a friend to read the *Search after Truth* before his own *Treatise*, and his famous argument about causation is largely Malebranche with God removed.

## 1. Seeing All Things in God (*Search* III.2)

**The problem** (ch. 1)
- We don't perceive the sun "by itself"; the soul doesn't leave the body to wander among the stars. What the mind immediately perceives is an **idea**. So where do ideas of bodies come from?
- He lists five possibilities:
  - (a) bodies send out likenesses of themselves;
  - (b) the soul makes its ideas;
  - (c) God created them with us, or makes each one as needed;
  - (d) the soul sees everything by looking at its own perfections;
  - (e) the soul is united to a perfect being that contains the ideas of all things.

**Against the first four** (chs. 2–5)
- **Species** (the Aristotelian likenesses, "impressed" and "expressed" species): these would be little bodies crowding and colliding in the air. They would have to swell five hundred times when you pick up a microscope. A cube's faces reach the eye as unequal shapes, yet we see equal squares. So the object's "species" isn't what we see.
- **The soul makes its ideas**: an idea is a real spiritual being, so producing one would be **creation**. Making an angel out of a stone is harder than making it from nothing. And you can't paint an animal you've never seen: to make the right idea you would need the idea already. He also says the view flatters pride, imagining that man "shares in" God's creative power. The source of the error is the habit of calling whatever regularly comes first the "cause". His examples: a comet appears and a prince dies; I will, and an idea appears; a ball strikes, and another moves. This is the seed of his whole theory of causation.
- **Innate ideas**: for figures alone the mind would need "an infinity of infinite numbers of ideas". God acts in the simplest ways and would not stock each soul like that.
- **The soul sees things in itself**: only God sees all things by looking at Himself, because He made them. "Say not that you are a light unto yourself" (Augustine).

**The answer** (ch. 6)
- God must have in Himself the ideas of everything He made, or He could not have made it. And God is intimately present to our minds: He is "**the place of minds** as space is, in a sense, the place of bodies". So we see bodies by seeing what in God represents them, as and when He wills to show it.
- This is not seeing God's essence. We see God's ideas "only as relative to creatures".
- Reasons:
  - it is the **simplest** way;
  - it puts minds in **complete dependence** on God: "Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God" (2 Cor 3:5);
  - we can turn our mind to *any* thing, so all beings must already be confusedly present to us;
  - **the infinite comes first**: we think "being" before we think "finite being", so every particular idea is a "participation in the general idea of the infinite";
  - ideas act on the mind, and only what is above the mind can act on it.
- The parallel with will: we love particular goods only by bending toward them the impulse to love the good in general that God gives us. So "if we do not to some extent see God, we see nothing, just as if we do not love God… we would love nothing".
- He corrects Augustine on one point. The **truths** (that 2 × 2 = 4) are relations, not beings, so they aren't God. But the **ideas** they relate are in God. We see the ideas in God; sensations, like pain or colour, are God's modifications of our soul, not ideas we see in Him.
- His conclusion: "God is the intelligible world or the place of minds, as the material world is the place of bodies." That is Acts 17:28, read as epistemology.

**Four ways of knowing** (ch. 7)
- **God** we know by Himself, directly: "Only God do we perceive by a direct and immediate perception."
- **Bodies** we know through their ideas, in God, and so quite perfectly: geometry and mechanics.
- **Our own soul** we know only by **consciousness**, "inner sensation", with no clear idea of it. So we know the soul's *existence* better than the body's, but its *nature* worse. He even suggests God hid the idea of the soul so that we would not regard it as too separate from the body.
- **Other minds** we know by **conjecture**. When the body is involved, "I am almost always mistaken in judging others by myself."

## 2. Occasionalism (*Search* VI.2.3)

The chapter title is "The most dangerous error of the philosophy of the ancients", and the danger is religious before it is philosophical.

**The charge of idolatry**
- To say bodies contain "forms, faculties, qualities, virtues… capable of producing certain effects through the force of their nature" is to put "something divine in all the bodies around us". For "the idea of a sovereign power is the idea of sovereign divinity, and the idea of a subordinate power is the idea of a lower divinity."
- We love and fear what can do us good or harm. So if bodies are real causes, it is reasonable to love and fear them, and that is how men came to worship the sun. Even the Israelites who longed for the leeks and onions of Egypt were, on this view, not wrong. "If the heart is Christian, the mind is basically pagan."
- This philosophy is "received… from the serpent", not from Adam.

**The arguments** (as Nadler orders them)
1. **No necessary connection.** "A true cause as I understand it is one such that the mind perceives a necessary connection between it and its effect." There is none between bodies (a ball need not move another), none between my will and my arm, and none between a twitch of bile and the passion of anger. The only connection we see is between the will of an omnipotent being and what it wills: "it is impossible to conceive that He wills a body to be moved and that this body not be moved."
2. **Bodies are passive.** A Cartesian body is only extension, so its properties are "relations of distance". Force is not a relation of distance. And if a body passed its force on to another, a mode would travel between substances, which is impossible.
3. **Knowledge.** People who know nothing of nerves and "animal spirits" move their arms better than anatomists. "There is no man who knows what must be done to move one of his fingers." To be the real cause of something you must know how to do it (Geulincx says this outright). So we don't move our arms; God does, when we will it.
4. **Continuous creation.** Conservation is creation continued: "if God ceased to conserve it, it would necessarily revert to nothing" (*Dialogues* VII). A body can't be created "nowhere", so God creates it at each moment *somewhere*. Creating it in successive places is moving it. So only the Creator can be the mover: "It is only the Creator of bodies who can be their mover" (*Dialogues* VII.13).
5. **God cannot share it.** If an angel's will were a true cause, God would have to make His will follow the angel's. Then the angel would also be a true creator, since God could equally create whenever the angel willed. "He cannot make them gods."

**What is left to creatures**
- Natural causes are "**occasional causes**" that "determine the Author of nature to act in such and such a manner in such and such a situation". God acts by a few **general laws**: bodies move in straight lines, and impact distributes motion. A collision is the occasion on which the law applies.
- This leaves science alone. "We would be ridiculous were we to say… that it is God who dries the roads." Physics still looks for mechanical "causes"; metaphysics adds that their force is God's will.
- **Minds**: we do will freely. Freedom is the one thing he insists on, otherwise there is no sin or merit. But our willing is only the "determining" toward a particular good of the impulse to the good in general that God gives us. "From himself man has only error and sin, which are nothing."

## 3. Elucidation 15: The Seven Proofs and His Replies

He collects the standard Scholastic arguments for real secondary causes and answers each.
1. *Then living and non-living things would be indistinguishable.* No: we tell them apart by what they visibly do. A dog's life is the same kind of thing as a watch's motion. (This is Cartesian animal-mechanism, which he defends.)
2. *Then natures would be unfixed, and fire might cool.* No: the laws are constant, so fire always burns. Its "nature" is just a consequence of God's general will.
3. *Then ploughing and watering would be pointless, since God needs no preparation.* God *could* act without them, but only by miracle. By His simple laws, water is what carries salts up a plant.
4. *Bodies collide and resist, and wolves eat sheep. Would God fight Himself?* This argument proves too much. If God "concurs" in every action, He fights Himself anyway. God *wills* collisions, because impact is the simplest occasion for His laws. Destruction is restored by the same law that caused it. And "when a house crushes an honest man to death", God doesn't multiply volitions to stop it.
5. *Then everything would be supernatural.* The natural/supernatural distinction stands if "natural" means "according to God's general laws". Augustine: a miracle is not against nature but "contrary to what is known… about nature", since "the will of the Creator is the nature of each" creature (*City of God* XXI.8).
6. *Man wills and sins, so creatures act.* Willing, yes. But our volitions are "impotent in themselves". The "effort" we feel when moving our arm is only a sensation God gives us "to make us understand our weakness".
7. *Scripture says "Let the earth bring forth" (Gen 1:11–24) and "the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself" (Mark 4:28).* Scripture speaks according to appearances, as it calls riches "goods". The passages that go *against* common prejudice must be taken literally: "I am the LORD that maketh all things; that stretcheth forth the heavens alone" (Isa 44:24); the Maccabean mother: "it was not I that formed the members of every one of you" (2 Macc 7:22). On the Hebrew of Genesis 1, he argues it means "let the earth be green with green", not "let the earth have a power to produce".

**The danger restated.** Scholastic **concurrence** (God acts in every creaturely act, but the creature also really acts) is for him a muddle that comes "after the event, as it were, in order to justify our prejudices and the philosophy of Aristotle". Real natural powers give the sinner an excuse: "Why then should I not love sensible objects, if they are the true causes of the happiness I find in their possession?" Following Maimonides, he finds the origin of idolatry in the generation of Enosh, who honoured the stars as God's ministers. So: love and fear God *in* all things. Honour father and prince only "as unto the Lord, and not to men" (Eph 6:7).

## 4. General Volitions and Theodicy

From Rutherford's chapter and the end of Elucidation 15:
- God need not create. He creates only for His own glory, and nothing finite is worthy of Him except the **Incarnation**: the world exists "for the Church, the Church for Jesus Christ". The fall was permitted because "the universe, restored through Jesus Christ, is worth more than the same universe in its initial construction" (*Dialogues* IX.5).
- God governs nature and grace by **general volitions**, never "particular" ones except for miracles. Monsters, earthquakes and the honest man crushed by a house follow from laws chosen for their simplicity. God "could… make a world more perfect", but not by ways as simple. Wisdom outranks particular outcomes.
- The same holds for **grace**. God wills all to be saved, yet gives grace by general laws, so many graces fall where they do no good. The damned are condemned "by an order that is as worthy of our adoration" as the order that saves the elect.
- **Arnauld** answered him furiously over ideas (*True and False Ideas*, 1683) and over grace. Leibniz took the "simplest ways" principle into his best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy.

## 5. Hume's Theft

Nadler sets out how close Hume follows him.
- Same definition: a cause requires a **necessary connection**.
- Same negative arguments: "There is no object, which implies the existence of any other." Experience shows only "contiguity and succession", "constant conjunction". Matter shows no power: "Solidity, extension, motion; these qualities are all complete in themselves." The mind's power to make an idea would be "a real creation; a production of something out of nothing".
- Hume's one move: apply the test to God too. Saying God's will is connected with its effects "is an identical proposition, and gives us no insight into the nature of this power". With no impression of power, "we have no idea of a being endow'd with any power, much less of one endow'd with infinite power."
- So Malebranche's "only God is a cause" becomes Hume's "nothing is a cause, as far as we can know". The occasionalist emptied creation of power so that all of it would flow to God. The sceptic only had to doubt the last step. See [[Hume on Causation and Induction]].

## 6. The Classical Reply: Aquinas

Malebranche's real target was concurrence, and Aquinas had already answered the occasionalists, who in his day were the Islamic *mutakallimūn*. In ST I q.105 a.5 he names the view that "it is not fire that gives heat, but God in the fire" and calls it impossible:
- "the order of cause and effect would be taken away from created things: and this would imply **lack of power in the Creator**: for it is due to the power of the cause, that it bestows active power on its effect."
- The powers we see in things "would be bestowed… to no purpose"; "the purpose of everything is its operation".
- To the objection that one act can't come from two agents: not from two agents "of the same order", but the same act can come wholly from the First Cause and wholly from the second, at different levels.

## 7. Comparison

| | **Aristotle / Scholastics** | **Malebranche** | **Hume** | **Orthodox (Damascene, Maximus, Palamas)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| do creatures act? | yes, by natures and powers | no; they are occasions | unknowable; we see only conjunction | yes: every nature has its own energy |
| God's role | first cause, concurring | sole cause, by general laws | (no idea of power at all) | Creator who gives natures their energies and works with them |
| what is "nature"? | an inner principle of motion | God's general will | habit of expectation | a *logos* in the Word, with its own energy |
| how we know bodies | by abstraction from sense | by seeing their ideas in God | impressions and copies | the senses plus the *logoi* seen by the purified *nous* |
| the danger he fears | — | idolatry of creatures | dogmatism | a God who works without a real creature (monoenergism) |

## 8. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What he gets right**
- His piety is real and much of it is the Gospel's: "In him we live, and move, and have our being." Without God, creatures "would necessarily revert to nothing". That is Athanasius' "corruption unto non-being" (*Inc.* 4) and Philaret's "bridge of diamond". His warning that we make idols of second causes is just: the sun, money, the "market", "nature" itself. See [[Nothingness East and West: Nishitani, Tanabe, Abe and the Christian Nihil]] and [[Creation out of Nothing]].
- His attack on muddled talk is fair. Calling "concourse" a word that "rouses not a single distinct idea" was a fair hit at late Scholastic jargon, and Hume would not have had his material if it weren't.
- He grounds knowledge in God, against the self-sufficient Cartesian ego. "Say not that you are a light unto yourself" is Augustine, and close to Maximus: we know things truly by their *logoi* in the Logos. See [[Divine Illumination]] and [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].

**Where it goes wrong: monoenergism of the cosmos**
- The Sixth Council settled a question that is Malebranche's question in miniature: does Christ's humanity have its own operation, or does the divine operation work through a passive instrument? The answer was **two natural energies**. St John of Damascus states the general principle: "the nature can neither exist nor be known without its natural operation… where has anyone seen a nature without a motion or without any operation at all?" (*Exact Exposition* III.15). Occasionalism denies exactly this of every creature. It is monoenergism applied to the whole cosmos. If even Christ's humanity has its own energy in perfect union with God, a stone and a fire certainly do. See [[St. Maximus on the Two Wills in Christ]], [[Will Is Proper to Nature]] and [[Action Is Not Proper to the Person]].
- Aquinas' point is the patristic one in Latin: to make creatures powerless is to make God less powerful, not more. A God who can create beings but not *agents* has a smaller creation and a lonelier glory. Malebranche fears that real powers make creatures "gods". But giving operation is not giving divinity. A creature's energy is created and received, and it is *for* communion. The fire burns because God made fire a thing that burns and keeps it so. To admire the fire is to admire the Maker, which is Psalm 104, not idolatry.
- Scripture is plainer than he allows. "Let the earth bring forth"; "the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself" (Mark 4:28); "I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase" (1 Cor 3:6). Paul did plant. His rule that only the verses against "prejudice" count is rigged in his own favour.

**Synergy, not occasion**
- The worst consequence is for the will and grace. If our volitions are "impotent in themselves", and grace comes by general laws that save some and not others "by an order… worthy of our adoration", then human cooperation is a polite fiction. The Orthodox answer is **synergy**. God's uncreated energy and our created energy really work together, two real agents in one act at different levels. "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do" (Phil 2:12–13). Both clauses are true together, and neither is an occasion of the other. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]].
- His theodicy has the same flaw. A God who lets the honest man be crushed rather than "multiply His volitions" values the elegance of His laws over the man. The Gospel's God numbers the hairs of the head and feeds the sparrow, and providence is *personal*. The patristic answer to suffering is not "general volitions" but the Cross, where God enters the particular. See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].

**Seeing in God: close, but not the same**
- The vision in God is near to the Orthodox teaching that the *nous* knows creatures truly by their *logoi* in the Logos. But two differences matter.
  - For Malebranche, what we see in God are Cartesian ideas of *extension*: geometry, not the *logoi* of living natures.
  - The Orthodox view also needs **purification**. Natural contemplation (*theoria physike*) follows ascesis. It is not a law of every mind, as his "union with universal Reason" is.
- He also merges God's ideas with God's essence ("the efficacious substance of the Divinity"). Here he lacks the **essence/energies** distinction. Our minds participate in God's *energies*, His wisdom going out to creatures, not in an intelligible copy of His substance. Without the distinction, he has to choose between seeing God's essence (which he denies) and seeing a created idea (which he also denies). He ends with the unclear "God's substance as relative to creatures". The Palamite distinction is the clean answer. See [[Essence-Energies Distinction]].

**What I take from it**
- Malebranche and Hume show where a philosophy of pure, inert matter ends: either God does everything or nothing can be known to do anything. Both conclusions come from first stripping creatures of *energeia*. The way out is the older one: real natures with real activities, held in being by the Word and working *with* Him.
- "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work" (John 5:17). The Father works, and the Son works, and in the Son created nature works too.
