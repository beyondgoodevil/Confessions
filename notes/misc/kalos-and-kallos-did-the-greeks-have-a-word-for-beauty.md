We translate **kalos** as "beautiful," but in Greek it also means **fine, well made, noble and honourable**, so some scholars say the Greeks had **no word for beauty** at all. David Konstan argues the opposite: there was such a word, the noun **kallos**, but it was **narrower** than ours and tied above all to **erotic attraction**. Here's how the argument goes:

### 1. The Problem: Kalos Is Too Broad

- **The range.** The standard lexicon defines *to kalon* as "beauty" but also as "virtue" and "honour." The adjective is used of clothes, armour, laws and knowledge (Konstan, pp. 31–32). Sider finds all three senses already in Homer: **beautiful** (Achilles is the most *kalos* warrior), **good of its kind** (harbours, winds and wine are *kalos* for their use), and **noble** (Antigone: "It is *kalon* for me to die having done what I have done").
- **Courage.** When Aristotle says the courageous man acts *kalou heneka*, translators rightly say "for the sake of the **noble**," not "of the beautiful" (Kosman, quoted by Konstan, p. 32).
- **The sceptics.** Kosman admits an "urge" (which he resists) to say the Greeks had no concept of beauty. He notes that the modern idea of beauty involves **art** and **nature** in a way *kalon* doesn't. Irwin thinks Aristotle's *kalon* is ambiguous between beauty and moral rightness and prefers "fine" or "admirable." Eco puts it most strongly: "Beauty had no autonomous statute in ancient Greece," and "*Kalón* ... only improperly may be translated by the term 'beautiful'" (Eco, pp. 37–39).
- **The defender.** Drew Hyland translates *kalos* as "beauty" throughout, since English "beautiful" is just as broad ("that's a beautiful idea"). But he sometimes has to write "beautiful **and noble**," which Konstan takes as a giveaway (p. 34).

**Eco's evidence for beauty tied to other values.** The Muses' refrain at the wedding of Cadmus and Harmony: "Only that which is beautiful is loved, that which is not beautiful is not loved." The Delphic oracle, asked for the criterion of beauty: "The most beautiful is the most just." And the ideal of **kalokagathia**, beauty of body joined with goodness of soul (Eco, pp. 37–45).

### 2. Konstan: Kallos Is the Greek Word for Beauty

Konstan's thesis: the noun **kállos**, related to *kalós* but used differently, "comes much closer to the modern notion of beauty". If anything it is **narrower** than English *beauty*: its primary meaning is **physical beauty, above all the beauty associated with erotic attraction** (pp. 34–35).

1. **Who has kallos in Homer.** Only three men in the *Iliad*: **Paris** (whose beauty made Helen run off with him), **Bellerophon** (the object of a woman's passion), and **Ganymede** (carried off by Zeus). Never Achilles or Agamemnon, and among gods only Aphrodite. Women prized as brides have it: Helen, Nausicaa, Penelope bathed by Athena in "ambrosial *kallos*" (pp. 35–37).
2. **Kalos is used differently.** When Priam asks who the impressive Greek leader is, *kalos* means imposing and kingly, with no sexual allure. When Achilles says "Don't you see how *kalos* and big I am?" to the man he is about to kill, he isn't boasting of his looks (pp. 39–40).
3. **The test case.** A verse ascribed to Theocritus: "a boy's beauty [*kállos*] is a fine thing [*kalón*], but it endures a short while." If the two words meant the same thing, the line would be nonsense (p. 37). Clement of Alexandria likewise tells Hadrian to keep the *kallos* of his beloved Antinous pure "so that it may be *kalon*," i.e. honourable.
4. **The one object.** Apart from people, the only thing with *kallos* in Homer is a **silver mixing bowl** of Sidonian make that Achilles offers as a prize (p. 42): a highly desirable artefact.

- **Significance:** if Konstan is right, Greek beauty began as **what is desired**. That bears on a modern quarrel: Scruton says beauty, from Plato on, is "a call to renounce" desire, while Nehamas says that for Plato beauty is "the quarry of *erôs*." Konstan thinks that because they disagree about Plato, the vocabulary has to be settled first (pp. 9–10, 28–30).

### 3. The Hippias Major: "What Is the Fine Itself?"

The one dialogue devoted to defining *to kalon* shows why the word is so slippery. Woodruff's translation in Cooper uses **"fine,"** and the editors explain that *kalon* covers "our 'beautiful' (in physical, aesthetic, and moral senses), 'noble,' 'admirable', 'excellent', and the like."

Socrates wants "not what is a fine thing, but what is the fine" (287e). Hippias' answers:

1. **"A fine girl is a fine thing"** (287e). But a fine mare, lyre or pot is fine too, and the finest girl is "foul" next to a goddess (289a–b, citing Heraclitus).
2. **Gold.** But Phidias made Athena's face of ivory (290b), and a figwood spoon is "more appropriate" for the soup than a gold one, so finer (290d–291c).
3. **A rich, healthy, honoured life, buried well by your children.** Not fine for heroes born of gods, who could not be buried by their descendants (291d–293b).

Socrates then tries **the appropriate** (it may only make things *seem* fine), **the useful** and **the beneficial** (but then the fine causes the good and so differs from it), and finally **"what is pleasant through hearing and sight"** (297e–298a), which can't say what those two pleasures have in common. The dialogue ends: "What's fine is hard" (304e).

- **Sider's pot.** Socrates' example is a *chytra*, a common, undecorated cooking pot, "smooth and round and finely fired" (288d). Sider's point: Socrates appreciates the **craft** in an ordinary object, which seems to have no literary parallel. He also reads Socrates' demand that "the thing as a whole" be fine in its arrangement as a dig at Hippias' collections of facts: a work is beautiful by **unity**, not accumulation.
- **Konstan's reading.** The dialogue isn't really "about beauty." Its *kalon* starts out as excellence in general (Hippias' money-making is *kalon*), and only the last definition comes near beauty. Once, exasperated, the questioner asks "what is ***kállos*** in and of itself?" (292d), as if narrowing the target (Konstan, pp. 116–119).

### 4. How Plato Lets the Words Slide

In the *Symposium*, *Phaedrus* and *Republic*, Plato puts beauty among the transcendent Forms, and Konstan shows how the two words **slide into each other** to make that possible (pp. 119–125):

- **Symposium.** Socrates argues that if Eros is *erôs* for *kallos*, Eros lacks *kallos*, so isn't *kalos*. Then, by "a certain sleight of hand," he identifies *kalos* with *agathos* ("good"), which only works because of the adjective's moral sense. Diotima's ladder starts with *kallos* in bodies and ends with "the *kalon* itself."
- **Phaedrus.** Of all the Forms, **beauty is the brightest** and the only one we glimpse through the eyes, so physical beauty, especially a boy's, is what most draws us up to the Forms (250b–d).
- **Plotinus**, *Ennead* I.6, goes from sights and sounds up to fine habits and knowledge and ends with "the *kallos* of the virtues," so that something abstract still sounds visible.

- **Significance:** the Platonic synthesis ("we seek the good because the good is beautiful") works by joining the **erotic** word (*kallos*) to the **moral** word (*kalon*). Desire for beauty becomes the ladder to goodness. Gregory of Nyssa then empties divine *kallos* of shape and colour: God's beauty is "purity, freedom from passion, blessedness," and only the word and its link to love remain (Konstan, p. 126).

### Conclusion

*Kalos* is wider than "beautiful": fine, well made, noble. That is why scholars from Kosman to Eco doubt the Greeks had a separate idea of beauty, and why the *Hippias Major*'s search for "the fine itself" collapses into fine girls, gold spoons and pots. Konstan's answer is that Greek did have a word for beauty, *kallos*, but it meant **desirable physical beauty** first. Plato's achievement was to fuse that erotic word with the moral *kalon*, so that the desire beauty provokes could lead upward to the good. Our modern puzzles about beauty and desire (Scruton against Nehamas) are partly the afterlife of that fusion.
