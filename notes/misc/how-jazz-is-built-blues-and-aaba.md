Jazz sounds free, but almost every performance runs on a **repeating cycle of bars and chords** called the **chorus**, and two cycles account for most of it: the **twelve-bar blues** and the **thirty-two-bar AABA song**. Giddins and DeVeaux say you only need to "hear four beats in a measure, count to thirty-two," and tell an A melody from a B. Here's how it works:

### 1. The Chorus: A Cycle

- **Theme and variations.** Gioia says over 95 percent of the jazz you'll hear has three parts: the band plays the **melody** (the "head"), the players take **solos** over the song's chords, and the band **returns to the melody**.
- **The cycle comes from Africa.** In African music improvisation happens within a rhythmic cycle; in jazz the cycle, the **chorus**, has two dimensions, **rhythm and harmony**: a fixed number of bars, with chords changing at fixed points (Giddins).
- **Fixed layer, variable layer.** Giddins' example is the two-bar chord loop amateur pianists play from "Heart and Soul." The last chord demands a return to the first, so it never ends. The left hand repeats it endlessly while the right hand makes up melodies on top. Neither works alone: the loop is boring, the melody is fragments. Jazz is that, with a band.

> [!definition] Chorus
> One complete pass through a tune's form (twelve bars for a blues, thirty-two for most songs). Solos are measured in choruses: "a five-chorus solo".

### 2. The Twelve-Bar Blues

The blues comes from African American **folk poetry**: a three-line stanza where the second line repeats the first and the third answers it (Giddins quotes Bessie Smith's "Reckless Blues").

1. **Three lines of four bars.** Each line is about two and a half bars of singing followed by **a bar and a half of instrumental response**: **call and response**. Three lines of four bars make twelve.
2. **Three chords.** The basic progression uses only the **I** (tonic), **IV** and **V** (dominant) chords:

| Line | Bars 1–4 / 5–8 / 9–12 |
|---|---|
| 1 | I · I · I · I |
| 2 (repeats line 1) | IV · IV · I · I |
| 3 (the answer) | V · V · I · I |

The move to **IV** marks the start of the second line, perhaps because its words repeat the first line (Giddins).

3. **Substitutions.** Musicians vary the long stretches of one chord, e.g. moving to IV in bar 2, or adding a V chord in bars 2 and 6 as in "Reckless Blues."
4. **The turnaround.** Bars **11–12** carry a transitional progression leading back to I and the next chorus.

**Listening (timings from Giddins).**

- **Louis Armstrong, "West End Blues" (1928).** Choruses start at 0:16, 0:50, 1:24, 1:59 and 2:32. In each, IV arrives about twelve seconds in. Chorus 4 is Earl Hines's piano solo, where he swaps the simple on-the-beat chords of chorus 1 for "a dense harmonic thicket."
- **Pete Johnson and Big Joe Turner, "It's All Right, Baby."** A fast blues: each chorus takes only **fifteen seconds**, so there are ten of them.
- **Charlie Parker, "Now's the Time" (1953).** Still a twelve-bar blues, though more dissonant. The **head** (a six-note riff) is played twice; then Parker solos for **five choruses**, the pianist for two, bass and drums for one each, and the head returns at 2:42. In a blues the chords are always the same, so the head is what makes one tune different from another.

The blues later became the foundation of **rhythm and blues** and **rock and roll** (Giddins).

### 3. The Thirty-Two-Bar AABA Song

The other great form comes from the **golden age of American songwriting** (about 1925–60): Gershwin, Kern, Berlin, Porter, Rodgers, Arlen, Mercer, and jazz writers like Ellington and Fats Waller.

- **Verse dropped.** Theatre songs had an introductory **verse** and a thirty-two-bar **refrain**. Jazz musicians ignored the verse and turned the refrain into a **repeating cycle**, "not unlike the African tradition" (Giddins).
- **The recipe.** Write an eight-bar phrase, repeat it, contrast it with a new eight-bar phrase (the **bridge** or **release**, ending on a half cadence to push forward), then repeat the first phrase once more:

| Section | Bars | Job |
|---|---|---|
| A | 8 | statement |
| A | 8 | repetition (often ending with a full cadence where the first ended on a half cadence) |
| B (bridge) | 8 | contrast |
| A | 8 | return |

- **It's about the music, not the words.** The words of the three A's differ; the melody and chords are what repeat. And unlike the blues, AABA has **no fixed chords**: it is a pattern, filled with whatever harmonies the composer chose.

### 4. Borrowed Chords: Rhythm Changes and Other Variants

1. **Rhythm changes.** The most used song in jazz is Gershwin's **"I Got Rhythm"** (1930). Musicians loved its chords but not its melody, which (with the lyrics) was **the only part protected by copyright**. So they kept the chords ("changes") and wrote new melodies on them, also cutting Gershwin's two-bar tag to keep a clean **32 bars**. Ellington's "Conga Brava" and Monk's "Rhythm-a-ning" came out of this (Giddins).
2. **Other borrowed progressions.** Charlie Parker's "Ko-Ko" is built on the chords of the 1939 song "Cherokee."
3. **Modal AABA.** In Miles Davis's **"So What,"** each A is a riff in call and response with the bass, and the **bridge moves the whole thing up a half step**. Listen for that shift and you can follow every chorus (Giddins).
4. **ABAC.** A variant where the opening melody returns halfway through instead of a bridge, or think of it as two sixteen-bar halves, the first ending on a half cadence and the second on a full one. "Star Dust" and "Embraceable You" are examples (Giddins).

### 5. Why Jazz Forms Are So Simple

Gioia's answer is **Thomas Edison**. Early records held only about **three minutes**.

- **Before:** Scott Joplin's rags used four sections arranged AABBACCDD, and early jazz composers like **Jelly Roll Morton** kept that complexity. Morton's "Sidewalk Blues" (1926) even switches from twelve-bar blues to a thirty-two-bar song in mid-performance. Gioia tells how Morton settled an argument with a trombonist about playing a melody as written by putting a large pistol on the piano; Gioia reads it as a sign of how seriously Morton took his written structures.
- **After:** to leave room for solos, most bands switched to twelve- and thirty-two-bar forms. **Duke Ellington** was the exception: his "Sepia Panorama" (1940) is a palindrome of four themes, **ABCDDCBA**. When Paul Bowles attacked his suite *Black, Brown & Beige* for trying to make jazz into art music, Ellington never again wrote anything on that scale, though Gioia says history has proved him right.

### 6. Who Does What in the Rhythm Section

- **Bass.** The foundation: a steady **walking bass** of four even beats a bar, while also playing the **root** of each new chord. A **pedal point** is when the bass stops moving and holds one note (Giddins).
- **Piano (or guitar).** The chart only names the chords ("Cmaj7"); the pianist chooses the voicing, adds extra notes, uses **substitutions**, and places chords irregularly. This is **comping**, from "accompanying" (Giddins).
- **Drums.** A constant **ride pattern** on the cymbal plus accents.
- **For listeners:** Gioia says **follow the bass, not the drums**, to find the beat, since modern drumming happens largely between the beats.

**Four-bar units.** All the main forms (12, 16, 32 bars) divide by four, and since Kansas City jazz in the 1930s, jazz has breathed in four-bar phrases. When a soloist swaps phrases with the drummer it's called **trading fours**; if not fours, then eights or twelves. In Gioia's words: "Jazz players aren't prime number folks."

### 7. How to Listen

1. **Count.** Find the beat (from the bass), count bars in fours, and feel where each chorus begins: the head, then each soloist's choruses, then the head again.
2. **Recognise the form.** If the chords come round every twelve bars with a shift to IV in bar 5, it's a blues. If there's a contrasting section after sixteen bars, it's AABA, with the bridge at bar 17.
3. **Then forget it.** Giddins says knowing the form isn't necessary for enjoying the music, but once you have it you hear the soloists' choices: Davis's taut phrases against Coltrane's "lengthy gusts of notes" over the same cycle in "So What."

### Conclusion

Jazz improvisation isn't formless. It runs on a cycle, the chorus, inherited from African music: a fixed layer of bars and chords under a variable layer of melody. Two cycles dominate: the twelve-bar blues, from folk poetry, with its three lines, call and response, and I–IV–V progression; and the thirty-two-bar AABA song, from Broadway and Tin Pan Alley, with its bridge. Rhythm changes, borrowed progressions and ABAC are variations on these, and the three-minute 78 is a large part of why they became so dominant. Count to twelve or thirty-two, follow the bass, and the apparent free-for-all becomes, in Gioia's comparison, something closer to a very fast chess match.
