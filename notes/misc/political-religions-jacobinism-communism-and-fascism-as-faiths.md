In 1792 the American minister in Paris, Gouverneur Morris, wrote that he had lived to see a "new Religion arise. It consists in a Denial of all Religion and its Votaries have the Superstition of not being superstitious." It had "as much Zeal as any other Sect" and was "as ready to lay Waste the World in order to make Proselytes" (quoted in Stuart, *Gods of Revolution*, p. vii). The idea that modern revolutionary movements were religions in disguise has been put forward ever since, by Tocqueville in the 1850s, by a cluster of exiles in the 1930s, and by historians today. This note follows three bodies of work. Christopher Dawson's *The Gods of Revolution*, written mostly in the 1930s and published after his death in 1972, reads the French Revolution as the realisation of a "religion of democracy". Michael Burleigh's two volumes, *Earthly Powers* (2005) and *Sacred Causes* (2006–07), run from the Jacobins through the nineteenth-century "religions of humanity" and Russian terrorism to Bolshevism, Fascism and Nazism. George Weigel's *The Final Revolution* (1992) argues that communism was a secularised religion and fell to what he calls "a revolution of conscience". All three writers are Christians, and their sympathies are discussed in the assessment. The Chinese case is in [[Was Mao a Monster? Chang and Halliday and Their Critics]].

### 1. The Idea and Its Pedigree

Burleigh traces the phrase "political religions" to the Calabrian friar Tommaso Campanella. Responding to Machiavelli, Campanella complained that religion had become a political convenience instead of an end in itself (*Earthly Powers*, p. 19). Campanella's own utopia, *The City of the Sun* (written 1602), was ruled by a priest-king and walled with didactic images, with property and sex regulated. Burleigh calls it "a proto-totalitarian environment, with a state religious cult" (p. 21), and Lenin later wanted Campanella's name on a Moscow obelisk (p. 17). During the Revolution itself, the German writer Christoph Martin Wieland saw "a type of new political religion" being preached by French generals at the head of their armies, intolerant as "Mohamed and the Theodosians": "Whoever is not with them, is against them" (pp. 21–22). Tocqueville gave the classic formulation:

> Because the Revolution seemed to be striving for the regeneration of the human race even more than for the reform of France, it lit a passion which the most violent political revolutions have never before been able to produce. It inspired conversions and generated propaganda. … it itself became a new kind of religion, an incomplete religion, it is true, without God, without ritual, and without life after death, but one which nevertheless, like Islam, flooded the earth with its soldiers, apostles, and martyrs. (quoted p. 3)

After 1917 the comparison became common. In 1920 Bertrand Russell concluded that Bolshevism's hopes were "as admirable as those instilled by the Sermon on the Mount, but they are held as fanatically", and that the war had left a mood "which calls aloud for a new religion … Bolshevism has supplied the new religion" (*Sacred Causes*, p. 39). The 1930s produced the theorists. Franz Werfel called Communism and Nazism "anti-religious surrogates for religion" (*Earthly Powers*, p. 4). Waldemar Gurian called Bolshevik atheism "the expression of a new religious faith, the faith in an earthly absolute", whose "new 'God' is the Socialist society" (*Sacred Causes*, p. 118). Eric Voegelin's *The Political Religions* (1938) distinguished "world-transcendent" from "world-immanent" religions, which Burleigh glosses as "the difference between a god and an idol". Voegelin traced the second kind from the pharaoh Akhenaton through medieval Gnostic heresies to the Führer, to whom alone "the god speaks" (*Earthly Powers*, pp. 5–6). Later he described the ideologies as a "radical immanentizing of the eschaton" (p. 7). Raymond Aron, writing in London in 1944, defined "secular religions" as "doctrines that, in the souls of our contemporaries, take the place of the faith that is no more, placing the salvation of mankind in this world, in the more or less distant future, and in the form of a social order yet to be invented" (*Sacred Causes*, p. 122).

Two earlier voices deserve mention. In the 1909 *Vekhi* (*Landmarks*) symposium, the Russian philosopher Semyon Frank wrote of the revolutionary intelligent: "Sacrificing himself for the sake of this idea, he does not hesitate to sacrifice other people for it … Thus the great love of mankind of the future gives birth to a great hatred for people; the passion for organizing an earthly paradise becomes a passion for destruction." The revolutionaries were "militant monks of the nihilistic religion of earthly contentment" (pp. 39–40). In 1924 the German writer Christoph Bry, in his forgotten *Verkappte Religionen* ("Hidden Religions"), saw Hitler as "a cross between a holy-roller and a provincial prima donna". Bry also argued that Marx had turned utopian socialism "into a hidden religion supported by what passed for science but which was a form of prophecy involving his own chosen people – the industrial proletariat" (pp. 29–31).

Burleigh himself does not treat these movements as cynical. Following the Italian priest Luigi Sturzo, he calls them "the abusive exploitation of the human religious sentiment" (*Sacred Causes*, p. vi), movements that "metabolised the religious instinct" (p. viii). He also flags the concept's weakness. Aron's definition "defines religion so broadly that it could encompass old graffiti about the pop star 'Eric Clapton is God' or fans' 'worship' of Manchester United" (*Earthly Powers*, p. 10).

### 2. Dawson: Rousseau and the Religion of Democracy

Dawson's starting point is anthropological. "Every living culture must possess some spiritual dynamic … Normally this dynamic is supplied by a religion, but in exceptional circumstances the religious impulse may disguise itself under philosophical or political forms" (from *Progress and Religion*, quoted by Stuart, p. xv). His story of secularisation begins with persecution. The revocation of the Edict of Nantes scattered Huguenot publicists across Europe, and the Enlightenment that followed "like most of the movements that have changed the world, was religious in origin, although it was anti-religious in its results" (p. 14). In France there was no room for an English-style compromise. The philosophes aimed "to replace one unity by another, to substitute the universal reign of science and reason for that of religion and authority" (p. 25). Yet their liberalism was aristocratic and cold. Voltaire never meant to enlighten "shoemakers and servant girls" (p. 26), and "the Enlightenment had swept and garnished the western mind without bringing anything to take the place of the religion that it had destroyed" (p. 31).

For liberal ideas to reach the people, they "had to be transformed from a philosophy into a religion: to cease to be mere ideas and to become articles of faith". That was the work of Rousseau, "who thus became the founder and prophet of a new faith—the religion of democracy" (p. 31). On the road to Vincennes in 1749 Rousseau grasped that men's ills came "not to man's own sin or ignorance but to social injustice and the corruptions of an artificial civilization". His teaching came into the salons "like a warm west wind from the fields into a lighted salon", and he "first fired men's minds with the ideal of democracy not as a mere system of government but as a new way of life, a vision of social justice and fraternity which is nothing else than the kingdom of God on earth" (pp. 32–33). Dawson notes ironies. Rousseau was anti-bourgeois, closer on usury and the poor to the Catholic preachers than to the economists, and thought democracy suited only small states (pp. 33–34). The real social contract had been signed in 1620 by Puritans on the *Mayflower*, a "democratic theocracy" (pp. 34–35).

Thomas Paine supplied the missing element. In *Common Sense* ("We have it in our power to begin the world over again") Dawson finds, he thinks for the first time, revolution as "a universal and almost cosmic change" together with "messianic idealism, which looked forward to a social millennium and the birth of a new humanity". Both had roots in the Anabaptists and the millenarian sects, and their union with Enlightenment rationalism "marks the definite emergence of the modern revolutionary creed" (pp. 40–41). The Revolution therefore met in France "an immense movement of social idealism which was of the nature of a religious revival … a real religion, with a definite though simple body of dogmas which aspired to take the place of Christianity". It even had a ready-made hierarchy in Freemasonry (p. 51). The Declaration of the Rights of Man became its creed: "Without it, the Revolution might have been nothing more than a new Fronde. With it, it changed the world" (p. 53).

### 3. The Jacobin Church

Dawson and Burleigh describe the same institutions. After the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) tried to bind the old Church to the new state, Dawson argues, what the Revolution really demanded was "a new civic religion which would be entirely totalitarian in spirit and which would recognize no higher duty than the service of the state" (p. 60). The Jacobin clubs, 406 affiliates by May 1791, "were in fact to be the churches of the new religion". One Jacobin wrote: "How was the Christian religion established? By the preaching of the apostles of the Gospel. How can we firmly establish the Constitution? By the mission of the apostles of liberty and equality." The missionary was to carry the Declaration, the Constitution, an almanac, "a good tract against fanaticism, a good journal and a good model of a pike" (pp. 64–65). The creed was the Declaration, the scripture the *Social Contract*, and the cult centred on "the Altar of the Fatherland, the Tree of Liberty, the Book of the Constitution", addressed to deified Reason, Liberty, Nature and the Fatherland. Dawson sums it up: "The Cross has been replaced by the Tree of Liberty, the Grace of God by the Reason of Man, and Redemption by Revolution" (p. 66). After the purges of 1794, the Jacobin society was "the established Church of the republic", and Robespierre "the Pope of the new church" (p. 90).

Burleigh adds the texture. Rousseau's *Social Contract* (IV.8) had proposed a civil religion with belief in God and an afterlife, the sanctity of the social contract, and the banishment of unbelievers and execution of apostates. Rousseau, Burleigh notes, was more admiring of Islam's fusion of sacred and temporal than of Christianity's separation of the two, which was "with hindsight its major saving grace" (*Earthly Powers*, p. 78). Mirabeau wrote that "the Declaration of the Rights of Man has become a political Gospel and the French Constitution a religion for which people are prepared to die". Marie-Joseph Chénier wanted "the single universal religion … of which our law-makers are the preachers, the magistrates the pontiffs" (p. 81). Religious vocabulary (catechism, credo, gospel, martyr, missionary, sermon) migrated wholesale. "Regeneration" borrowed from baptism, and the Committee of Public *Salut* promised the citizen: "We will never leave him to himself alone" (pp. 81–82). Rejecting original sin, the Jacobins thought the child "a soft wax capable of receiving whatever imprint one wishes". Their catechism answered "What is Baptism?" with "the regeneration of the French begun on 14 July 1789", and "Penitence" with the banishment of traitors (pp. 82–83).

The festivals were the liturgy. David, "a sort of 'Robespierre of the brush'", painted the martyrs Le Peletier, Marat ("part antique torso, part dying Christ") and the boy Bara, and staged the pageants (pp. 69–76). Michelet said that at these feasts "time and space … are no more" (p. 76). The cycle ran from the Federation of 1790, with Talleyrand saying mass at the Altar of the Fatherland, to the Festival of Unity in 1793, where a statue of Nature poured water from her breasts, and finally Robespierre's Festival of the Supreme Being on 8 June 1794. There he burned a cardboard Atheism, from which a smoke-blackened Wisdom emerged (pp. 77–80, 103–04). In his report of 7 May 1794, Robespierre declared that "Half of the world revolution is already done; the other half has still to be achieved". France was "two thousand years in advance of the rest of the world", and recalling men to the Supreme Being would "strike a mortal blow at fanaticism" (Dawson, pp. 92–93). Dawson takes the religious feeling seriously. Robespierre's prayer at the feast was sincere, and for every good Jacobin the day "seemed to consecrate the triumph of the cause of humanity" (p. 94).

### 4. Terror as Purification

The core of both accounts is that the religious structure helped produce the killing. Dawson insists "there was no contradiction between the Reign of Terror and the religion of humanity. The guillotine was as much an emblem of the Jacobin ideal as the Tree of Liberty, and the Goddess of Liberty was a jealous deity who could not be appeased save by the traditional rite of human sacrifice" (p. 95). Paris executions rose from 122 in March 1794 to 966 in July (p. 95 n. 3). "The religion of virtue which Robespierre preached was responsible for as much injustice and human suffering as any of the fanaticisms or superstitions of the barbaric past." Robespierre destroyed "with the cold benevolence of an inquisitor who was determined to stamp out heresy and make men virtuous and orthodox whether they wished it or not" (pp. 95–96). Saint-Just, who "carried his head like a holy sacrament", spoke of terror as "the fire of liberty which must purify us as the dross is purged from the molten metal" (pp. 87–88). He also said: "You have to punish not only the traitors but also the indifferent" (p. 83). Dawson notes that Robespierre's distinction between revolutionary and constitutional government anticipates the communist distinction between proletarian dictatorship and the classless society (p. 84). He calls the Jacobin order "a democratic dictatorship, not unlike that of the Soviets in its earlier phases" (p. 82).

Burleigh describes the mentality as Manichaean. "Openness versus Hypocrisy, Virtue versus Vice, Good versus Evil, Light versus Darkness: this was a Manichaean view of the world, heavily indebted to monotheistic religion", and dissent "bore the taint of moral leprosy", culminating in the Law of Suspects (*Earthly Powers*, p. 91). The Jacobins' intolerance "derived and distilled from religious discourse, as mediated and secularised by the intellectual intolerance of the Enlightenment" (p. 93). The cult turned an injured locksmith named Geffroy into a Christ-figure of the "new man": "Our regeneration will be sublime, it will consume the old man to form the new man: it will annihilate kings and priests" (p. 94). In the Vendée, a peasant rising whose primary impulse was hostility to de-Christianisation, the Committee of Public Safety ordered its generals to "exterminate all in this race of bandits". General Turreau ordered the same for "women, girls and children", and at Nantes Carrier drowned some 1,800 people in what were called "vertical deportations into the national bathtub" (pp. 96–101). Burleigh's verdict is blunt: "This was the first occasion in history when an 'anticlerical' and self-styled 'non-religious' state embarked on a programme of mass murder that anticipated many twentieth-century horrors … tantamount to genocide" (p. 97). Dawson, writing in the 1930s, was more restrained, but described the same "official policy of indiscriminate massacre" (p. 105).

### 5. Why the Civic Cults Failed

The Jacobin religion did not take. Only 62 of 593 children born in Poitiers in Year II received revolutionary names (*Earthly Powers*, p. 84). The Directory's theophilanthropy filled nineteen Parisian temples, and "None of the civic cults of the Revolution had managed to engender much of a popular response" (p. 106). Burleigh's summary: "The first sustained attempts to create secular civic cults, rivalling traditional Christianity, had failed miserably" (p. 111). Dawson's explanation is that persecution revived what it attacked. The Abbé Pinot went to the scaffold saying "Introibo ad altare Dei", and the generation of Lamennais and the Curé d'Ars turned to Christianity with fervour. "Thus the Revolution, which was the child of the Enlightenment, also proved to be its destroyer." "Rationalism flourishes best in a prosperous age and a sheltered society; it finds few adherents among the unfortunate and the defeated" (p. 118). Joseph de Maistre drew the opposite lesson from the same facts: "What we are witnessing is a religious revolution; the rest, immense as it seems, is but an appendix" (p. 121). Napoleon, who said he would "rebuild the Temple of Solomon" if he governed Jews, settled matters with the Concordat (*Earthly Powers*, p. 107). In Dawson's view he also destroyed "the revolutionary idealism that had been the dynamic spiritual force behind it" (p. 132).

### 6. Religions of Humanity: Saint-Simon, Comte, Owen

The nineteenth century saw, in Burleigh's phrase, a retreat from Christianity "into irreligion" that was "paralleled by a much larger retreat from Christianity into religion" (*Earthly Powers*, p. 222). "Virtually all of the Utopians, except the most successful, felt compelled to crown their creations with a religion". All of them shared the "inherently despotic" belief that human nature was good and evil the product of circumstance. Burleigh quotes an 1840 pamphlet: "What if people do not want this?" — "What if the inmates of the Bicêtre [mad house] refuse to have baths?" (p. 223). Saint-Simon, for whom "The throne of the absolute could not remain untenanted", proposed a "New Christianity" and admitted that "Religion is the collection of applications of general science by means of which enlightened men rule the ignorant" (p. 227). When an aspiring founder of a new religion complained to Talleyrand that he was making few converts, the reply came: "I would recommend you to be crucified and rise again the third day" (p. 228). Comte's Religion of Humanity had saints' days for Archimedes and Gutenberg, nine sacraments and a Positivist hell. British sceptics called it "Catholicism minus Christianity", and its founder "gradually metamorphosed" from a would-be Aristotle "into a would-be St Paul" (p. 230). Robert Owen announced in 1834 "the great Advent of the world, the second coming of Christ, – for Truth and Christ are one and the same". His Queenswood community bore the letters "C. M.", "Commencement of the Millennium", and sang "Community is happiness, / Community is heaven" (pp. 239–40). Burleigh notes that as a creed Positivism "was eclipsed by socialism, the only non-supernatural religion of humanity with mass currency" (p. 232).

Dawson pairs these new religions with the Christian revival as two halves of a single reaction against the Enlightenment. On one side were "the new Christianity of the St. Simonians, Comte's Religion of Humanity, or Mazzini's Religious Nationalism"; on the other the Catholic renaissance. In his view "the nineteenth century, especially the first half of it, was a religious age" (p. 129).

### 7. The Nation as Church: Mazzini

Burleigh warns that "Nationalism was not simply a surrogate for Christianity … These relationships were subtler than surrogacy suggests". It borrowed divine election, and for elite minorities patriotism became "membership of an alternative Church, or in extreme cases worship of the nation as a God" (*Earthly Powers*, pp. 144–45). A Girondin said in 1791: "What the impostors did in the name of God and the King … you must do in the name of liberty and the patrie" (p. 145). Giuseppe Mazzini was "the most dedicated exponent of what one might call a religion of cosmopolitan Romantic nationalism" (p. 185). Young Italy's programme began: "The one thing wanting to twenty millions of Italians … is not power, but faith" (p. 186). Its rules required that "Young Italy must be neither a sect nor a party, but a faith and an apostolate", and its password was "Martyrdom", answered by "Resurrection". Mazzini called himself "a Christian plus something more" (pp. 187–88). Young Europe would raise two altars, Fatherland and Humanity, whose incense would rise "in two columns … until they are confounded in one point, which is God" (p. 189). Unified Italy then deliberately built a "religion of the Fatherland". One writer complained that "We have closed our new Gods of Reason and Duty within ourselves" and given the masses nothing in place of their faith. Garibaldi's wounds were painted as stigmata, and a set of patriotic commandments began "I am Giuseppe Garibaldi your General" (pp. 195–97). Crispi's roll-calls of the "absent" dead, Burleigh says, show "the sentiments that Fascism would develop and exploit … already gathering around the altars of the nineteenth-century fatherlands" (p. 198).

### 8. Marxism as Eschatology

Burleigh's section "Marxism as eschatology" argues that historians sympathetic to "scientific" socialism resist the notion "that Marxism was a religiously inspired mythopoetic drama carefully camouflaged within various scientific-sounding accretions" (*Earthly Powers*, p. 244). Early communism was full of religious symbolism. John Goodwin Barmby styled himself "Pontifarch of the Communist Church" and believed "that the divine is communism, that the demoniac is individualism". The League of the Just replaced its slogan "All men are Brothers", with "a whiff of Christianity", by "Proletarians of all Countries – Unite!" Engels's first draft of its programme was a "Confession of Faith" (pp. 244–48). Max Stirner had already objected in 1844 that "the human religion is only the last metamorphosis of the Christian religion … it exalts 'Man' to the same extent as any other religion does its God or idol" (p. 249). Burleigh maps the vocabulary: "'consciousness' (soul), 'comrades' (faithful), 'capitalist' (sinner), 'devil' (counter-revolutionary), 'proletariat' (chosen people) and 'classless society' (paradise)". He also maps the structure: a Fall (the end of primitive communism), linear time, and an Apocalypse-Revolution producing new men (pp. 250–51). His conclusion: "Marxism combined the assurance that everything was operating according to the dispositions of secularised versions of higher powers with Gnostic sectarian belief that the messianic elect that had grasped these laws was morally entitled to destroy existing society". Like medieval millenarians, communists "took it upon themselves to realise heaven on earth through transforming violence", killing "eighty or a hundred million people in the twentieth century" (p. 251).

Weigel sets the same thesis in a longer frame drawn from Norman Cohn and James Billington. Western millenarianism has two schools. The transcendent one says the Millennium comes by God's action and cannot be "forced"; the political one says an illuminated elite can hasten it (*Final Revolution*, pp. 4–5). Augustine's "solution" read Revelation allegorically and placed the Millennium proleptically in the Church, "not through human agency, especially human political agency. This is precisely what the medieval chiliasts rejected" (p. 7). Joachim of Fiore's three ages reappear in Hegel, Comte and Marx (p. 9). What was new with Rousseau was "the apotheosis of the political as the vehicle for creating the New Jerusalem in this world … politics as ontology, politics as metaphysics, politics as the object of faith". The revolutionaries tried "to steal the Millennium from God" (p. 10). Weigel lists the parts. Communism had a soteriology (revolution), an ecclesiology (the party), apostolic succession, sacred texts (*Manifesto*, *Capital*, *What Is to Be Done?*), rituals of expurgation, an ethics (Dzerzhinsky's), an eschatology, anathemas against whole classes, and heretics. It was "a secularized religion, to be sure; a false religion, certainly; but a religion" (pp. 11–12). Its staying power, greater than that of "its demonic twin, Nazism", came because "it ironically provided what it denied men needed—a faith and a community of faithful that was universal in scope" (p. 12).

### 9. New Men and Sacred Violence in Russia

Burleigh's Russian chapter links the theory to the practice of terror: "In the east a form of political religion was developing in which 'sacred violence' was intrinsic to men who considered themselves to be 'just' murderers" (*Earthly Powers*, p. 276). The "new men" of the 1860s took their name from a sense of "baptism into a new moral mode of being whose corollary was sometimes the rejection of moral discernment in general" (pp. 277–78). They "had a name and definable characteristics in fiction before" they existed. Chernyshevsky's *What Is to Be Done?* (1863), with its ascetic Rakhmetov sleeping on nails, worked like "a religious conversion" that created "a parallel radical morality" (pp. 278–83). Lenin said it had "ploughed me up more profoundly than anyone else … It's a thing which supplies energy for a whole lifetime" (p. 280). Nechaev's *Revolutionary Catechism* describes the revolutionary as "part revolutionary, part monk", a "doomed man" with "no personal interests or activities, no private feelings, attachments, or property, not even a name", for whom "The revolution sanctifies everything in this battle" (pp. 286–87). Bakunin hailed these "young fanatics" as "Believers without God and heroes without phrases!" (p. 286). Dostoevsky turned the Nechaev murder into *The Possessed*. His epigraph, the Gadarene swine, told "how those devils entered a herd of swine, that is, the Nechaevs" (pp. 292–93). Burleigh's final judgement on Dostoevsky's hope that a new intelligentsia would return to the people's faith: "The prophet was wrong, because the new intelligentsia was in fact inspired by hate, and its militant atheism would destroy the Orthodox Church" (p. 310).

### 10. The Bolshevik Church

In *Sacred Causes* Burleigh describes Soviet communism as both persecutor and counterfeit of religion. The Orthodox Church of 1917 had 100 million adherents and 75,000 churches. Within years its structures were gone, and the first camp of the gulag was opened in a monastery (p. 40). Lenin's memorandum of March 1922 seized on the famine. Now, "when in the regions afflicted by the famine there is cannibalism … we can (and therefore must) pursue the acquisition of church valuables with the most ferocious and merciless energy", subduing resistance "with such brutality that they will not forget it for decades to come" (p. 44). The confiscations had nothing to do with relief, since grain was being exported by late 1922 (p. 45). The regime also tried substitution. The "God-builders" Bogdanov and Lunacharsky taught that "Man does not need God, he himself is God", and Plekhanov mocked them for ending "by proclaiming man a god. But since humanity is not a fiction, why call it god?" (p. 42). Babies were "Octobered" as Octobrina or even "Robesper" with the words "We cover thee not with a cross, not with water and prayer … but with our Red banner". Komsomol carollers sang "We greet thee, sun of the Commune". The plot of revolution, Burleigh observes, "had largely been scripted in Paris in 1789". Yet "since such secular civil ceremonies lacked a transcendental dimension … they were not a success" (pp. 50–52).

The cults of persons did succeed. Zinoviev called Lenin "the leader by the Grace of God", and at the funeral Stalin, who had been schooled at a Tiflis seminary, intoned: "We vow to thee, Comrade Lenin, that we will with honour fulfil this, thy commandment". The mummy, preserved by an "Immortalisation Commission" of former God-builders, became "Saint Vladimir of the October Revolution" (pp. 53–54). By 1936 an orientalist overheard by an NKVD informer could say that "Communism is becoming the national religion of Russia, just as fascism is becoming the national religion of Germany and Italy", marked by hatred of the old religions and "a cult of the vozhd'" (p. 71). Burleigh, drawing on Kotkin, Riegel and Service, argues that the party "reproduced an evolution from sectarian adepts or virtuosi to established Church". It was "like holy water in which deracinated intellectuals and the occasional worker would be baptised". Confession, purification and purge "drifted from the Christian Church into the milieu of ostensibly atheistic revolutionaries", and the result was "a hierocratic Church" with cells for parishes and regional committees for bishoprics (pp. 75–77). Stalin explained the purges: "the master must without fail go through the ranks of the Party with a broom in his hands" (p. 77). Show trials parodied the Inquisition. Where the Inquisition used torture openly to obtain repentance and forgiveness, "In the Soviet cover version, torture was frequently used but never publicly acknowledged, and confession did not bring forgiveness". The faithful could believe "that black was white … if the Party required it" (pp. 81–82). Pyatakov, who had called for Zinoviev to be "destroyed like carrion", ended in the dock as "a man who has lost his Party … who has lost his very self" (pp. 82–84). The "new man" was a diarist like Stepan Podlubnyi, who remade his kulak self on a pattern Burleigh compares to Loyola's Spiritual Exercises and wrote of the Ukrainian famine: "And those who die of hunger, let them die" (pp. 92–94).

### 11. Fascism: "Not Only a Faith, but a Religion"

Mussolini was candid: "Fascism is not only a party, it is a regime, it is not only a regime, but a faith, it is not only a faith, but a religion that is conquering the labouring masses of the Italian people" (*Sacred Causes*, p. 57). Giuseppe Bottai called it a way of "transforming [the war's] values into a civil religion" (p. 57). Fascism dated a new calendar from Year I, October 1922, "in obvious echo of the Jacobins". It held stations of the March on Rome, eternal flames and towers with bells, and called its dead "Present" at roll-calls (pp. 59–61). Its squads were "missionaries" among "idolatrous tribes", wielding the "holy Manganello" (p. 61). The liberal Giovanni Amendola saw the claim clearly: "Fascism wants to own the private conscience of every citizen, it wants the 'conversion' of Italians … It does not promise happiness to those who convert; it allows no escape to those who refuse baptism." A Fascist answered: "Yes indeed, we are totalitarians! We want to be from morning to evening, without distracting thoughts" (p. 61). Alfredo Rocco openly compared the Fascist state's parallel organisations to the Catholic Church's (p. 62). Yet Mussolini drew a line: "The Fascist State does not create a 'God' of its own, as Robespierre once … wished to do; nor does it vainly seek, like Bolshevism, to expel religion from the minds of men" (p. 62). He settled the Roman Question in 1929 and conceded Catholic Action an autonomous sphere. A state that styled itself "totalitarian" thus left the Church its own associations (p. 71).

### 12. Nazism: The Blood and the Banner

Hitler, in Burleigh's portrait, valued belief as such. Goebbels wrote that "It is almost immaterial what we believe in, so long as we believe in something". Hitler admired the Church's "fanatic intolerance" toward pagan altars and complained that Weimar parties lacked "blind faith" (*Sacred Causes*, p. 95). He expelled the völkisch prophet Artur Dinter "not for being religious, but for being too religious in a divisive sectarian way" (p. 99). He presented his movement as Christian when it suited him ("in fact our movement is Christian", 1928), and as a political version of Christ's success: "We National Socialists see in the work of Christ the possibility of achieving the unimaginable through fanatical belief … We want to bring about the same thing in the political sphere" (pp. 99–101). In private, "Pure Christianity" was "merely whole-hearted Bolshevism, under a tinsel of metaphysics" (p. 101). His speeches borrowed the Lutheran Bible directly: "Once you heard the voice of a man, and that voice knocked at your hearts … Yet I feel you, and you feel me!" (pp. 104–05). Burleigh calls the creed "soteriological, a redemptive story of suffering and deliverance … from division to mystic unity based on the blood bond" (p. 105). Its "inner-worldly Nazi Church had 'Blood' as the centre of its creed", followed by Volk, Soil, Reich and Führer. Its holiest relic was the "blood banner" of 1923, which Hitler rubbed against new standards to consecrate them (p. 111). The ninth of November had a "Last Supper" in the Bürgerbräukeller and sixteen "Apostles" in open-air temples, which Hitler called "not vaults but an eternal guardhouse" (pp. 114–15). Watching a Nuremberg rally on film, Victor Klemperer wrote: "The rally is a ritualistic action, National Socialism is a religion – and I would have myself believe that its roots are shallow and weak?" (pp. 113–14). Burleigh adds an uncomfortable point. Liberal Protestant "orders of creation" theology had already extended God's ordinances to the race, and "The sanctification of earthly collectivities inevitably led to the desanctification of individual life" (pp. 105–06).

### 13. Weigel: A Political Religion Undone

Weigel's book is the sequel the earlier analysts hoped for. The Revolution of 1989, he argues, was "first and foremost a revolution of conscience". By "reversing Marx and Lenin", it "overthrew them", making "the transcendent hope which communism understood as an expression of human alienation" the motive of liberation (*Final Revolution*, pp. 3, 6). He builds on Václav Havel. Communism was "not simply a different way of organizing a state. It was an alien worldview … Unlike other forms of modern dictatorship, communism had the formal elements of a religion: it had a doctrine, an ethic, and a ritual" (p. 39). Havel's greengrocer, who puts "Workers of the world, Unite!" among the onions, is really saying "I am afraid and therefore unquestioningly obedient". Each day the citizen burned "that modest pinch of the incense of acquiescence". The system, a "secularized religion" offering "a ready answer to any question whatsoever", demanded "the consignment of reason and conscience". It was "caesaro-papism without God, Christ, or the Church" (pp. 40–45). The answer was "pre-political": living in the truth, "calling good and evil by name", and the norms of truth, responsibility, solidarity and nonviolence. Nonviolence was "a rejection of the Jacobin tradition in which 'revolutionary' was synonymous with 'executioner'". In Adam Michnik's words, "those who start by storming bastilles will end up building their own" (pp. 48–54). For Weigel, John Paul II's pilgrimage of June 1979 "broke the fever of fear". His "Be not afraid!" and "Open the doors to Christ!" answered the question "Whose humanism?" (pp. 56–57, 77–79). The lesson Weigel draws is that "the absolutizing of the political necessarily yields the tyranny of the political" and that "only a reality that transcends politics can truly transform politics" (p. 13).

### 15. Assessment

**What the concept explains.** The political religion thesis accounts for features that interest-based explanations struggle with. These include the movements' claim to make "new men", their liturgies and calendars, and their demand for inner assent, not just outward obedience (Amendola's "conversion", Havel's daily "incense"). They also include the moralised language of heresy and purification, and the readiness to kill for a future paradise. Dawson's link between the Jacobin cult and the Terror, Frank's insight that love of future humanity becomes hatred of present people, and Voegelin's point that a dream world licenses acts that would be "morally insane" in the real one (*Earthly Powers*, p. 7) all point to the same mechanism. When salvation is located in history and the obstacles are human beings, removing them becomes a sacred duty. The continuity the sources trace, from Rousseau and Paine through Babeuf (whose conspiracy Dawson, following Espinas, calls "the logical culmination and the last expression of Jacobinism", pp. 110–11) to Chernyshevsky, Lenin and the Soviet festivals "scripted in Paris in 1789", is well documented in the texts these movements wrote about themselves.

**Its limits.** The concept also has real problems, and the sources themselves name several.

- **Breadth.** Burleigh admits that Aron's definition would cover football fans (*Earthly Powers*, p. 10). Without some test, such as claims on conscience, transcendent ends and rituals of membership, "religion" becomes a loose metaphor for anything people care about intensely.
- **Narrative.** Hans Maier, cited by Stuart, warns that political religion writing tends to neglect events and personalities in favour of mental structures, and Stuart concedes that Dawson "sometimes falls into this weakness" (p. xiii). Dawson's reviewers also found the thesis "overstated and unoriginal", since Tocqueville had made the comparison in 1856 (p. xii). And Dawson largely wrote before the social history of the Revolution, with no scholarship after 1935 in his bibliography.
- **Differences between the movements.** Burleigh warns that the Jacobins "were not simply Lenin's distant French grandparents": they defended property and the family and abhorred class struggle (*Earthly Powers*, p. 90). Mussolini explicitly refused to create a god or expel religion. Hitler expelled the one Nazi who tried to found a church. Nationalism, Burleigh insists, was "subtler than surrogacy suggests". The analogy fits Bolshevism best and Fascism least.
- **The surrogacy assumption.** If political religions simply filled a void left by Christianity, one would expect them to thrive where faith had collapsed. Yet both Dawson and Burleigh show the Jacobin and Soviet mass cults failing among populations that remained religious, while the cults of a living leader worked. The evidence suggests these regimes drew on religious habits of mind more than they replaced religion.

**The authors' standpoint.** All three main sources are Christian, and their conclusions run in a Christian direction. Dawson ends by saying the West must look to Christianity to restore its spiritual unity, or else "it means either the failure of Christianity or the condemnation of modern civilization" (p. 147). Weigel's book is in part a celebration of John Paul II. Burleigh's polemical asides, against "the postmodern Left university", multiculturalism and the BBC, are frequent and sometimes distracting. Burleigh himself notes that many of the most insightful critics of political religion came from religious backgrounds (*Sacred Causes*, p. viii). That cuts both ways. Believers were perhaps quicker to recognise rival faiths, but also inclined to find religion, and its counterfeits, everywhere. The strongest test of the thesis is whether non-believers make the same comparison. Some did, independently: Russell, Aron, Borkenau and Klemperer. And the movements' own statements, from Mirabeau's "political Gospel" and Mussolini's "religion" to Goebbels's "catechism of a new political credo", confirm the analogy at least as self-description.

**Weigel's argument.** Weigel's claim that 1989 was won by conscience and faith is persuasive for Poland. The Church's role there, and the effect of the 1979 pilgrimage, are attested by participants across the religious spectrum. He also sets out the other explanations (Gorbachev, Reagan, Helsinki, economic decay) and does not dismiss them. But his argument is weaker as a general theory. It fits Poland and Czechoslovakia better than the GDR or Hungary, and the post-1989 history of the region shows the "final revolution" was less final than the title suggests. His deeper point stands independently of the Catholic framing, and it matches Dawson and Voegelin: politics that claims to be ultimate becomes tyranny. His contrast between Augustine's deferred City of God and Joachim's realised Third Age names a real fault line in Western thought (see [[Augustine's City of God]]).

### Conclusion

The idea that Jacobinism, communism and fascism were religions began with eyewitnesses like Morris, Wieland and Tocqueville. It was given theoretical form by exiles in the 1930s and has been documented in detail by Dawson, Burleigh and Weigel. Its core is sound. These movements had creeds, scriptures, churches, liturgies, saints, heretics and eschatologies. They demanded conversion, not just compliance, and their promise of an earthly paradise made the extermination of its obstacles a sacred duty, from the Vendée to the kulaks. The concept needs disciplining, though. It fits Bolshevism better than Fascism and party-churches better than nation-states. It describes borrowed religious habits better than true replacements for religion. Its main proponents were Christians whose diagnosis of idolatry implied its cure. The strongest evidence for the thesis is the movements' own words: Mirabeau's "political Gospel", Robespierre's Supreme Being, Mussolini's "religion that is conquering the labouring masses", and the Komsomol's "sun of the Commune". The strongest evidence against simple surrogacy is that the rites failed while the older faith they attacked survived them.
