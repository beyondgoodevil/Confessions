> "Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's." (Matt 22:21)

The Orthodox world has almost never had to *write* a political theology. For eleven centuries in Byzantium and four in Russia the answer was simply lived: a Christian emperor and an Orthodox patriarch working together. Under the Ottomans and then Communism there was only "strategies for survival" (Papanikolaou). Since 1991, for the first time in about six hundred years, most Orthodox live under neither an emperor nor a persecutor, and the question has come back. One emperor and one Church? An established Church inside a democracy? Or a free Church in a free state?

## 1. The Scriptural Starting Points

Every position below argues from the same handful of texts:

- **Israel's king was a concession.** "They have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them" (1 Sam 8:7). God gives a king, but warns what kings do (1 Sam 8:11–18).
- **Power comes from God.** "There is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God… he is the minister of God to thee for good" (Rom 13:1–4). Christ to Pilate: "Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above" (John 19:11).
- **The kingdom is not of this world.** "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36). "Here have we no continuing city, but we seek one to come" (Heb 13:14).
- **Pray for rulers.** "For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life" (1 Tim 2:2). This was written under pagan emperors.
- **Obey God rather than men** when they conflict (Acts 5:29).
- **"He who now letteth"** (2 Thess 2:7), the restrainer who holds back the man of sin. The Fathers often read this as the Roman Empire. That reading is central to the monarchists.

## 2. The History in Brief

### Eusebius: the empire as icon (Papanikolaou ch. 1)

- Eusebius' *Ecclesiastical History* reads three centuries as **providence** culminating in Constantine.
- In the *Tricennial Oration*, Constantine is the Word's "viceroy". The empire is an **image of the heavenly kingdom**. The emperor is the true philosopher-king because he has mastered his passions and knows that every good comes from God.
- **"Two roots of blessing, the Roman Empire and the doctrine of Christian piety, sprang up together."** The *pax romana* lacked only true religion.
- Monarchy is the only fitting constitution. Democracy is "anarchy and disorder", like the many gods of paganism.
- Papanikolaou's point: this is not a crude monotheism (one God, one emperor). It is **sacramental**: the Word, already present in creation, makes the Christian empire an icon of the kingdom. That sacramental instinct runs through all later Orthodox political thought.
- His cost: an empire that images the truth "could not tolerate the free expression of deviations". Jews and non-Chalcedonians were persecuted. It was "a peace based on exclusion and purity of space".

### Chrysostom and the Cappadocians: the state as tutor

- Government as such is from God, even if not every ruler is: "I am not talking about each ruler individually, but about the institution of government."
- **"The king is entrusted with the care of our bodies, the priest with our souls"**, and the priest's office is higher.
- The ruler educates in virtue **through fear**. He "makes virtue easier" by punishing the wicked. Papanikolaou ties this to the ladder of the spiritual life: fear first, then love, which "casteth out fear" (1 John 4:18). So the state is **provisional**: if all loved perfectly, there would be nothing for it to do.

### Justinian: *symphonia*

- **Novella 6** (535): "The greatest blessings granted to human beings… are priesthood and kingdom." The first serves divine things, the second human affairs, and both come "from the same source". If both do their part well, there will be "a good symphony".
- **The *Epanagoge*** (9th century), attributed to Photios: the empire and the priesthood relate "as body and soul".
- In practice the Church was **"keeper of the empire's conscience"**, and **no one questioned the system**. Ambrose barred Theodosius until he repented. Chrysostom rebuked Eudoxia. The monks resisted iconoclast emperors. Bishops and monks resisted heretical or immoral emperors, never the idea of a Christian empire.

### Russia's landmarks

Papanikolaou lists them:

1. **Novgorod's republic** (12th century to 1478), a Christian city with an elected prince and assembly.
2. **Non-possessors against possessors** (16th century). Nil Sorsky's party wanted a poorer Church at a distance from the state. **Joseph of Volokolamsk** won. He was "an extravagant apologist of royal absolutism" who introduced something like divinisation of the tsar.
3. **Philotheus of Pskov's Third Rome** ("two Romes have fallen, the third stands, and there shall be no fourth").
4. **Nikon's reforms and the Old Believer schism**, which weakened the Church.
5. **Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate** (1721) and created a Holy Synod run by a lay ober-procurator, a government official. One ober-procurator was a professed atheist. Priests had to report political confessions to the police. This was **unprecedented** in Orthodox history. The real caesaropapism people attribute to Byzantium "would actually take hold in the emerging Russian Empire".

### The Russian thinkers: freedom grounded in God

- **Vladimir Solov'ev**, the first systematic Orthodox political theology. He proposed a **"free theocracy"**: since communion with God cannot be forced, the Church should be separate from the state. The state protects rights, including religious pluralism and the freedom to reject God. But freedom is an illusion unless it is rooted in the love of God.
- **Sergius Bulgakov**. *An Urgent Task* (1905) called for a Union of Christian Politics: the Church should side with labour, support unions and land reform, and stop being the tsar's tool. He wrote that the Christian form of government is "in no way" the "Tatar-Turkish type of despotic autocracy" raised up by Byzantium, but the federative democratic republic. In exile, *The Orthodox Church* (1935) said separation of Church and state "corresponds with its dignity and its vocation", and that American liberty is "the regime most favorable to the Church". In it the Church influences souls by "the way of liberty".
- **Ethnotheology.** In interwar Romania, Nichifor Crainic proposed an ethnocratic Orthodox state, and the national churches carried symphonia into the nation-state uncritically. Papanikolaou's summary of the whole history: "Eusebius and Chrysostom hated democracy; Solov'ev and Bulgakov saw it as the necessary precondition for realizing divine-human communion."

## 3. Answer One: The Russian Church's *Basis of the Social Concept* (2000)

The fullest official statement of Orthodox social teaching, adopted by the Bishops' Council of 2000.

**Church and nation (II)**
- The Church is "universal and therefore supranational" (Rev 5:9; Col 3:11). Christians have "no continuing city" (Heb 13:14).
- Yet Christ identified with his own people ("salvation is of the Jews", John 4:22) and paid tribute to Caesar. Patriotism is a form of love of neighbour. The document cites St Sergius blessing Dimitry Donskoy before Kulikovo (1380), Patriarch Hermogenes in 1612, and St Philaret of Moscow in 1813.
- A people that is overwhelmingly Orthodox "can in a certain sense be regarded as… an Orthodox nation".
- But "aggressive nationalism, xenophobia, national exclusiveness" are sins. It is "even more contrary to Orthodoxy" to put the nation in the place of God.

**Church and state (III)**
- **The state is not God's original design.** It arose after the Fall as God's "granting human beings an opportunity to order their social life by their own free will", to restrain sin (1 Sam 8; Rom 13). **Anarchy** is "contrary to the Christian outlook". So is making the state **absolute**.
- **Different ends.** The Church's goal is "eternal salvation", the state's "well-being on earth". Where Christ is "all in all", "there is no state".
- **The secular state is acceptable.** It means "a certain division of domains", not that religion "should be radically forced out of all the spheres" of life.
- **The ideal is symphonia**, but it is possible "only in an Orthodox state": "co-operation, mutual support and mutual responsibility without one side intruding into the exclusive domain of the other". The document quotes Novella 6 and the *Epanagoge* and admits that symphonia "did not exist in Byzantium in an absolutely pure form". It names caesaropapist emperors (inherited from the pagan *pontifex maximus*), Ivan the Terrible, Alexis against Nikon, and the Synodal period's "Protestant doctrine of territory and established church".
- **Other models.** It rejects the Western "two swords", where both powers go back to the Pope, and Protestant *cuius regio, eius religio*. It notes that American separation sprang from a plurality of churches, while French separation sprang from anti-Christian ideology.
- **Freedom of conscience** is presented as a sign that "society has lost religious goals and values and become massively apostate". All the same, it is "one of the means of the Church's existence in the non-religious world".
- **Forms of government (III.7).** Under the Judges, rule was by authority, which needs strong faith. Under the kings, rule was by coercion: "weakening faith… caused the need to replace the King Invisible by the king visible". Modern democracies "do not seek the divine sanction of power". Imposing a more religious form on a society that is not spiritually ready would be "falsehood and hypocrisy". So **"the Church does not give preference to any social system or any of the existing political doctrines"** (1994 Council).
- **Limits of obedience (III.5, IV.9).** If the state forces apostasy or grave sin, "the Church should refuse to obey". The steps: first dialogue, then use of democratic mechanisms, then appeal to international bodies, then "peaceful civil disobedience".

**Law and rights (IV)**
- Rights grow from man as **image of God**. St Antony: princes "have power over your body alone, not over your soul".
- Secularism turned them into "the protection of self-will", rights of "a self-sufficient and self-sufficing subject". Positive law then validates anything, even abortion, if society approves.

**Politics (V)**
- Clergy may not stand for office or campaign. St Tikhon (1919): they "should be above and outside any political interests". The Synod of 1993 made it a defrocking offence.
- Laity may enter politics and found Christian parties, but may not speak *for* the Church.
- A politician needs "special self-sacrifice": most decisions "benefit only a part of society" and are "stained with sin or connivance with sin". Chrysostom: "A true king is he who conquers anger and jealousy and voluptuousness."

**War (VIII)**
- "War is evil", a sign of "fratricidal hatred" since Cain. Yet the Church does not forbid fighting "if at stake is the security of their neighbours".
- **St Cyril (Constantine) among the Saracens.** Asked why Christians fight when Christ said to turn the other cheek, he answered that Christians bear injuries privately but "in company… give our lives in battle for our neighbours" (John 15:13).
- The Western just-war criteria are listed, and the method of war is a test: how a side treats prisoners and civilians.
- **The icon of St George**: the white horse never touches the black dragon's colour. "In struggling with sin it is important to avoid sharing in it." What is condemned is "malice in the human heart", not force as such. Tolstoyan non-resistance is "resolutely" rejected.

**Death penalty (IX)**: not forbidden by the New Testament or Tradition, but the Church has always interceded for the condemned.

## 4. Answer Two: Moss, *A Monarchist Theology of Politics*

Vladimir Moss writes from the Russian "True Orthodox" tradition, which broke with the Moscow Patriarchate over its accommodation with the Soviets.

**Against liberal democracy (Part I)**
- He reads **Fukuyama's** *End of History* through Plato's reason, desire and *thymos* (the desire for recognition). Hobbes and Locke built on desire. Hegel built on *isothymia*, equal recognition.
- So **"democracy rests on the twin pillars of greed and pride"**: "If ever a house were built on sand".
- Democracies do not prevent tyranny. Athens killed Socrates and butchered Melos, and Cromwell, Napoleon, Lenin and Hitler all rose on revolutions. Democracy must dissolve both **nations** and **religions with absolute truths**. **Ecumenism** "is, in essence, the application of the principles of liberal democracy to religious belief".
- Its endpoint is **Dostoevsky's underground man**, the will that wants only itself, and so **nihilism**. See [[The Underground Man and the Death of God: Dostoevsky, Shestov, Girard]].

**Orthodox autocracy (Part II)**
- **Anthropology.** Man is not reason, desire and thymos but the image of God, called to deification (he quotes Lossky). Building on fallen nature as if it were the norm produces "subhumanism".
- **Israel.** Theocracy under the Judges fell into anarchy ("every man did that which was right in his own eyes"). Asking for a king "like all the nations" was apostasy. From Saul on, the people of God split into **Church** (sacred) and **State** (profane). Melchizedek, priest and king, prefigures Christ. Otherwise the two offices must stay in different persons.
- **New Rome.** He defends Byzantium against the charge of caesaropapism. Anointing gave the emperor a real rank "in and for the Church", as first layman and defender of the faith. Marcian and Pulcheria rescued Orthodoxy at Chalcedon when bishops failed.
- **St Theodore the Studite** argues that monarchy follows the divine *monarchia*: "one patriarch… one bishop… one abbot… one king". "A multiplicity of wills destroys everything."
- **Third Rome.** He judges Russia better than Byzantium. Its long humility under Constantinople and its rejection of Florence made it the true heir. Byzantium fell because its symphony shrank to Greek nationalism and then to union with Rome.
- **The restrainer.** When lawful monarchy falls, "a period of democracy and anarchy" prepares for the Antichrist (2 Thess 2:7). Since 1917, "the True Church… has fled into the wilderness" (Rev 12).
- **What power is of God? (Appendix IV).** Romans 13 calls for **conditional** obedience, to a ruler who is "not a terror to good works". St Isidore of Pelusium: a tyrant who seizes power is not "established" by God but "allowed", as a chastisement. Soviet power was therefore an **"anti-authority"**. Patriarch Tikhon anathematised the Bolsheviks in 1918. St Basil prayed for Julian the Apostate's defeat.
- **The cure** is not a restored liberal Russia but repentance and **restoration of the Orthodox monarchy**. Until then, only a "provisional" government can be legitimate.

**A warning about this book.** The later appendix slides into an antisemitic conspiracy theory. It claims the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* are authentic and treats Bolshevism and Zionism as two horns of one beast. That is false. The *Protocols* were proved a plagiarised forgery (by *The Times* in 1921 and the Bern trial of 1934–35). The theory is also incompatible with the Gospel ("there is neither Jew nor Greek", Gal 3:28), and the Church condemned the kindred sin of phyletism in 1872. The theological core of the book can be argued with on its merits. This part has to be rejected outright.

## 5. Answer Three: Papanikolaou, *The Mystical as Political*

**The thesis.** Theosis, which he prefers to call **divine-human communion**, is a political principle: "the mystical is the political". Followed through consistently, it leads to an "unequivocal" Orthodox endorsement of liberal democracy, meaning freedom of religion, human rights and church-state separation, "minus the anthropological baggage of modern liberalism".

**The diagnosis**
- Post-Communist Orthodoxy is **postcolonial**. Having never felt inferior to the Turks, it took "the West" as its defining Other.
- Yannaras traces Nietzsche's nihilism back to Augustine's rejection of the essence–energies distinction. Papanikolaou calls this "a distorted apophaticism": Orthodoxy defined as "what the West was not".
- The pattern across Russia, Romania, Greece and Serbia is the same: half-hearted support for democracy combined with a push for cultural hegemony. He cites the Russian law of 1997 restricting new religions. An American dean summed it up: "I cannot imagine my life in any other society… [but] I cannot imagine a way of life more insidious to Christian Orthodoxy."

**Chapter 2, Eucharist or democracy?**
- **Harakas** reinterpreted symphonia for America: one God, independence of both, one *common good* in place of one faith, the people in place of the emperor.
- **Guroian**, drawing on Zizioulas' eucharistic ecclesiology, answered that this is accommodation. Separation of Church and state implies the world "does not need the Church or God". **Cavanaugh** goes further: the Eucharist is itself a politics, and the modern state lives by torture.
- Papanikolaou's immanent critique turns their ecclesiology against them:
  1. The Church grows only by **persuasion**, never coercion. So until all are persuaded there must be a political community distinct from it, and diverse.
  2. A common good (laws against murder, not laws about fasting) is "included in, though not exhaustive of" Christian morality.
  3. Their position is sectarian in effect. It parochialises communion into small enclaves, "a Gnostic either/or logic".
  4. **Eucharistic ecclesiology must be tempered by the ascetic tradition.** The Liturgy "simply does not appear to be realized in all its fullness in the eucharistic gatherings of local parishes". Christians have to *learn* to love (Maximus: "Blessed is the man who has learnt to love all men equally"). "One cannot rely on Christians being Christian." So we need a polity that guards against Christian slaveholding, persecution and contempt.

**Chapter 3, personhood and rights**
- He surveys the anti-rights voices: Yannaras, Guroian, the ROC's *Basic Teaching on Human Dignity, Freedom and Rights* (2008), which ties dignity to morality, and Metropolitan Hilarion. The pro-rights voices are McGuckin (rights language in the canons and Justinian's code) and **Archbishop Anastasios of Albania**: rights are "a starting point", and the churches should become "laboratories of selfless love".
- He traces the Orthodox theology of personhood from Lossky (irreducibility, freedom from nature) through Yannaras (*ecstasis* as eros) to Zizioulas ("otherness in communion"). Rights language falls *short* of personhood, since it is needed only "out of fear" that we have not learned to love. But it **mirrors** a sacramental community by treating each person as unique.
- So the ROC's claim to privilege is "not consistent" with theosis. God's dignity-giving love is not earned by morality: "God loves us even when we are immoral."

**Chapter 4, Hauerwas, Milbank and the common good**
- Milbank's "deified democracy" is really the Church. Since not all will be persuaded, it needs coercion. Milbank himself cites Dostoevsky's "monasticization of all society".
- Ward, Gregory and Mathewes rightly turn to Christian practices, but they forget that Christians often practise **anger, fear and hatred**. Much Christian political writing "looks more like a battle plan for war and destruction". He calls it "unascetical".
- He proposes a **non-natural-law common good**: revisable, debated, a form of transcendence even for secularists.

**Chapter 5, truth-telling and forgiveness**
- Confession should be understood less as a juridical contract and more as **truth-telling** before a listener who is an icon. He draws on Marion, Rowan Williams and Dionysius.
- Forgiveness is an event of communion, not a debt cancelled. He discusses Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission.
- **Free speech** shapes relationships. Under the Stasi and KGB, every listener "symbolizes the terror of the state".

**The conclusion.** Politics is "one of the deserts" in which the Christian learns to love the stranger. Real Christian politics "risks losing the debate, but waits patiently".

## 7. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What all three get right**
- Every Orthodox answer starts from the same axiom: **creation is made for communion with God**, so no sphere, politics included, is neutral. That rules out both a Manichaean flight from the world (*Basis* I.3) and a privatised faith. "The earth is the Lord's, and the fulness thereof" (Ps 24:1).
- All three also agree that **the state is post-lapsarian and provisional**. Chrysostom's logic is the decisive one. The state exists because "the law is not made for a righteous man, but for the lawless" (1 Tim 1:9), and perfect love casts out the fear that coercion runs on. No regime is the Kingdom. "My kingdom is not of this world" judges tsars and parliaments alike.

**Where symphonia stands**
- Symphonia is a real and venerable ideal, the Incarnation's pattern applied to society: two distinct authorities, "without confusion, without separation", as soul and body. But it was always conditional on an Orthodox *people* and an Orthodox *ruler*. The *Basis* says so frankly, and so does 1 Sam 8: a more sacred form of government imposed on an unbelieving society is "falsehood and hypocrisy". That is the strongest sentence in any of the three sources.
- History also counsels humility. The worst wounds to the Church came from Orthodox rulers: iconoclast emperors, Peter's Synod, the Florentine union pushed by emperors for military aid. The Church's saints were most often the ones *standing against* the throne (Chrysostom, Maximus, Theodore the Studite, Philip of Moscow).

**On Moss**
- His reading of Romans 13 (conditional obedience; a usurping tyranny is "allowed", not "ordained") is sound and patristic. So is his insistence that democracy as a *theory of where authority comes from* (the people, not God) cannot be Christian. Even an elected ruler holds power "from above" (John 19:11).
- His defence of Byzantium against the Western caricature of caesaropapism is fair. But the step to "the restoration of the Orthodox monarchy" as the first prayer of every believer goes beyond the Church's mind. The Church prays for "our civil authorities", whoever they are. And Christ, not a tsar, is the one who restrains evil in the end (Rev 19). His conspiracy material, as noted, is simply false.

**On Papanikolaou**
- His best point is **ascetic**. Political anger "justified in the name of truth" is a passion, and Maximus would call it so. "The wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God" (Jas 1:20). His reminder that "one cannot rely on Christians being Christian" is very Orthodox: it is the realism of the *Ladder*. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- His weakness is the opposite of Moss's. He makes a contingent arrangement, liberal democracy, into an "unequivocal" consequence of theosis. The *Basis* is wiser: the Church "does not give preference to any social system". The Church survived and sanctified under Diocletian, Justinian, the Sultan and Stalin. Her freedom is finally not granted by any constitution. It is the freedom of the martyrs.
- He also tends to treat the freedom to reject God as a positive good to maximise. Orthodoxy can defend that freedom as **God's own respect for the will** (God "will have all men to be saved", 1 Tim 2:4, but does not force them). It need not celebrate it.

**My conclusion**
- The *Basis* is the most balanced of the three. It holds symphonia as the ideal where an Orthodox people exists. It accepts the secular state where one does not. It forbids clergy partisanship. It sets the limit of obedience at apostasy. And it judges politics by the inner state of the heart (the St George icon) more than by forms.
- The real Orthodox political theology is less a constitution than a **way of being in any city**: pray for rulers, obey where you can, refuse where you must, and never let the struggle against evil make you share in it.
