**Vatican I (1870)**, in contrast to the idea of **first among equals**, declared the **Pope's supremacy and infallibility** in matters of faith and morals. The council taught that the Pope has **full and supreme jurisdiction** over the universal Church, not just a primacy of honor.

The key statement from **Vatican I's "Pastor Aeternus"**:

> **"We teach and define that it is a dogma divinely revealed: that the Roman Pontiff, when he speaks ex cathedra, that is, when, in discharge of the office of pastor and teacher of all Christians... is possessed of that infallibility with which the divine Redeemer willed His Church to be endowed."**

This marks a clear shift from the collegial structure implied by **first among equals** to a centralized, supreme authority vested in the Pope alone.

## Supremacy vs Infallibility

Two different claims in *Pastor Aeternus*:
- **ch. 3, universal jurisdiction**: the pope has "full and supreme power of jurisdiction over the whole Church", "ordinary and immediate" over every church and every member. Bishops owe "hierarchical subordination and true obedience".
- **ch. 4, infallibility**: see [[Vatican I's View of Papal Infallibility]].

The **jurisdiction** claim is what Orthodox see as the real break: it makes every diocese answerable directly to Rome.

## The First Millennium Canons

| Canon | Says | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Apostolic Canon 34** | the bishops of each nation must recognise **the first among them** as head and do nothing important without him, and he nothing without all of them | primacy is **regional** and **conciliar** |
| **Nicaea 6** (325) | Alexandria's authority over Egypt, "since the like is customary for the bishop of Rome"; also Antioch | Rome's authority is a **regional custom**, parallel to others. See [[Nicene Creed Canon 6: First Among Equals Explanation]] |
| **Constantinople 3** (381) | the bishop of Constantinople has "primacy of honour after the bishop of Rome, **because it is New Rome**" | honour based on the **city**, not Peter |
| **Chalcedon 28** (451) | the fathers gave privileges to Old Rome "**because it was the imperial city**" and equal privileges to New Rome | same; Rome objected |
| **Sardica** (343) canons 3–5 | a deposed bishop may appeal to Rome, which can order a rehearing | a real Roman role, but as court of **appeal**, not ordinary jurisdiction; and Sardica wasn't ecumenical |

## Rome's Primacy in History

- **Rome's prestige** in the early Church was real: the church of Peter and Paul, the martyrs' church, the imperial capital, famous for orthodoxy (Irenaeus: "every church must agree with this church because of its more powerful pre-eminence", *Against Heresies* 3.3.2: much debated; Siecienski).
- But in the East, Rome's role was **primacy within communion**: honoured, consulted, sometimes appealed to, never governing (Siecienski).
- Popes like **Leo I** and **Gregory the Great** made strong claims. But Gregory himself rejected the title "ecumenical patriarch" for Constantinople: "whoever calls himself universal bishop is the forerunner of Antichrist" (*Letters* 5.18; Siecienski).
- The **Gregorian reform** (11th c.) and *Dictatus Papae* (1075) turned primacy into monarchy; the **Fourth Crusade** (1204) made it a sore point in the East (Siecienski).
- **Florence** (1439): the pope's primacy was defined with the clause "**as is contained in the acts of the ecumenical councils and the holy canons**". The East understood that as a limit; the West later dropped it (Siecienski).

## What the Orthodox Would Accept

- A **primacy of honour**, and even a real role as **first** in a conciliar Church: convening, presiding, appeals. Ravenna (2007) Orthodox–Catholic document: primacy exists at local, regional and universal levels, always with synodality.
- Not universal immediate jurisdiction or personal infallibility.
