title: St. Maximus on the Two Wills in Christ

---

St. Maximus the Confessor (c. 580–662 AD) played a pivotal role in opposing the Monothelite heresy, which proposed that Christ had only one will, the divine will, instead of two distinct wills—one divine and one human. The Monothelites argued that Christ’s humanity was completely absorbed into His divinity, leading to the idea that Christ's human will did not exist independently. St. Maximus, however, defended the orthodox position that Christ, as fully God and fully man, must have both a divine will and a human will.

Maximus argued that Christ's two wills were not in conflict, but rather, His human will was perfectly aligned with His divine will. The human will of Christ did not act contrary to the divine will, but it remained fully human—capable of choice, yet always in complete submission to the divine will. This teaching was essential to affirming the true humanity of Christ, as a human will was necessary for Christ to experience temptation, suffering, and the fullness of human life, yet without sin.

This theological position was formally recognized and ratified by the Sixth Ecumenical Council (680-681 AD), which condemned Monothelitism and affirmed that Christ possessed two wills and two natures. The council's definition safeguarded the fullness of Christ's humanity and divinity, rejecting any notion that Christ's human will was overshadowed or absorbed by His divine will.

Maximus’ defense of the two wills was central to maintaining the reality of Christ's human experience and His redemptive role. It became a cornerstone of Orthodox Christology, ensuring that Christ was truly human and truly divine, and that His redemptive work was complete and effective for humanity. Through this distinction, Maximus preserved the integrity of Christ’s person and work, which remains a crucial element of Orthodox theology to this day.
