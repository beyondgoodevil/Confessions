*Bacchae* was written in Macedon at the end of Euripides' life and staged after his death (405 BC). It is the only surviving tragedy with Dionysus, the god of the theatre, as its main character. Everyone who has written about Dionysus has had to deal with it: Nietzsche, Rohde, Dodds, Otto, Girard. And no one agrees what it means.

## 1. The Play

**Prologue**: Dionysus, son of Zeus and the Theban princess **Semele**, returns from Asia disguised as a human stranger, a long-haired, effeminate, smiling "Lydian". His mother's sisters said Semele lied about Zeus and that the lightning killed her as punishment. So he has driven all the women of Thebes mad and sent them to the mountain as maenads. He means to prove to the city, and to its young king **Pentheus** (Semele's nephew), that he is a god.

**The scenes:**
1. **Two old men**, Cadmus (the founder) and Teiresias (the prophet), dress as bacchants to go dancing.
   - Teiresias defends the god with sophisticated arguments: Demeter is the dry, Dionysus the wet, wine is his gift; the "thigh" story is a pun.
   - Pentheus mocks them. Cadmus: even if he isn't a god, "say that he is"; it's good for the family.
2. **The god and the king.** Pentheus arrests the Stranger, cuts his hair, takes his thyrsus, and jails him.
   - "Who is your god?" "You cannot see him." "Where is he?" "Here, where I am."
   - The **palace miracle**: earthquake, Semele's tomb flares up, the palace falls, and the Stranger walks out calm.
3. **The first messenger**: on Cithaeron the maenads are peaceful at first. They suckle fawns and wolf-cubs, strike the rock and wine flows. Disturbed by herdsmen, they **tear cattle apart** with bare hands, raid villages, carry off children, and wounds don't hurt them.
4. **The turn.** Pentheus calls out the army. The Stranger asks: "Would you like to see them on the mountain?" Pentheus agrees. The god's hold takes him: he will dress as a woman to spy.
   - Dionysus to Pentheus: "Do not kick against the goads, a man against a god" (795).
5. **The dressing scene.** Pentheus comes out in women's clothes, fussing over his curls and how to hold the thyrsus. He sees two suns and the Stranger as a bull. Dionysus will lead him "into his mother's arms".
6. **The second messenger.** The god sets Pentheus on top of a pine tree, then calls out to the women. They pull down the tree. His mother **Agave**, foaming and rolling her eyes, takes him for a lion. "Mother, it's me!" She tears off his arm, and they rip him apart (*sparagmos*).
7. **Agave's return.** She enters carrying her son's head on her thyrsus, proud of her "lion cub", calling the chorus to "share the feast". **Cadmus** brings her back to sanity by questions: "Look at the sky... whose head is this?"
8. **The epiphany.** Dionysus appears as god and pronounces sentence: exile for Agave; Cadmus and his wife will be turned into snakes. Cadmus: "Gods should not be like mortals in their anger." Dionysus: "Zeus my father willed it long ago."

## 2. Was Euripides For or Against Dionysus?

The oldest debate, and it shapes everything else.

- **The rationalist reading** (Verrall, early 20th century): Euripides the sceptic shows a cult's "miracles" on bad evidence. The palace never really falls; the messengers are deluded. Winnington-Ingram's appendix answers it: "This is the conclusive argument against Verrall... to all but prosaic minds" the poetry won't support it.
- **The "palinode" or conversion reading**: the old sceptic repented and wrote a hymn to the god. Nietzsche half believed this (§3).
- **Winnington-Ingram, *Euripides and Dionysus* (1948)** is the classic middle reading:
  - Dionysus is **both** "most terrible, yet most kindly to men" (859–61), the "peace and terror of Dionysiac religion": "the peace comes first, the terror afterwards, and it is the terror that is left uppermost in our minds."
  - The god "as a person" is treated like other Euripidean gods, "as odious at the end of the play as he was enigmatic at the beginning".
  - But the play isn't atheist propaganda. As a symbol, Dionysus stands for "the impersonal force, which is beyond good and evil": nature, instinct, the crowd.
  - **The chorus's wisdom**: "what the mass of ordinary men believes and practises, that would I accept" (430–3). It is the wisdom of the crowd, which will turn bloodthirsty.
  - **Pentheus**: "unrealised sexual preoccupation, of dark forces working in the dark". What the god does is release what was repressed.
  - "Euripides recognized but hated Dionysus" (as Hinden quotes him). Or rather: he recognized his power and refused to worship it.
- **Looking at Bacchae** (Stuttard and others, 2016) shows the range today:
  - a **revenge play** (Dionysus avenging his mother's honour, like Medea);
  - **"horrid laughter"** in the transvestite scene;
  - a **political** play about new religion and old;
  - a **mystery** play (Seaford).

## 3. Nietzsche's Problem

- *Birth of Tragedy* §12: Euripides, the poet of "aesthetic Socratism", "banished" Dionysus from tragedy. "Sacrilegious Euripides... because you had abandoned Dionysus, Apollo abandoned you." See [[Apollo and Dionysus: The Birth of Tragedy]].
- But then there is *Bacchae*, the most Dionysian play we have. Nietzsche's answer: Euripides, "who opposed Dionysus with heroic valour throughout a long life... finally ended his career with a glorification of his adversary". His last play is a late recantation, though by then it was too late for tragedy.

**Hinden's argument** ("Nietzsche's Quarrel with Euripides", 1981):
- *Bacchae* is actually the play that "most fully bears out Nietzsche's argument against Euripides, although (embarrassing to say) Nietzsche himself somehow seems to have been fooled by it". It is "a critical dialectic on the nature of religious ecstasy... [it] assesses the horrors and attractions of Dionysian experience".
- **Pentheus has no mask.** He isn't raised to tragic grandeur by the god but "reduced in stature to a neurotic adolescent, parting the bushes with obscene delight to spy upon his mother". His death is "grotesque", and so the god's "victory" is "ironically diminished".
- Cadmus's protest that "gods should be exempt from human passion", and the mechanical epiphany, make Dionysus "the butt of a satiric attack similar to that which the Olympians received in other of Euripides' plays".
- So Euripides separates the Dionysian–Apollonian unity into psychological forces set against each other. That is what Nietzsche says Socratism does.
- **Why Nietzsche needed Euripides as an enemy**: "Euripides the passionate doubter, the ironic tragedian, was to his models Sophocles and Aeschylus what Nietzsche was to Schopenhauer, Hegel, Kant." The quarrel was "partly an internal one": Nietzsche "had caught a glimpse of something growing in himself: an enormous doubt eating with irony not only mysticism but everything in its path".
- Nietzsche sought the Dionysian ecstasy; "the Greeks themselves held the experience in dread".

## 4. Dodds and Seaford: The Rites Are Real

- **Dodds** (commentary 1944/1960; *Greeks and the Irrational* appendix I): maenadism was a **real** phenomenon, not poetic invention:
  - Diodorus on companies of women every second year;
  - Plutarch on maenads rescued from Parnassus in a snowstorm;
  - the head tossed back, flute and drum, handling snakes, *sparagmos* and *omophagia*.

  The *Bacchae* is "the only surviving Dionysiac passion-play". Dodds suggests Euripides may also have had in mind the cult of Sabazius, then entering Athens.
- **Seaford** (*Dionysos*; and in *Looking at Bacchae*): the play is full of allusions to **mystery initiation**.
  - The parodos: "Blessed is he who... knows the rites of the gods... and **joins his soul to the thiasos**."
  - **The palace scene follows the pattern of initiation**: darkness and desolation, the chorus fallen to the ground "in isolated desolation", earthquake and lightning, then the god appearing in light, and joy.
  - **Pentheus' dressing** is the transvestism of initiation, which detaches the initiand from his old identity. His linen robe is the burial cloth "of those called Orphics and Bacchics" (Herodotus 2.81). And "the killing of Pentheus has to be real rather than merely ritually enacted", because he refused the symbolic death.
  - **Theatre itself** came from "the confluence of Dionysiac mystery-cult with... hero-cult". Fundamental to it is "radical transformation of identity": the mask.
  - **Dionysos and money**: tragedy arose with coinage. Its central figure is the **tyrant**, "isolated from the gods and even from his own kin, obsessed with money". Pentheus is a tyrant, Dionysus the communal god who "gave the pain-removing delight of wine equally to the wealthy man and to the lesser man" (421–3).

## 5. Otto: The God Who Is Mad

Walter F. Otto, *Dionysus: Myth and Cult* (1933), is the Romantic, Nietzschean reading by a classicist.
- Dionysus is "**the god who comes**". His myths are myths of **epiphany** and resistance (Pentheus, Lycurgus, the daughters of Minyas, Perseus), always arriving and always rejected.
- **The mask**: he is "the genuine mask god", the god of confrontation, of presence and absence at once, of "the eternal enigmas of duality and paradox".
- **Madness** isn't sickness but "a companion of life at its healthiest". "All intoxication arises from... the depths which have become fathomless because of death." He unites "the generating, nourishing, intoxicating rapture... and the tearing pain, the deathly pallor, the speechless night of having been."
- **"He is rent apart and devoured in the first flowering of his youth because he is himself the render and devourer of young life."** Victim and killer at once. That is Girard's point too, with the opposite valuation.
- Seaford places Otto as producing "an ahistorical vision of a thoroughly Greek Dionysos" built on "the spirit of duality", taking Nietzsche's contradiction further.

## 6. Girard: The Lynching Revealed

*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 5:
- The bacchanal is a **sacrificial crisis** staged on purpose. It perpetuates "an essential aspect of the sacrificial crisis: the destruction of differences":
  - male and female (Pentheus in a dress; women as warriors);
  - man and beast (maenads suckling fawns; Pentheus seen as lion, Dionysus as bull);
  - god and man (the god disguised as a man; Pentheus "made like a god").
- **The monstrous double.** Pentheus sees "two suns" and the Stranger as a bull. Each antagonist sees the other as a monster. Dionysus' "You see what you ought to see" is the moment of the double. The final victim is "the thousand-headed dragon" of the chorus.
- **The unanimous lynching**: the whole band of women, the mother first, against one victim. "The murder thus appears as the outcome of a divine plan and, at the same time, the result of a spontaneous outburst."
- **Dionysus** is the god who "prepares from afar the details of the first sacrifice, his own sacrificial enterprise". He is both instigator and, in other myths (the Titans), victim. See [[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]].
- **The women** may be a mythic displacement: "an effort to exonerate from the accusation of violence... adult males".
- **Euripides comes close to revealing it**: "the tragic demystification discloses a bacchanal that is pure frenzy, naked violence". The play's oscillation between "audacity" and "timidity" is the poet half-seeing what his tradition hides.

## 7. Dionysus and Christ: The Afterlife of the Play

Seaford, *Dionysos* ch. 9:
- **Dionysus was Christianity's most widespread rival.** Son of the ruler of the gods and a mortal mother, appearing among men, killed and restored to life, god of the vine. The Jews were pressed by Antiochus IV to walk in ivy wreaths for Dionysus (2 Macc 6:7). Herod's Temple had a great golden vine at its entrance. Tacitus says some thought the Jews worshipped Liber Pater.
- **Cana and "I am the true vine"** (John 2; 15:1). Opinion is divided. Seaford takes a middle view: the Gospel answers the challenge of the Dionysian wine-miracles. A Tyrian story has Dionysus give a shepherd wine and say, "this is water of harvest, this is blood of the grape".
- **Acts and the Bacchae.** The parallels are "too numerous to be coincidental":
  - **the prison at Philippi** (Acts 16:25–30): missionaries of a new god jailed, singing at midnight, earthquake, doors open, chains fall (as at *Bacchae* 447–8); the jailer seizes a sword, calls for light, falls trembling, and **is converted**, where Pentheus with his sword is not;
  - **Saul on the road to Damascus**: sudden light like lightning, falling to the ground, a voice heard but the god not seen, the command to rise;
  - and the line itself: "**It is hard for thee to kick against the pricks**" (Acts 26:14). It is found nowhere else in the New Testament, and is Dionysus' warning to Pentheus (*Bacchae* 795). Pentheus and Saul are both told not to resist the god whose new cult they persecute. One refuses and is torn apart; the other is converted.
- **Paul's "through a glass, darkly"** (1 Cor 13:12, literally "through a mirror in a riddle") may echo mystery initiation, where mirrors and riddles came before the revelation.
- **Clement of Alexandria** (*Protrepticus* 12) addresses Pentheus directly: "**Throw off your headband! Throw off your fawnskin! Be sober! I will show you the Word and the mysteries of the Word...** If you wish, you too be initiated, and you will dance with angels around the unbegotten and imperishable and only true God." Christianity as the true mystery, Christ as the true vine. Clement also calls Christ "the great grape-cluster, the Word crushed for our sake".
- **Romanos the Melodist** (6th century): Mary to her son, "O my grapevine, may they not squeeze you out."
- **The *Christus Patiens*** (*Christ Suffering*, a Byzantine cento, perhaps 11th–12th century) gives the Mother of God's lament for Christ partly in lines taken from **Agave's lament over Pentheus**, which survive only there. The mother who held her son's torn body and the mother at the Cross.
- **The Council in Trullo** (691), canon 62, still had to forbid grape-treaders shouting the name of the "detested Dionysus".
- Early apologists (Justin) explain the parallels as **demonic imitation** in advance.

## 8. Comparison

| reader | Dionysus is | Pentheus is | the play is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nietzsche** | the primal unity, the truth beneath appearances | the individual who must be broken | the old Socratic poet's recantation |
| **Winnington-Ingram** | a real and terrible force, beyond good and evil, odious as a person | a repressed man destroyed by what he denies | a poet's clear-eyed refusal to worship power |
| **Hinden** | the target of irony | a pathetic adolescent, no tragic mask | the strongest case *for* Nietzsche's charge |
| **Dodds / Seaford** | the god of real ecstatic and mystery cult | the initiand who refuses initiation and dies for real | a mystery play and a civic drama |
| **Otto** | the god of madness, mask and duality, life and death in one | the resister of the god who comes | the deepest revelation of the Dionysian |
| **Girard** | the god of the founding lynching | the scapegoat of a staged crisis | a myth half-exposed by tragedy |

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What the play shows**
- **The crowd's religion.** The chorus's "what the mass of ordinary men believes" turns into "Go, hounds of madness!" (977). A religion of ecstasy and unanimity is one step from the lynch mob. Girard and Winnington-Ingram agree on this from different directions.
- **Repression is not the answer either.** Pentheus, the order-obsessed tyrant who despises what he secretly wants, is destroyed by it. Neither denial nor surrender to the irrational works. The Fathers know a third way: the passions are neither denied nor indulged but **transformed**, the irascible and desiring powers redirected to God. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- **The mother and the son.** Agave's slow recognition ("whose head is this?") is one of the most terrible scenes in literature. That Byzantium put her lament into the mouth of the Theotokos shows the Christian imagination taking pagan grief and placing it at the foot of the Cross. But the difference is total: Agave killed her son in ecstasy; Mary stands by while others kill hers, and he rises.

**Dionysus and Christ**
- **Parallels are real; derivation is another matter.** Seaford's evidence for the *Bacchae* echoes in Acts is good. The phrase "kick against the pricks" was proverbial (also in Aeschylus and Pindar), but the cluster is striking. Luke, writing for Greeks, tells Saul's conversion so that a Greek ear hears it as the *Bacchae* reversed: the persecutor who did not resist.
- **The decisive contrast** is Girard's and Clement's. Dionysus drives others into frenzy and lynches his resister. Christ is lynched and prays for his killers. Dionysus' wine dissolves the self into the crowd. Christ's cup is "the new testament in my blood" (Luke 22:20), and makes a body of persons, not a mob. "Be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit" (Eph 5:18). Pentecost was mistaken for drunkenness ("These men are full of new wine", Acts 2:13), and Peter's answer is to speak clearly and call to repentance, not frenzy.
- **The vine**: "I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman" (John 15:1). "True" here suggests that there are other, false vines.
