In May 1963 the Oxford classicist E. R. Dodds gave four lectures at Queen's University, Belfast, on religious experience in the Roman Empire between the accession of Marcus Aurelius (161) and the conversion of Constantine (312). Published in 1965 as *Pagan and Christian in an Age of Anxiety*, they became one of the most influential short books ever written on late antiquity. The title phrase was borrowed from W. H. Auden, who had applied it to the twentieth century, and Dodds meant the echo. His thesis is that pagans and Christians in this period shared far more than they knew: the same contempt for the world and the body, the same reliance on dreams and inspired voices, the same longing for union with God, and the same underlying guilt. He describes his own stance plainly. "As an agnostic I cannot share the standpoint of those who see the triumph of Christianity as the divine event to which the whole creation moved. But equally I cannot see it as the blotting out of the sunshine of Hellenism by what Proclus called 'the barbarian theosophy' ... I stand outside this particular battle, though not above it: I am interested less in the issues which separated the combatants than in the attitudes and experiences which bound them together" (p. 5). This note follows his four chapters in order and then sets three other scholars beside him: Jean Daniélou, who rejects Dodds's claim that Christian mysticism came from Plotinus; Werner Jaeger, who tells the same story as a meeting of two educational traditions; and David Bentley Hart, who reverses Dodds's picture of a Christianity that darkened a sunny pagan world. Dodds's earlier book is the subject of [[Dionysus, Orpheus and the Mysteries: Dodds and the Irrational]].

### 1. The Period and the Method

Dodds starts from two historians. Rostovtzeff had concluded that a change in people's outlook was "one of the most potent factors" in the decline of the Empire, and Nilsson that the study of late paganism had neglected "the spiritual soil from which these growths arose ... yet that is the heart of the matter" (pp. 1–2). Dodds therefore studies religious experience in William James's sense, "the feelings, acts and experiences of individual men in their solitude," not doctrines or rituals (p. 2).

The period he chooses was one of "barbarian invasions, bloody civil wars, recurrent epidemics, galloping inflation and extreme personal insecurity" (p. 4). But he insists that the mood came first. The Gnostic systems of Valentinus and Basilides were built "in the prosperous years of the Antonine peace," just as, in Jung's phrase, "long before 1933 there was already a faint smell of burning in the air" (p. 4). He also warns against his own method: "Strictly speaking, there are no periods in history, only in historians" (p. 3).

### 2. The World Devalued

The first lecture describes "the progressive withdrawal of divinity from the material world" (p. 37). Late antiquity inherited Aristotle's cosmos: the eternal heavens above the moon, and below it the earth, "the mere dregs and sediment of the universe" (p. 7). The moralists drew the lesson that human life was tiny and unreal. Marcus Aurelius, ruler of the world, calls human activity "smoke and nothingness," the clash of armies "the quarrel of puppies over a bone," and his own triumph over the Sarmatians the self-satisfaction of a spider that has caught a fly. Dodds insists this "is meant in deadly earnest" (p. 8). Plotinus calls men "fair and lovely living puppets" in God's play, and treats the sack of cities as a passing scene in the drama (p. 10). Augustine says that "this life is nothing but the comedy of the human race," and the pagan poet Palladas writes, "The world's a stage and life's a toy" (p. 11). Cyprian, a contemporary of Plotinus, gives the social reason:

> The world today speaks for itself: by the evidence of its decay it announces its dissolution. The farmers are vanishing from the countryside, commerce from the sea, soldiers from the camps ... (p. 12)

"To identify oneself with such a world ... must have demanded more courage than the average man possessed: better treat it as an illusion or a bad joke, and avoid heartbreak" (p. 12).

No Stoic, Aristotelian or orthodox Platonist could condemn the cosmos as a whole; for the *Timaeus* it was "a perceptible god." Where condemnation appears, Dodds traces it to a more radical dualism, found in three forms: Matter as an evil principle not made by God; Fate, enforced by planetary demons who guard the seven gates between man and God; and a personal evil ruler of the world (pp. 13–16). The Gnostics drew the conclusion that the world was made by an inferior god, either ignorant (Valentinus) or harsh (Marcion's God of the Old Testament). Dodds reads their myths psychologically. The Valentinian "Bythos," the primal Deep, is "what Augustine called the *abyssus humanae conscientiae* and ... what we now call the Unconscious"; and the split between a remote merciful Father and a stupid cruel Creator "seems to reflect a splitting of the individual father-image into its corresponding emotional components" (pp. 19–20).

The result was alienation. Christians called themselves "strangers and pilgrims," and the *Letter to Diognetus* says that "every foreign land is their country, and every country is foreign to them." Marcus calls life "a warfare and a sojourn in a strange land" (pp. 20–21). People began to ask what we are here for, "not in fact a question which happy men readily ask themselves" (p. 21). The pessimistic answer was a Fall: birth as punishment, or as the soul's own wilful choice (*tolma*). "Wise men do not celebrate their birthdays," and Plotinus refused to (p. 23). Dodds credits Plotinus with growing out of this view after his break with the Gnostics: in his late essays souls descend "neither deliberately nor at God's command" but by nature, "as a cow grows horns" (pp. 25–26).

**The turn inward and against the body.** Resentment of the world turned into resentment of the self: Seneca's *displicentia sui*. Marcus writes at twenty-five, "I do penance, I am cross with myself, I am sad and discontented, I feel starved," and later, "It is hard for a man to endure himself" (pp. 28–29). Others diverted the hatred onto the body. It was "clay and gore," "a filthy bag of excrement and urine"; "Plotinus appeared ashamed of having a body at all; St Anthony blushed every time he had to eat"; a Desert Father says of his body, "I am killing it because it is killing me" (pp. 29–30). Dodds catalogues the Christian extremes: marriage as "a competition in continence," the claim in the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* that only virgins rise again, Origen's self-castration, stylites, chains, competitive fasting ("I am deader than you") (pp. 32–34). He finds no adequate source in Greek tradition, the Old Testament or the teaching of Jesus. "For the fantastic value attached to virginity St Paul would appear to be mainly responsible," though he adds that 1 Corinthians 7 shows Paul less extreme than his readers (p. 35). His verdict is harsh: "a strong injection of fanatical rigorism had been absorbed into the Church's system. It lingered there like a slow poison, and (if an outsider can judge) has not yet been expelled from it" (p. 35). The chapter's thesis:

> contempt for the human condition and hatred of the body was a disease endemic in the entire culture of the period ... I incline to see the whole development less as an infection from an extraneous source than as an endogenous neurosis, an index of intense and widespread guilt-feelings. (pp. 35–36)

Dodds is fair enough to record the other side. Other-worldliness did not mean indifference. Marcus worked hard for human welfare; Plotinus turned his house into an orphanage and checked the wards' accounts; the Roman church in the mid-third century supported more than 1,500 "widows and poor persons"; and Julian complained, "These godless Galilaeans feed not only their own poor but others, while we neglect our own" (p. 27).

### 3. Dreams, Prophets and Daemons

The second lecture takes as its text Diotima's definition in the *Symposium*: the daemonic is "intermediate between God and mortal," for "God has no contact with man" except through it (p. 37). By the second century every pagan, Jew, Christian and Gnostic believed in such mediators, and "the 'daemonic man', who knew how to establish contact with them, was correspondingly esteemed" (p. 38). Dodds gives four case studies.

**Aelius Aristides** kept a "night book" of his dreams that grew to 300,000 lines, and wrote them up as the *Sacred Teachings*, "the first and only religious autobiography which the pagan world has left us" (p. 40). A brilliant orator struck down at twenty-six by what Dodds reads as psychosomatic illness (asthma, hypertension, insomnia), he spent years obeying the dream prescriptions of the healing god Asclepius: no hot baths for five years, running barefoot in winter, bathing in icy rivers. Dodds sees in these "the unending expiation of an unconscious guilt," the same impulse as Isiac penances and the self-torments of Christian ascetics (p. 42). Aristides believed two friends had died in his place. Yet "Brainsick he was, and in a not very pleasant way, yet his experience must be classed as religious." When Asclepius told him in a dream, "Thou art uniquely chosen," it restored meaning to his life; he took the name Theodorus and declared that "everything is nonsense compared with obedience to God." A dream in which his own statue turns into a statue of Asclepius Dodds reads as "the reconstruction of a broken personality which has found peace through self-identification with the image of an ideal Father" (pp. 43–45). Aristides was not unusual: Marcus thanks the gods for dreams that cured him of spitting blood, and Galen says dreams helped him save lives. It was a time when, in Eitrem's words, "daylight reality was ceasing to be trusted" (p. 45).

**Perpetua**, martyred at Carthage in 203 at twenty-two, left a prison diary of four dreams. Dodds distrusts the rest of the *Passio* but defends the diary as genuine: its plain style differs from the editor's, it seems to have been written in Greek, and its dreams have "the true dreamlike inconsequence." She climbs a dangerous ladder to a shepherd who gives her curds; she sees her dead brother Deinocrates suffering and then healed; she fights an Egyptian gladiator and finds she has become a man; the prize is the golden apples of the Hesperides. The pagan imagery is "entirely natural in the dreams of a quite recent convert" (pp. 47–52). Dodds calls the diary "a touching record of humanity and courage, quite free from the pathological self-importance of an Ignatius or an Aristides," and compares Sophie Scholl's dream on the night before the Nazis executed her: she falls into a crevasse, but the child she carries is saved. "Faith in a miraculous future is a harder thing in the twentieth century than it was in the third" (pp. 52–53).

**Peregrinus**, known from Lucian's hostile pamphlet, was suspected of killing his father, became a Christian prophet in Palestine, was imprisoned, gave away his estates, quarrelled with the Christians, turned Cynic, insulted the emperor, and burned himself alive at the Olympic Games of 165, after which his statue worked miracles. Dodds guesses that the charge of parricide explains both his defiance of authority and his drive to self-punishment, and notes his last words: "Spirits of my mother and my father, receive me with kindness" (pp. 59–63).

**Montanus**, around 172, spoke as the mouthpiece of a voice: "I am the Lord God Almighty dwelling at this moment within a man ... man is like a lyre, and I play upon him like the plectrum: while the human being sleeps, I am awake." Dodds stresses that Montanus no more claimed to be God than a modern medium claims to be Confucius (p. 64). The movement's message was disappointing ("the Holy Spirit seemed to say nothing of any religious or intellectual value," in S. L. Greenslade's phrase), chiefly that the New Jerusalem would descend at the Phrygian village of Pepuza. Yet it spread across the Empire, and the last Montanists burned themselves in their churches under Justinian (pp. 65–67). The bishops won, and Dodds draws a pointed conclusion:

> From the point of view of the hierarchy the Third Person of the Trinity had outlived his primitive function ... The old tradition of the inspired *prophetes* who spoke what came to him was replaced by the more convenient idea of a continuous divine guidance which was granted, without their noticing it, to the principal Church dignitaries. (pp. 67–68)

Prophecy went underground, to resurface in medieval millenarians and later evangelical revivals. John Wesley called Montanus "one of the holiest men in the second century" (p. 68).

### 4. Union with God

The third lecture turns to mysticism, which Dodds defines strictly, with Lalande, as "belief in the possibility of an intimate and direct union of the human spirit with the fundamental principle of being" (p. 70). He separates it from three things often confused with it. Philo's prophetic *ekstasis*, in which "the mind in us is banished from its house upon the coming of the divine spirit," is possession, "trance-mediumship," not union (pp. 71–72). The magical formula "I am Thou and Thou art I" is used in the papyri to gain power. And "divinisation," a man becoming "a god walking about in the flesh" (Clement), is in its strong form a permanent change of identity that Dodds connects with the experience of conversion and its sense that the slate has been wiped clean (pp. 74–78).

True mystical union in this period he finds clearly only in **Plotinus**, "the perfect type of the introvertive mystic," whose motto was "the sum of things is within us." Porphyry reports that Plotinus reached union four times in the six years they worked together; Porphyry himself once (p. 84). Dodds quotes his description:

> The soul sees God suddenly appearing within it, because there is nothing between: they are no longer two, but one; while the presence lasts, you cannot distinguish them. It is that union which earthly lovers imitate when they would be one flesh ... When in this state the soul would exchange its present condition for nothing in the world, though it were offered the kingdom of all the heavens: for this is the Good, and there is nothing better. (pp. 85–86)

Dodds thinks "it is recognisably the same psychological experience everywhere, however different the glosses that have been put upon it" (p. 86). What is distinctively Greek in Plotinus is the approach: "no breathing exercises, no navel-brooding," no ritual, but mathematics and dialectic, so that "mystical union is not a substitute for intellectual effort but its crown and goal," and moral discipline, for "without true virtue all talk of God is but words" (pp. 86–88). And the interpretation: union is a natural event, not a gift of grace, and the One "has no desire towards us" (p. 89). Dodds rejects R. C. Zaehner's claim that Christian mystics differ because in their case "God takes the first step": that, he suspects, is "a theological gloss" read back into the same experience (p. 90).

Among Christians of the period he finds talk of assimilation to God, but "I have not found in any Christian writer of the period a single explicit reference to the possibility of mystical union in this life" (p. 97). Origen is at most "a mystic *manqué*," who wrote, "Often, God is my witness, I have felt that the Bridegroom was approaching me ... then he suddenly vanished" (p. 98). The first true Christian mystic, Gregory of Nyssa, had read Plotinus, uses his images, and differs mainly in insisting on grace. Dodds concludes: "I think Gregory had enjoyed the same experience as Plotinus; but I think he also knew what Plotinus had said about it ... To that extent and in that sense Christian mysticism springs from a pagan source" (p. 99). The period as a whole, he ends, was "moving into a phase in which religion was to be coextensive with life, and the quest for God was to cast its shadow over all other human activities" (p. 101).

### 5. The Dialogue and Christianity's Victory

The last lecture follows the debate between pagans and Christians through three phases: the Apologists and Celsus (c. 178); a period of relative peace and intellectual growth from Origen's teaching (203) to his *Contra Celsum* (248); and the age of the Decian and Diocletianic persecutions, Porphyry's *Against the Christians* (c. 270) and Hierocles, who both wrote against Christianity and helped carry out the Great Persecution (pp. 103–109).

**Popular hatred.** The masses hated Christians as atheists, as a secret society suspected of incest and cannibalism, as disloyal subjects who would not burn incense to the emperor ("rather like refusing to stand up when the national anthem is played"), and as the cause of every disaster. Tertullian put it: "If the Tiber floods the town or the Nile fails to flood the fields, if the sky stands still or the earth moves, if famine, if plague, the first reaction is 'Christians to the lion!'" (pp. 110–114). Christians also divided families: "Like all creeds which claim the total allegiance of the individual—like communism, for example, in our own day—early Christianity was a powerful divisive force" (p. 115).

**The learned debate** was not monotheism against polytheism; Celsus was arguably the stricter monotheist, and both sides believed in subordinate spirits, which Christians simply called demons (pp. 116–118). Nor was it rigour against laxity, since the ethics were nearly the same; Celsus found Christian morals unoriginal, and Origen replied that Christian preachers "cook for the multitude" what Plato spiced for the gentry, so that Christianity seemed to him at times "Platonism for the many" (p. 120). The real dividing line was between *logismos* and *pistis*, reasoned conviction and faith. Galen thought Christians had courage, self-control and justice but lacked intellectual insight; Celsus heard them say, "Ask no questions: just believe"; Julian scoffed, "There is nothing in your philosophy beyond the one word 'Believe!'" (p. 121). But the sides crossed over. Origen came to treat mere faith as "useful for the multitude," while later Neoplatonism became "less a philosophy than a religion" in which faith was the first condition of salvation: "we grow like what we hate" (pp. 122–123). Porphyry, "the first man ... to apply the canons of historical criticism to the Bible," dated Daniel to the reign of Antiochus Epiphanes on historical grounds (pp. 126–127). On the Christian side, Origen's *De principiis* rewrote the faith in Platonic terms: an eternal soul that rises and falls through many lives, a hell that is "the penalty and torture of the soul's want of cohesion," and a heaven that is "an endless university" (pp. 127–130). Dodds thinks it "a major historical calamity" that Gregory of Nyssa's rejection of eternal punishment was not accepted, and notes that "It was not Origen but Augustine who determined the future pattern of Western Christianity" (pp. 131–132).

**Why Christianity won.** Paganism by the fourth century was "a kind of living corpse"; it "had lost faith both in science and in itself" (p. 132). Christianity, by contrast, "was judged to be worth living for because it was seen to be worth dying for" (p. 132). Dodds then gives four psychological reasons:

| Reason | Dodds's argument |
|---|---|
| Exclusiveness | "There were too many cults, too many mysteries ... you could pile one religious insurance on another, yet not feel safe. Christianity made a clean sweep. It lifted the burden of freedom from the shoulders of the individual" (p. 133). |
| Openness | It accepted slaves, the poor and ex-criminals, offered "an open career to talent," and did not demand education; it was "an army of the disinherited" (p. 134). |
| Hope | In an age of guilt it offered "a bigger stick and a juicier carrot": fear, but also "lively hope." Porphyry said only sick souls needed Christianity, but "sick souls were numerous in our period" (p. 135). |
| Community | It cared for widows, orphans, the old, the unemployed and the disabled, buried the poor and nursed the sick in plague; above all it gave "the sense of belonging" to the uprooted of the great cities (pp. 136–137). |

He ends: "Christians were in a more than formal sense 'members one of another': I think that was a major cause, perhaps the strongest single cause, of the spread of Christianity" (p. 138). Even Julian attributed Christianity's success to "their philanthropy towards strangers, their care for the burial of the dead" (p. 138 n.).

### 6. Daniélou: The Christian Mystic Behind the Platonic Words

Dodds's claim that Christian mysticism "springs from a pagan source" is aimed at a named opponent: Jean Daniélou, whose 1944 study of Gregory of Nyssa he cites and contradicts (p. 99). Daniélou's own case, now available in English, starts from the same observation Dodds makes: "On first reading the works of Gregory of Nyssa, we were struck above all by the considerable number of terms borrowed from the language of Plato, Philo, and Plotinus. A more in-depth reading, however, led us to discover under this veneer an experience and a doctrine that are authentically Christian" (Introduction). Words like *apatheia* and *theoria* keep their Greek form but change their content. His key example is purification. For Plotinus "the soul is divine by nature," and it need only wash off the mud of the body to see itself. For Gregory "the image of God is divine life, which is a gift of grace"; the soul must first be united to Christ, and then it perceives a life "which is not its own essence but a free gift from God" (Introduction). He goes so far as to say that "the Platonic structure is incidental. The same basic ideas could be expressed by borrowing the framework, say, of Buddhist techniques" (Introduction).

On *ekstasis* itself, the point where Dodds sees borrowing, Daniélou compares Philo, Plotinus and Gregory directly:

> this same psychological experience is interpreted differently by our three authors. For Plotinus, it entails a suppression of one's individuality and a fusion with the One; for Philo, it means expulsion of the *nous idios* [one's own mind] ... and possession ... of the soul by the divine Spirit; for Gregory, on the other hand, it is a suspension of the natural life that leads to a blissful contemplation of the divine reality. (ch. 8)

Notice that Daniélou concedes Dodds's central claim: it is "this same psychological experience." The dispute is over whether interpretation is a "gloss" on the experience, as Dodds holds, or part of what the experience is. Daniélou adds a theological point Dodds does not consider. Gregory wrote against Apollinarius, who denied that Christ had a human mind because two minds would mean two persons. Gregory's answer was that the Word could assume humanity without expelling its mind, and "mutatis mutandis, the same is true for ecstasy: the Spirit can inhabit the soul without expelling the nous from it. In both cases, it is a matter of deepening the concept of person" (ch. 8). Gregory's ecstasy is also tied to the Eucharist, the "sober drunkenness" of the cup, which Daniélou calls "one of the oldest pieces of evidence" for "the sacramental character of Christian mysticism" (ch. 8). And it is not solitary. Daniélou's conclusion stresses that in Gregory "the ascent of the soul is never presented as a solitary affair": Moses always leads his people, the mystic's graces have "an apostolic aim," and "nothing brings us closer to God than to imitate his love" (Conclusion). That is the opposite of Plotinus' solitary ascent to the One, and it connects Gregory's mysticism to the very thing Dodds names as the strongest cause of Christianity's success: the community.

### 7. Jaeger: Christianity as Paideia

Werner Jaeger's Harvard lectures, delivered in 1960, cover the same centuries from a different angle. Where Dodds looks for the shared psychology of pagans and Christians, Jaeger looks for the shared culture, Greek *paideia*, the education and literature through which Greeks formed human beings. "With the Greek language a whole world of concepts, categories of thought, inherited metaphors, and subtle connotations of meaning enters Christian thought" (p. 6). Even the word "conversion" comes from Plato (p. 10). An apocryphal Acts has the apostle Philip tell the Athenians, "I have come to Athens in order to reveal to you the paideia of Christ" (p. 12).

Jaeger agrees with Dodds that Greek philosophy had taken on a religious function and that its revival was due to Plato: "The reason for its survival as a whole was the fact that it possessed Plato; had it not been for him, the rest of Hellenic culture might have died along with the old Olympian gods" (pp. 44–45). But where Dodds sees Origen's Platonism as a bold rewriting that the Church later condemned, Jaeger sees it as the centre of a long, successful project. Origen's whole thought rests on "the Greek idea of paideia in its highest philosophical form" (p. 68): Christ is the divine teacher, history is God's education of mankind through both prophets and philosophers, and human free will is the precondition of education. Against the "deep pessimism" of the Gnostics and Manichaeans, "Plato stood like a rock with his conviction that the seed of the good is to be found in everything" (p. 64). That is a direct answer to Dodds's first chapter: the Platonism that the Christians took over was the anti-pessimist strand of their culture.

Jaeger also carries the story past Dodds's end date to the Cappadocians, who built "a whole Christian civilization" and produced "a true renaissance" in the fourth century (pp. 74–75). He reads Gregory of Nyssa not as a borrower but as a thinker who turned Plato's idea of education as formation (*morphosis*) into the Christian *imitatio Christi*: "The form is Christ" (p. 93). He notes, as Dodds does, that Gregory followed Origen in rejecting eternal punishment, and that Epiphanius said all Origen's errors came from his Greek *paideia* (p. 89). Without this "ancient Christian humanism," Jaeger ends, "how little of classical literature and culture would have survived!" (p. 102).

### 8. Hart: A Glorious Sadness

David Bentley Hart's *Atheist Delusions* is a polemic, not a study of the period, but its eleventh chapter makes the strongest case against one of Dodds's implications. Dodds is careful not to say Christianity caused the world-weariness of late antiquity, but his catalogue of Christian body-hatred and his "slow poison" leave a reader with that impression, and the popular picture Hart attacks (Nietzsche, Swinburne, Gore Vidal's *Julian*) says it outright: that Christianity darkened a sunny, sensual pagan world.

Hart argues the reverse. Pagan culture as a whole was marked by "a kind of omnipresent dejection," "a kind of glorious sadness": a closed cosmos governed by fate, sacrifices of appeasement, gods to be fed and feared (pp. 131–32). The late-antique world was "more than a little burdened by a sort of cosmic disenchantment," and its religions and philosophies "often encouraged a contempt for the flesh more absolute, bitterly unworldly, and pessimistic than anything found in even the most exorbitant forms of Christian asceticism." So "if the Christianity of the early centuries was marked by any excessive anxiety regarding the material world or life in the body, this was an attitude that had migrated from pagan culture into the church" (p. 134). The Gnostics, whom Hart treats as a "trans-religious theosophy" rather than an alternative Christianity, are the clearest symptom (pp. 135–41). Even Plotinus, who defended the beauty of the world against them, still taught that "this world, for all its beauty, must be fled" (p. 143).

Christianity, on Hart's account, entered this "twilit world of pervasive spiritual despondency" not as one more cult of escape but "as a religion of glad tidings" (p. 143). Bound to the goodness of creation and the bodily resurrection, it had to treat the body as "God's good creature, the proper home of the soul," and its ascetics disciplined the body to restore it, not to escape it (p. 144). He cites Robin Lane Fox: among second-century writers "it is the Christians who are most confident and assured." The gift Christianity brought, Hart concludes, was "a liberation from spiritual anxiety ... a deep and imperturbable joy" (pp. 144–45).

Hart and Dodds are not as far apart as their tones suggest. Both see the pessimism as older than Christianity and shared by pagans; Dodds himself says its "symptoms show themselves in a milder form in pagans of purely Hellenic education" and that its beginnings predate the third-century crisis (pp. 35–36). They differ in where they put the extremes. Dodds finds the worst cases among Christians and Gnostics; Hart finds the logic of world-denial in paganism and treats Christian excesses as an infection the Church never quite cured. Each can quote the evidence.

### 9. Assessment

Dodds's book has lasted because its central observation is true and was not obvious in 1963: pagans and Christians of the third century were far more alike in religious temperament than either side's later heirs liked to think. His evidence for a shared mood is strong, and his case studies are vivid and carefully judged. His reading of Perpetua's diary, in particular, is a model of sympathetic source criticism, and his account of the Montanist crisis explains in a few pages how prophecy gave way to the bishop.

His weaknesses are the ones his admirers noted at once. Henry Chadwick, in the foreword to the paperback, observed that "the unity of the book comes more from the author's mind than from the evidence presented," and that "another account of the same period might produce far more inconsistencies" (pp. ix–x). The "age of anxiety" is a modern frame, as Dodds admitted by borrowing it from Auden. And the psychoanalytic readings (Aristides' ideal Father, Peregrinus' parricide, the Gnostic split father-image) are suggestive but untestable. Dodds offers them with appropriate caution, but they carry much of the weight of his claim that the whole mood was "an endogenous neurosis."

On mysticism, Daniélou exposes the weak joint in Dodds's argument. Dodds and Daniélou agree that Plotinus and Gregory had the same experience. Dodds then treats everything else, grace versus nature, solitary versus communal, fusion versus participation, as interpretation added afterwards. But an experience of union that is understood as received from a loving God and shared with a people is plausibly not the same experience as one understood as a natural return to one's own divine essence. Dodds's own principle, that the experience is everywhere the same, is a philosophical assumption, not a finding. Daniélou's Apollinarian parallel gives a concrete reason why a Christian could take over Plotinus' vocabulary of ecstasy without taking over its meaning.

On why Christianity won, Dodds's four reasons, and especially the last, have held up well and been widely adopted. His emphasis on community anticipates later sociological studies of early Christian growth. What his account underplays is the content of the faith. He states that he "will not discuss the intrinsic merits of the Christian creed" (p. 133), which is fair for an agnostic historian, but it means his explanation is functional throughout: Christianity won because it met needs. Jaeger and Hart supply what Dodds leaves out. Jaeger shows Christianity winning the educated through argument and education. Hart argues that it won because its doctrine of a good creation and a risen body actually answered the despair Dodds describes, rather than merely sharing it. Dodds's own book half concedes this: the community he praises is the practical form of the love of neighbour that Daniélou finds at the summit of Gregory's mysticism.

### Conclusion

*Pagan and Christian in an Age of Anxiety* argues that between Marcus Aurelius and Constantine the whole Roman world, pagan and Christian alike, withdrew its trust from the visible world, turned to dreams, prophets and daemons, and sought union with a God beyond it, driven by a guilt that Dodds reads as a collective neurosis. Its portraits of Aristides, Perpetua, Peregrinus, Montanus and Plotinus remain among the best introductions to the religious life of the period, and its four reasons for Christianity's victory, ending with the sense of belonging, are still the starting point for discussion. Its limits are the limits of a single mind imposing unity on scattered evidence. Daniélou shows that shared words need not mean a shared faith; Jaeger shows Christianity winning through a culture as well as a mood; and Hart turns Dodds's picture round, arguing that the sadness belonged to paganism and that what Christianity offered the age of anxiety was joy.
