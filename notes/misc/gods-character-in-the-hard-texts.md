A second group of passages troubles readers less because of what God commands people to do than because of what they seem to say about God himself. In Isaiah he says "I create evil." In Exodus he hardens Pharaoh's heart and then punishes him for it, and he visits the sins of fathers on their children. In Genesis he "regrets" making humanity, as if he had not foreseen the result, and he tells Abraham to sacrifice his son. In Job he lets the Satan destroy a righteous man's family to settle an argument. Skeptics read these as signs of a God who is capricious, unjust or not all-knowing. This note takes each in turn, states the objection at its strongest, and sets out what the commentaries answer. The commentators come from several traditions: evangelical (Motyer, Murray, Stott, Hamilton, Steinmann, Andersen, Atkinson, Wright, Guthrie), Lutheran (Fretheim), Catholic (Cotter), critical (Hendel), Orthodox (Farley) and patristic (Chrysostom, Augustine, Salvian), and on several of these texts they disagree with one another. The companion note on God's commands to Israel is [[Violence and Divine Commands in the Old Testament: The Conquest, the Flood, the Firstborn, Psalm 137 and Judges]].

### 1. Isaiah 45:7: "I Make Peace, and Create Evil"

**The objection.** In the King James Version God says, "I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things." Skeptics read this as God admitting that he is the author of moral evil.

J. Alec Motyer answers first from the word. "The older translations made needless trouble by rendering 'I create evil'; the NIV correctly has create disaster." The Hebrew *raʿ* ranges "from a 'nasty' taste to full moral evil," and "Out of about 640 occurrences of the word ... there are 275 instances where 'trouble' or 'calamity' is the meaning. In every case the context must judge." Here the context is the conquests of Cyrus, a passage "full of historical calamities coming on people through Cyrus," so calamity is what *raʿ* means. The pairing is with "peace" (*šālôm*, well-being), not with goodness, and the light and darkness are "well established metaphors for the pleasant and the unpleasant." The verse is a "magnificent monergistic statement": "The Lord is executively behind all the diversities of experience which life contains." Motyer rejects the idea that the verse attacks Persian Zoroastrian dualism; if it attacks anything, it is "the inherent dualism of polytheism or the sinful dualism of the human heart," which wants one God but balks at saying with Job, "Shall we accept good from God, and not trouble [*raʿ*]?" (Job 2:10). Its purpose, he concludes, "is, however, not polemical but rather credal and comforting" (on 45:6d–7).

Matthieu Pageau will not let the verse off so lightly. He quotes it to show that "God is credited with creating both 'good' and 'bad.'" One could argue "that the first was created directly, while the second was only created indirectly, as a by-product or remainder," but he notes that in the Bible "negative or false events are often considered helpful and even necessary," and "it is often the case that two wrongs make a right," especially "when the second 'wrong' is an ironic cause of renewal and resurrection" (p. 142). For Pageau the verse is not about the origin of sin but about the pattern of judgment and renewal by which God governs a fallen world.

### 2. The Hardening of Pharaoh's Heart

**The objection.** God tells Moses, "I will harden Pharaoh's heart" (Exod. 4:21; 7:3), does so repeatedly, and then judges Egypt for Pharaoh's refusal. Paul draws the conclusion: "he hath mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth" (Rom. 9:18). If God makes a man refuse and then punishes him for refusing, he is unjust.

This is the passage where the commentators differ most, and their positions form a spectrum.

**Fretheim: both, with a limited determinism.** Terence Fretheim's excursus rejects both extremes, that "God has determined Pharaoh's behaviors" from the start, or that "Pharaoh's freedom of will is intact until the end." He argues for "a limited determinism of Pharaoh's behaviors that emerges in the course of the conflict." His evidence comes from the sequence of the text. Pharaoh is the subject of the hardening first; God first hardens his heart only at the sixth plague (9:12); Pharaoh is last the subject at the seventh (9:34–35); from then on God is the sole subject. The earlier divine statements (4:21; 7:3) "only promise future action." So "Each refusal makes it easier for Pharaoh to refuse the next time." He also points to the conditional "if you refuse" that God tells Moses to speak to Pharaoh (8:2; 9:2; 10:4). If refusal were already fixed, "to hold it out as a possibility is deceitful." His conclusion:

> Both need to be said: Pharaoh hardens his own heart, and so does God. (Excursus)

Fretheim refuses to exonerate God entirely: "God bears responsibility for keeping the word of God coming on and on. Hence no interpretive attempt should be made to get God off the hook." The hardening becomes part of the judgment itself. He compares it to "a boat on a fast-moving river, headed for a gorge or a waterfall," where human choices have narrowed history's possibilities "to a single one," and cites Psalm 81:12: "So I gave them over to their stubborn hearts."

**Murray: a positive, sovereign act.** John Murray, a Reformed scholar, rejects the softer reading that God merely "permitted" Pharaoh to harden himself. He grants that "There can be no question but Pharaoh hardened his own heart," but the text "preponderantly" says that the Lord hardened it, and in Romans 9:18 the parallel with mercy must be kept: "determinative will comes to effect in the act of hardening." So "a positive infliction on God's part is the only interpretation that fits." What keeps this from injustice is that hardening is "of a judicial character. It presupposes ill-desert." But Murray will not make Pharaoh's prior sin the reason God hardened him rather than someone else: all deserve hardening, so "Sovereignty pure and simple is the only reason for the differentiation by which some are consigned to hardening while others equally ill-deserving are made the vessels of mercy" (on 9:18).

**Stott: judicial abandonment.** John Stott, quoting Leon Morris, puts the emphasis on Pharaoh's prior choice: "Neither here nor anywhere else is God said to harden anyone who had not first hardened himself." God's hardening "was a judicial act, abandoning him to his own stubbornness," like his "giving them over" in Romans 1. Since all are guilty, "If therefore God hardens some, he is not being unjust, for that is what their sin deserves." And: "The wonder is not that some are saved and others not, but that anybody is saved at all" (on 9:17–19).

**Farley and Chrysostom: free choice first.** The Orthodox reading is the most insistent on human freedom. Lawrence Farley writes that "It is not the case that God hardens innocent hearts, overriding free will." God's hardening "only comes in response to man's prior sin": "God hardened Pharaoh into the attitude he had already steadfastly chosen" (p. 125). John Chrysostom, preaching on Romans 9, says the potter and clay illustration is used "not to do away with free-will," but to silence the presumption of calling God to account (*Homily 16 on Romans*, p. 833). Pharaoh "was a vessel of wrath, that is, a man who by his own hard-heartedness had kindled the wrath of God"; he was "fitted for destruction" in the sense of "fully fitted indeed, but by his own proper self." God "endured him with much long-suffering, being willing to bring him to repentance" (p. 834). And his answer to the whole question is short: "Whence then are some vessels of wrath, and some of mercy? Of their own free choice" (p. 835).

All five agree that Pharaoh hardened himself and that God's hardening was a judgment, not an arbitrary act on an innocent man. They divide on whether God's choice of whom to harden rests finally on his sovereign will (Murray) or on the person's prior free choice (Chrysostom, Farley), with Fretheim and Stott between.

### 3. "Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers upon the Children"

**The objection.** The second commandment says God punishes "the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me" (Exod. 20:5; Deut. 5:9). Yet Deuteronomy 24:16 says children shall not be put to death for their fathers, and Ezekiel 18 says "the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father." The Bible contradicts itself, and in the first text God punishes the innocent.

Christopher Wright answers by distinguishing two different things. The commandment states a fact about how sin works in families: "sin in one generation will afflict following generations in its consequences." Israelite households contained "three and often four generations," so a generation's apostasy "would detrimentally affect the succeeding ones, who would grow up without teaching and with an idolatrous example and environment" (on 5:9–10). Deuteronomy 24:16, by contrast, is a law for "human courts": "it is not for human courts to inflict specific punishment on those who are legally innocent." Quoting Driver, he concludes:

> it is one thing that, in virtue of the physical and social conditions in which they live, children should suffer for their fathers' sins; it is another that, by the deliberate intervention of human authority they should be punished for criminal acts which they have not committed. (additional note on 24:16)

Wright also denies that individual responsibility was a late discovery of Jeremiah and Ezekiel. It is "fundamental to their earliest recorded laws," and Deuteronomy 24:16 "contrasts, probably intentionally, with the laws of Hammurabi," which punished a negligent builder by executing his son (on 24:16). Above all, he says, readers miss the proportion of the commandment itself. Punishment reaches three or four generations, while God's love reaches "thousands." "Such is the nature of God that God speaks of punishment in terms of living memory and of covenant love in terms of an unimaginable long-distance future. It is a sad distortion that popular caricatures of the OT God get stuck on verse 9b and miss the breathtaking vistas of promise in verse 10" (on 5:9–10).

Terence Fretheim takes a different, more historical line. Because God himself restates this formula after the golden calf, in Exodus 34:6–7, the version in 20:5 "is finally provisional. Hence it cannot stand alone in any theological statement." In the restatement, mercy is doubled, the conditions on God's love are dropped and wrath is no longer listed among God's attributes: "Wrath is not a continuous aspect of the nature of God but a particular response to a historical situation." The retained phrase about visiting iniquity is "a continuing recognition of the moral order" (on 34:6–7). He adds, provocatively, that "God is here strikingly revealed as an experimental theologian!" (on 20:5–6).

Francis Andersen, writing on Job, puts the two truths side by side: Jeremiah and Ezekiel stressed individual responsibility "not as the only truth, not even as the highest truth, but to correct the error of those who quoted a proverb about social solidarity ... in order to evade blame." "Each man stands directly before God; but no man stands alone. One man sins, and the consequences reverberate through the entire race" (Introduction).

### 4. Genesis 6:6: "It Repented the LORD That He Had Made Man"

**The objection.** If God regretted making humanity, either he did not foresee how it would turn out, and so is not all-knowing, or he changes his mind, and so is not unchanging. Either way the Bible contradicts its own claims about God (Num. 23:19: "God is not a man ... that he should repent").

The commentators give three answers.

**It is accommodated language.** The Fathers took this line. Salvian: Scripture says "God repented" not because "God is affected by emotion or is subject to any passion," but because "the Divine Word ... speaks 'as if' in terms of human emotions"; "God's anger is simply the punishment of the sinner" (*Governance of God* 1.7, in Louth, p. 127). Augustine: "God cannot 'repent,' as human beings repent, of what he has done, since in regard to everything his judgment is as fixed as his foreknowledge is clear." Scripture speaks this way to reach "the lowliest readers," so that it can "frighten the proud and arouse the slothful" (*City of God* 15.25, ibid., p. 128). The Septuagint translators already felt the problem: Victor Hamilton notes that the Greek here avoids the usual verbs for repenting and says instead that God "considered" and "became angry" (on 6:6).

**It is real, and the doctrine must make room for it.** Hamilton is less willing to dismiss the language. Here "the God of the OT is not beyond the capability of feeling pain, chagrin, and remorse. To call him the Impassible Absolute is but part of the truth." He notes that "the vast majority of the instances of Yahweh's *nḥm* have to do with his possible change of will concerning a future plan of action," not regret over the past. "Still, the fact that the OT affirms that God does repent, even over a fait accompli, forces us to make room in our theology for the concepts of both the unchangeability of God and his changeability" (on 6:6). Andrew Steinmann calls the verses "heavily laden with anthropomorphic language," but sees in them God's "deep disappointment," and notes that the word for "grieved" is the same root as the "pain" of Adam and Eve in 3:16–17: "Human sin brought sorrow not only to humankind, but also to God" (on 6:5–6). David Cotter points out the same echo and draws out its effect on the reader, who "is invited into the heart of God" and "overhears these deepest concerns." He asks the skeptic's question himself: "How can God regret what he has done? Could God, all-knowing and all-seeing, not foresee the consequence of his creative acts?" (p. 53).

**The text really does present a God who is not omniscient.** Ronald Hendel, writing as a critical scholar, accepts the skeptic's reading of this source. In the J narrative "Yahweh is susceptible to strong emotions," and "a God who can regret is not omniscient, which Yahweh is not here. He is heartbroken at how the human experiment has turned out." But he notes that the Bible itself is divided: elsewhere, as in Numbers 23:19, "the idea that God could change his mind is anathema." "The concept of God's regret is changeable and disputed among the biblical texts" (p. 286; cf. p. 184 on "Where are you?" in Gen. 3:9).

A similar case is God's "now I know that you fear God" to Abraham (Gen. 22:12). Steinmann reads this as covenant language: God speaks "as the senior partner in a covenant," and the phrase is "evaluative and performative language," declaring that Abraham's act justifies God's promise, not reporting that God has learned something (on 22:11–14).

### 5. Genesis 22: The Binding of Isaac

**The objection.** God orders a father to kill his son as a burnt offering. Even if he stops him at the last moment, the command itself is monstrous, and the story praises Abraham for being willing to obey it.

David Cotter makes no attempt to settle this, and instead shows how deeply readers have split over it. He quotes George Steiner: "None but an evil demon can ask of a father to sacrifice his only son (so Kant); solely the true, omnipotent God can ask of a father to sacrifice his only son (so Kierkegaard)." He prints Wilfred Owen's war poem in which the old man refuses the angel and "slew his son, / And half the seed of Europe, one by one," and, opposite it, a kabbalist's reading in which Isaac is the self Abraham must surrender to God. "The will of a hideous demon; the expression of someone who wants a hug. ... it is this one story that evokes reactions so fiercely different" (pp. 144–45). His own focus is on how the narrative works. It is "the best exemplification of Hebrew style": no physical details, no access to anyone's feelings, and gaps everywhere, which "allows the reader to focus very narrowly on what God and Abraham will do." The narrator tells us at once that this is a test, "which most commentators take to be a hint to readers that Abraham will not kill his son." Cotter adds a darker note: on his record so far Abraham "seems quite willing to dispose of inconvenient members of his family," twice giving up Sarah and twice Hagar, so that "he finds it all too easy to pass this test" (p. 146).

Andrew Steinmann gives the traditional reading. The command "would have been shocking to the original readers of Genesis, since the law of Moses strictly forbade human sacrifice," and the firstborn, though dedicated to God, were redeemed by animal substitutes. Abraham's words to his servants, "we will come back to you" (22:5), "express his confidence that even after Isaac has been sacrificed, God can raise him from the dead in order to keep his promise." The test is whether Abraham trusts that God can keep his promise through Isaac "even if Isaac is slaughtered" (on 22:1–19). This is exactly how the New Testament reads the story. Hebrews 11:17–19 says Abraham "considered that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead." Lawrence Farley comments that Abraham "expected he would return with Isaac, even if that meant a return from death," and that Isaac's return became "a parable, a type and image (Gr. *parabole*) of the Resurrection of Christ" (on Heb. 11:17–19). George Guthrie calls Abraham "caught in a crisis charged with yearnings for his son and even greater yearnings to obey God" (on 11:17–19).

### 6. Job 1–2: God and the Satan

**The objection.** God boasts about Job to the Satan, then allows the Satan to kill Job's ten children and servants and cover him with sores, all to win an argument. God even admits it: "thou movedst me against him, to destroy him without cause" (2:3). Skeptics call it a heavenly wager paid for with human lives.

Francis Andersen rejects the word "wager." "Job has nothing in common with the vulgar stories of wagers between celestial beings over the corruptibility of some conspicuously good human. The agreements between Yahweh and the Satan are not bets" (Introduction). The Satan is not God's colleague: the phrase "among them" is used elsewhere of an intruder, and "It is because the Satan has no right to be there that he alone is asked his business." "There is evil here, but not dualism. The Satan may be the chief mischief-maker of the universe, but he is a mere creature, puny compared with the Lord" (on 1:6). The real issue is the Satan's question, "Does Job fear God for nought?" (1:9), which slanders both parties: "The Satan believes nothing to be genuinely good—neither Job in his disinterested piety nor God in his disinterested generosity." So the test raises "the basic questions of the book": "Is God so good that he can be loved for himself, not just for his gifts? Can a man hold on to God when there are no benefits attached?" (on 1:9–11).

On God's admission in 2:3, Andersen argues from the Hebrew *ḥinnām*. It can mean "without cause," but "there was a cause. The Lord did have a good reason for hurting Job, namely to disprove the Satan's slander." The better sense, as in Proverbs 1:17, is "in vain": "The Satan's experiment was all for nothing." He does not hide God's responsibility: although the agents were raiders and storms and the instigator was the Satan, "from God's point of view it was he who had destroyed Job" (on 2:3–5). And he notes that the whole problem exists only for a believer. Disasters are "not a problem for the polytheist, the dualist, the atheist ... An annoyance, a tragedy even, but not a problem," since suffering "is ultimately a problem only for a believer in the one Creator who is both good and almighty" (on 1:14).

David Atkinson uses the same formula, "There is evil here, but not dualism," and calls the Satan "an adversary on a chain." His answer to God's responsibility is the distinction between God's perfect and permissive will. God's "perfect order is: no sin, no sickness, no satanic tests," but "this world is not as God originally made it." The permission given to the Satan cannot be read "as God's perfect will as though we were still in the Garden of Eden." Job himself cannot see this, which is the point: "Job's faith does not relieve his suffering, it makes it worse" (on Job 1–2).

John Chrysostom reads the heavenly scenes as a literary device. The dialogue "is very anthropomorphic, the purpose being to give it a human coloring and thus attract the simpler listener"; "what he says the devil said he did not say to God, only thought to do so" (p. 24). The lesson is that the devil "can do nothing of what pleases him unless given permission from on high" (p. 25). Why, then, test someone God had already declared righteous? Chrysostom puts the question himself: "What need is there after your testimony of a further test?" His answer: "For the devil's mouth to be stopped ... for the righteous man to emerge more resplendent, for us to leave people coming later remedies of endurance and tribulation." God wanted Job "admired not only on the basis of his testimony but also from the trial of facts—after all, no one disputes a trial of facts, whereas many raise a dispute with God" (pp. 33–34).

### 8. Assessment

These passages split more cleanly than those in the companion note. In two cases the skeptic's reading really does rest on a misunderstanding. Isaiah 45:7 is a translation problem: the King James "evil" is a legitimate rendering of *raʿ*, but Motyer's statistics and the verse's own parallelism show that calamity is meant, and the verse is about God's rule over history, not the origin of sin. Exodus 20:5 is a case of reading one half of a sentence. Wright's distinction between the social consequences of sin and judicial punishment is sound, and the commandment's contrast between three or four generations and thousands is the point the objection leaves out.

The hardening of Pharaoh is a genuine theological question, not a misreading, and the most useful thing the commentaries show is that the text itself already answers the crude version of the objection. God does not harden an innocent man at random: Pharaoh hardens himself through five plagues before God is said to act, and every commentator here sees the hardening as judgment. What remains is the old dispute between sovereignty and free will, on which Murray and Chrysostom stand at opposite ends. That is a dispute within Christian theology, not evidence that the text is incoherent, though a skeptic may fairly say that if Murray is right the objection about justice has not been answered so much as declared out of bounds ("who art thou that repliest against God?").

Genesis 6:6 shows the commentators at their most divided, and Hendel's frankness is useful. If one reads Genesis as a set of sources, the skeptic is partly right: this narrator pictures God in very human terms. If one reads it as a single canonical book, Hamilton's position is the strongest: the Bible deliberately holds God's freedom to respond together with his faithfulness, and the doctrine of impassibility has to account for both. The patristic answer, that this is language adapted to the hearer, is sound as far as it goes, but used alone it risks emptying the text of the very pathos that Cotter and Steinmann rightly see in it.

Genesis 22 and Job 1–2 are not misunderstood by skeptics; they are understood and found troubling, and the honest commentators say so. Cotter's refusal to tidy up Genesis 22 is a better witness than a quick appeal to "it was only a test," which explains the outcome but not the command. The strongest Christian readings, Hebrews' resurrection reading of Genesis 22 and Andersen's reading of Job as a test of whether God can be loved for himself, do not make the stories comfortable. They show that the stories are asking the skeptic's own question, about whether faith is anything more than self-interest, and answering it at a cost.

### Conclusion

The hard texts about God's character are not all hard in the same way. "I create evil" and "the sins of the fathers" largely dissolve once the Hebrew word and the whole sentence are read. The hardening of Pharaoh and God's "regret" are real theological puzzles, and the commentaries show the range of honest Christian answers: sovereign judgment, judicial abandonment, free choice ratified by God, and genuine divine pathos. The binding of Isaac and the testing of Job remain disturbing, and the best commentators do not pretend otherwise. What they show instead is that these stories were written to raise exactly the questions skeptics put to them, and that the biblical writers did not flinch from those questions either.
