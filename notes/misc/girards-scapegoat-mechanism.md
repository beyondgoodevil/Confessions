"Things which have been kept secret from the foundation of the world" (Matt 13:35) is the verse Girard took for the title of his main book. His claim is that the secret is a murder: human culture began, and keeps itself going, by uniting against a victim, and the Gospels are the text that finally says so.

This note is the theory. For how Girard reads Nietzsche against it, see [[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]]. For the psychology of desire behind it, see [[Mimetic Desire: Girard, Oughourlian and Lacan]].

## 1. The Engine: Mimetic Rivalry

- Humans imitate more than any other animal, and they imitate **desires**, not just gestures. If my model wants something, I learn to want it too. (Developed in detail in the desire note.)
- Imitating someone's desire makes them my **rival**: two hands reach for the same object. The more we compete, the more alike we become. Girard calls the rivals **doubles**.
- Rivalry is **contagious**. Others copy the conflict as well as the desire. Left alone it spreads into a war of all against all, an endless cycle of revenge.
- **Primitive prohibitions make sense in this light** (*Things Hidden* I.1). Societies forbid imitation and treat twins, mirrors and resemblance as dangerous, nearly as dangerous as vengeance. The odd taboos are a way of preventing doubles.
- Plato alone among philosophers feared mimesis, so he came close to the truth. But he never connected it with the **object** the rivals fight over.

## 2. The Sacrificial Crisis (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 2)

- Cultural order is a system of **differences**: rank, kinship, roles. Girard quotes Ulysses on "Degree" in Shakespeare's *Troilus and Cressida*: "Take but degree away... and, hark, what discord follows!"
- In a mimetic crisis, differences collapse. Everyone becomes everyone's double. Greek tragedy shows it: Eteocles and Polynices, Oedipus against Creon against Tiresias, each accusing the other of what he does himself. Tragedy, unlike myth, makes the symmetry visible.
- Myths remember crises as **plague**, flood, chaos, monsters, the mixing of man and beast, incest (the ultimate confusion of family roles), and twins.
- **Sacrifice exists to prevent this.** "The sacrifice serves to protect the entire community from its own violence; it prompts the entire community to choose victims outside itself." The victim is a substitute for all, offered by all.
- **Who can be sacrificed?** Those without a full social link to the community, so their death brings no revenge: prisoners, slaves, children, outsiders, the king (set apart above), animals.
- **Judicial systems** do the same job more efficiently. They have "a monopoly on the means of revenge", so the last word is public and final. That only works while people believe in its transcendence.

## 3. The Single Victim Mechanism

- At the height of the crisis, the "all against all" suddenly becomes "**all against one**".
- Mimesis does the work. One accusation is copied, as the first stone is the hardest to throw and the second comes easier (*I See Satan* ch. 4). The whole community fixes on one victim and kills or expels them.
- **The choice is arbitrary**, but the victim usually bears the **marks of a victim**: foreign, disabled, ugly, a king or a pauper, a woman in some cultures, a twin. Anyone set apart.
- **And it works.** The community is suddenly at peace, united "unanimity minus one". Since the violence and the peace both seem to come from the victim, the victim is seen as the **cause** of both.
- So comes the **double transference** (*I See Satan* ch. 5–6):
  1. **Demonization**: the victim caused the crisis. They are guilty of parricide, incest, poisoning the wells.
  2. **Divinization**: the victim brought the peace. They must be a god.
- That is **the sacred**: violence experienced as coming from outside, both terrible and saving. The word *pharmakos* (the human scapegoat) is related to *pharmakon*, meaning both "poison" and "remedy".
- **Girard's sharpest formula** (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 1): "Because the victim is sacred, it is criminal to kill him, but the victim is sacred only because he is to be killed."

## 4. Ritual, Myth and Institutions

- **Ritual** re-enacts the founding event on purpose, to get its peace again: it repeats the crisis (festivals of disorder, mock battles, role reversals) and then the killing (sacrifice). Substitution lets an animal take the place of the original victim.
- **Myth** is the founding murder **told by the murderers**. It keeps the elements of the event but hides what really happened. The victim is guilty; the god arranged it; or the murder is erased (*The Scapegoat* ch. 6–7):
  - **Baldr**: everything swears not to harm him, the gods playfully throw things at him, the blind Hoehr is guided by Loki. A collective murder dressed as a game, with the blame on one person.
  - **The Curetes**, who dance around the infant Zeus to "protect" him: a group circling a child, the same scene as the Titans circling the infant Dionysus, but with the violence turned into protection.
  - **The Aztec sun-myth at Teotihuacan**: the gods "voluntarily" throw themselves into the fire. Girard: they speak with one voice to choose the second "volunteer".
- The "**crimes of the gods**" (Zeus's affairs, etc.) are traces of the accusations once made against victims.
- **Institutions** grow out of ritual (*Things Hidden* I.2):
  - **sacred kingship**: the king is a victim whose execution is deferred, hence royal incest and ritual kingly murder in African kingdoms;
  - **domestication**: every domesticated species has been a sacrificial victim;
  - **funeral rites**: "The tomb is nothing but the first human monument to be raised over the surrogate victim";
  - **marriage and exchange rules**.
- **Hominization** (I.3): Girard guesses that the victim mechanism is what made humans human. Weapons removed animal restraints on fighting. The victim became the first "**transcendental signifier**", the first thing set apart and named. Language and symbolic thought begin there.

## 5. Oedipus, Dionysus and the Greeks (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 3, 5)

- **Oedipus**: Thebes has a plague. Oedipus, Creon and Tiresias accuse each other in symmetry. Then the crisis is put onto one person, who becomes "the parricide and incestuous one", the man who **abolished differences**. Expelled, he brings peace, and in *Oedipus at Colonus* he becomes a sacred protector. A *pharmakos* made a king and a king made a *pharmakos*.
- Freud saw something real, but misread it. What's repressed is "not the desire for patricide or incest but the violence that lurked behind these all-too-visible motifs".
- **The *Bacchae***: the Dionysian festival is a sacrificial crisis staged on purpose. Differences dissolve, men and beasts merge, and the maenads tear Pentheus apart as a beast. Dionysus is the god who runs the whole lynching "from afar".
- Girard asks whether the role of women in the myth is itself a mythic displacement, sparing the men who were really responsible.
- **Euripides** is close to exposing the mechanism. He shows the bacchanal as "pure frenzy, naked violence".
- See [[Dionysus, Orpheus and the Mysteries: Dodds and the Irrational]].

## 6. Texts of Persecution (*The Scapegoat* ch. 1–2)

- **Guillaume de Machaut** (14th century): during the Black Death, Jews poisoned the rivers and were rightly massacred. We read this immediately as a **persecution text**: the violence was real, the accusation false, and the narrator shares the mob's illusion.
- Four **stereotypes of persecution**:
  1. a **crisis** (plague, social collapse)
  2. **crimes** that erase differences (incest, bestiality, poisoning, sacrilege)
  3. the **signs of a victim** (minority, deformity)
  4. **violence**
- Girard's bet: myths have the **same structure**. Oedipus has all four. If we read Machaut as a persecution text, we should read Oedipus the same way.
- **Why can we see through Machaut but not myths?** Because Western culture has learned to read from the victim's side, a skill that came from the Bible. The end of witch-hunts roughly coincides with the first scientific revolution: people stopped looking for guilty humans behind plagues and began looking for natural causes (ch. 8).
- "**Scapegoat**" has two senses:
  - the ritual (Leviticus 16), where the goat bears the people's sins into the wilderness. Notably, the victim there is never human;
  - the modern sense, an innocent blamed. The second meaning shows that the Bible has already done its work in us.

## 7. The Bible: Telling It from the Victim's Side

- **Same structure, opposite meaning.** Biblical stories have the same crises and expulsions as myths, but they side with the victim.
- **Cain and Abel.** Cain founds the first city after a murder (Gen 4:17). The founding murder is right there, but it is called a crime. "The voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me" (Gen 4:10). God's sevenfold vengeance protects Cain: the first law against killing.
- **Joseph** (*I See Satan* ch. 9; *Things Hidden* II.1) is a counter-Oedipus. Joseph is accused, by brothers and then by Potiphar's wife and the Egyptian crowd, and is innocent. Oedipus is accused and the myth agrees. Joseph forgives and saves.
- **Job** is not about the problem of suffering in the abstract. He is the victim of his community, his "friends" speak for the crowd's verdict, and he refuses it: "I know that my redeemer liveth" (Job 19:25). In Girard's reading, his Redeemer is his **defender**.
- **The Psalms** give the victim a voice for the first time: "They that hate me without a cause are more than the hairs of mine head" (Ps 69:4).
- **The Suffering Servant**: "he was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter" (Isa 53:7).
- **The tenth commandment** goes to the root: "Thou shalt not covet... any thing that is thy neighbour's" (Exod 20:17). The Hebrew means simply "desire". The commandments against killing, adultery, theft and false witness all follow from the rivalrous desire this one forbids.

## 8. The Gospels and the Passion

**The Passion is the complete mechanism, written down truthfully.**
- **The crisis**: Jerusalem divided, Herod and Pilate enemies.
- **The contagion**: the crowd that shouted "Hosanna" now shouts "Crucify him". Peter's denial is mimetic: by the fire he imitates the servants around him (*The Scapegoat* ch. 12).
- **Unanimity**: even the disciples flee. "The same day Pilate and Herod were made friends together" (Luke 23:12): reconciliation through the victim.
- **The political logic**, spoken aloud by Caiaphas: "it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not" (John 11:50).
- **The verdict refused**: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25). And the most important line: "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). This defines the persecutors' unconscious: they don't know what they are doing.
- **What's different from myth**: the Resurrection makes a small minority **break from the crowd** and proclaim the victim innocent. In myth, unanimity holds; here it is broken.

**Satan and the powers**
- **Satan** is the mimetic process itself (*I See Satan* ch. 3):
  - he is the **accuser** (that is what the name means);
  - he is "a murderer from the beginning... a liar, and the father of it" (John 8:44);
  - his kingdom is founded on the murder and kept going by the lie that the victim was guilty.
- "**How can Satan cast out Satan?**" (Mark 3:23). Girard takes it literally: the single victim mechanism is violence expelling violence. That is how disorder makes order, and why the kingdom of Satan stands, but only for a while (*The Scapegoat* ch. 14).
- **The Gerasene demoniac** ("My name is Legion: for we are many", Mark 5:9) lives among tombs and cuts himself with stones, a self-lynching. The demons go into the swine, which rush over the cliff: the expulsion turned back on the mob (ch. 13).
- **The Paraclete** is the "**advocate for the defence**", the opposite of the accuser (*The Scapegoat* ch. 15).
- **Powers and principalities** are human institutions founded on a collective murder. They are real and authorized by God (Rom 13), but rooted in a false transcendence (*I See Satan* ch. 8).
- **The Cross unmasks them**: "having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it" (Col 2:15). Girard: "The powers are not put on display because they are defeated, but they are defeated because they are put on display."
- **Satan duped by the Cross** (*I See Satan* ch. 11) is the patristic theme Girard rehabilitates. "Had they known it, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (1 Cor 2:8). Origen and the Greek Fathers, including Gregory of Nyssa's fish-hook image, say Satan was tricked into a killing that destroyed him. The West grew embarrassed by it as "magical thinking". Girard: it has an essential insight. Satan, thinking he was running one more lynching, set off the one that would reveal all the others.

## 9. Christ: the Non-Sacrificial Reading and Its Revision

- ***Things Hidden* (1978), II.2–3**: Girard's early claim.
  - The Gospels never call Jesus's death a sacrifice. Jesus quotes Hosea: "I will have mercy, and not sacrifice" (Matt 9:13).
  - The "**sacrificial reading**" (the Father demanding the Son's death to satisfy his honour) belongs to Hebrews and medieval theology. It has rebuilt the old sacred on top of the Gospel. Historical **Christendom** depends on it.
  - **The judgement of Solomon** (1 Kgs 3): the bad mother accepts the sacrifice ("divide it"); the true mother gives up her claim so the child may live. Christ is the true mother, giving himself not as a victim the Father demands but to stop the cycle.
  - **The apocalypse**: Jesus's warnings about wars and catastrophes are about violence men bring on themselves, not God's wrath. With the sacrificial safety valve gone, violence will either be renounced or escalate. Nuclear weapons make this literal.
- **The revision**: later (in essays and interviews from the 1990s on) Girard said he was wrong to reject the word "sacrifice" and to scapegoat Hebrews. There are two kinds of sacrifice:
  - sacrificing **the other** (archaic religion);
  - sacrificing **oneself** for the other (Christ, and Solomon's true mother).
  - The Cross is the second kind, done to end the first.
- **The divinity of Christ** (*Things Hidden* II.2): Christ is not God because he was killed (that would be the mythic pattern). He is the only one who could reveal the mechanism, because it never had a hold on him. The **virgin birth** says the same: he did not come out of the mimetic order.
- **John's Logos vs Heraclitus's Logos** (II.4):
  - Heraclitus: "War is the father of all". A Logos of violence, order through strife.
  - John: "He came unto his own, and his own received him not" (John 1:11). A **Logos expelled**, a Logos of love.
  - Philosophy, including Heidegger, has never been able to tell them apart. Compare [[John's Gospel and Greek Philosophy]].

## 10. Our World: The Concern for Victims

- **Modern concern for victims** (*I See Satan* ch. 13) is a Christian product:
  - hospitals, which begin as charitable houses;
  - human rights;
  - even political correctness.
  - We can still hate victims, but we can't worship them.
- The danger is that it becomes a new weapon: "**victim power**". People accuse others of persecution in order to persecute them. Paul: "thou that judgest doest the same things" (Rom 2:1).
- **Two Nietzschean legacies** (ch. 14):
  - Nietzsche saw that Dionysus and the Passion are the same kind of violence. He then chose Dionysus, and Girard thinks it broke him.
  - Hitler's attempt to abolish the concern for victims by force ended in failure.
  - The **deconstructionist** inheritance (Derrida's *pharmakon* essay) makes the opposite mistake: it sees the double meaning everywhere and refuses to say that the victim is innocent.

## 11. The Four Books at a Glance

| book | main contribution |
|---|---|
| *Violence and the Sacred* (1972) | the sacrificial crisis, the surrogate victim, Oedipus and Dionysus, critiques of Freud and Lévi-Strauss |
| *Things Hidden* (1978) | the full theory as dialogue: hominization, myth, the Bible as revelation, the "non-sacrificial" reading, interdividual psychology |
| *The Scapegoat* (1982) | persecution texts and stereotypes, myths as persecution texts, close readings of the Passion, Peter, the Baptist, Gerasa |
| *I See Satan* (1999) | the mimetic cycle and Satan, Apollonius of Tyana's lynching as a counter-Gospel, the triumph of the Cross, the modern concern for victims |

**Apollonius of Tyana** is Girard's best illustration (*I See Satan* ch. 4). In Philostratus, the pagan wonder-worker stops a plague at Ephesus by getting the crowd to stone a beggar. When they uncover the body, it is a demonic dog, and a statue of Heracles the Averter is set up on the spot. Put this beside John 8 ("He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her") and you have the difference between pagan miracle and Gospel.

## 12. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's convincing**
- The **persecution text** argument is strong. We do read Machaut as Girard says, and it really is hard to say why Oedipus deserves a different reading.
- It makes the Bible's distinctiveness visible without special pleading: Cain vs Romulus, Joseph vs Oedipus, Job vs his comforters, the Passion vs Apollonius.
- **Satan as accuser and liar, the powers unmasked, the patristic deception of the devil**: Girard gives these back their weight. The modern West had largely lost them, while the Orthodox liturgy never did ("by death He trampled down death").
- It explains much of the moral psychology of the Fathers: **envy** as the root of murder ("through envy of the devil came death into the world", Wisdom 2:24); the danger of judging; crowds. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- The **revised** account of sacrifice (self-offering against sacrifice of the other) fits Orthodox liturgy well: "Thou art he that offereth and is offered" (priest's prayer at the Cherubic Hymn). Christ is priest and victim freely; no crowd seizes him without his consent: "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself" (John 10:18).

**Where to be careful**
- **It's a hypothesis about everything.** One founding mechanism behind all religion, kingship, domestication and language is very hard to test, and anthropologists have mostly not accepted it. The Bible-reading parts stand without the full prehistory.
- **The early anti-sacrificial reading** was wrong, as Girard admitted. Hebrews and the Passover lamb ("Christ our passover is sacrificed for us", 1 Cor 5:7) are not later corruptions. But Orthodoxy also never had the Anselmian "Father appeased by the Son's death" Girard was really attacking. See [[Recapitulation Doctrine vs PSA]], [[Why Jesus' Death and Resurrection Had to Be So Violent]].
- **Revelation isn't the whole of salvation.** If the Cross mainly **exposes** violence, Christ risks becoming a teacher of anthropology. For the Fathers the problem is also **death and corruption**, which must be destroyed from inside by the incarnate God who rises. The Resurrection in Girard does real work (it breaks the crowd's unanimity), but it has to be more than that. See [[Death and Corruptibility as Privations of Life and the Ability to Sin]].
- **Christian anti-Judaism**: Girard is clear that the Passion accuses **all** humanity, not "the Jews". The persecution of Jews is exactly a case of Christians falling back into myth.
