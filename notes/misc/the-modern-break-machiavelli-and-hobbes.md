> "Again, the devil taketh him up into an exceeding high mountain, and sheweth him all the kingdoms of the world, and the glory of them; and saith unto him, All these things will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me." (Matt 4:8–9)

Leo Strauss's thesis is that modern political thought begins with a deliberate **"lowering of the ultimate goal"**. Classical and Christian thought asked how men *ought* to live and built the city around virtue, or around salvation. Machiavelli asked how men *do* live, and built on that. Hobbes completed the turn by rebuilding natural law on the passion that is strongest in most men most of the time, the fear of violent death. The aim was to make the good society *achievable*. The cost was the good itself.

## 1. Machiavelli: The Effectual Truth

Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527) was secretary of the Florentine republic. After the Medici returned in 1512 he was tortured and exiled to his farm, where he wrote *The Prince* (1513), dedicated to Lorenzo de' Medici, and the *Discourses on Livy*.

**Against imagined republics (*Prince* XV)**
- "It has appeared to me more fitting to go directly to the **effectual truth** of the thing than to the imagination of it. And many have imagined republics and principalities that have never been seen or known to exist in truth". That means Plato's *Republic*, Augustine's City of God, and the mirrors for princes.
- "It is so far from how one lives to how one should live that he who lets go of what is done for what should be done learns his ruin rather than his preservation." "A man who wants to make a profession of good in all regards must come to ruin among so many who are not good."
- "Hence it is necessary to a prince, if he wants to maintain himself, to **learn to be able not to be good**, and to use this and not use it according to necessity."
- "Something appears to be virtue, which if pursued would be one's ruin, and something else appears to be vice, which if pursued results in one's security and well-being."

**Virtue and fortune (VI, XXV)**
- *Virtù* is no longer moral virtue. It is the founder's spirited capacity to impose a "form" on the "matter" that opportunity provides. Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus "had nothing else from fortune than the opportunity".
- On Moses, slyly: "although one should not reason about Moses, as he was a mere executor of things that had been ordered for him by God… if their particular actions and orders are considered, they will appear no different from those of Moses, who had so great a teacher."
- **"All the armed prophets conquered and the unarmed ones were ruined"**, like Savonarola, burned in 1498. "Things must be ordered in such a mode that when they no longer believe, one can make them believe by force." The reader is left to ask about Christ, the unarmed prophet.
- **Fortune is "arbiter of half of our actions"**, a flooding river that dikes and dams can contain. "Fortune is a woman; and it is necessary, if one wants to hold her down, to beat her and strike her down." She favours "the young, because they are less cautious, more ferocious".

**Crime and cruelty "well used" (VIII)**
- Agathocles of Syracuse murdered the senate and rich men at a signal. Machiavelli first says, "one cannot call it virtue to kill one's citizens, betray one's friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; these modes can enable one to acquire empire, but not glory." Then he asks how such men could rule securely. The answer is "cruelties badly used or well used… (if it is permissible to speak well of evil)": done "at a stroke", then turned to the subjects' benefit.

**Loved or feared (XVII)**
- Cesare Borgia's cruelty pacified the Romagna, making him "much more merciful than the Florentine people, who so as to escape a name for cruelty, allowed Pistoia to be destroyed".
- "It is much safer to be feared than loved". Men "are ungrateful, fickle, pretenders and dissemblers, evaders of danger, eager for gain". "Love is held by a chain of obligation, which, because men are wicked, is broken at every opportunity". "Fear is held by a dread of punishment that never forsakes you."
- But avoid hatred: abstain from your subjects' property and women, "because men forget the death of a father more quickly than the loss of a patrimony".

**The fox and the lion (XVIII)**
- There are two kinds of combat, by laws (proper to man) and by force (proper to beasts). Achilles was raised by Chiron the centaur, "half-beast, half-man". The prince must be "a fox to recognize snares and a lion to frighten the wolves".
- "A prudent lord… cannot observe faith, nor should he, when such observance turns against him". Alexander VI "never did anything… but how to deceive men".
- "It is not necessary for a prince to have all the above-mentioned qualities in fact, but it is indeed necessary to **appear** to have them". He should appear "all mercy, all faith, all honesty, all humanity, all religion. And nothing is more necessary to appear to have than this last quality."
- "Men in general judge more by their eyes than by their hands… Everyone sees how you appear, few touch what you are". "Let a prince win and maintain his state: the means will always be judged honorable".

**The exhortation (XXVI)**
- Italy is "more enslaved than the Hebrews… without a head, without order, beaten, despoiled". She awaits a redeemer.
- The language is Exodus: "the sea has opened; a cloud has escorted you along the way; the stone has poured forth water; here manna has rained". Then: "The remainder you must do yourself. God does not want to do everything, so as not to take free will from us and that part of the glory that falls to us."
- Livy: "war is just to whom it is necessary, and arms are pious when there is no hope but in arms".

**Religion in the *Discourses***
- **The founder must be alone (I.9).** Romulus' murder of Remus is excused as necessary to establish sole authority.
- **Numa (I.11).** Numa "turned to religion as a thing altogether necessary if he wished to maintain a civilization". He "pretended to be intimate with a nymph". Roman citizens "feared to break an oath much more than the laws". "There was never any orderer of extraordinary laws for a people who did not have recourse to God". The test of a religion is what it does for the city.
- **Maintain it even if false (I.12).** Princes "should favor and magnify" everything that supports their religion, "even though they judge them false; and they should do it so much the more as they are more prudent". Italy has lost religion "by means of the Roman Church", whose bad example has made Italians "without religion and bad".
- **Christianity has made the world weak (II.2).** "Our religion has glorified humble and contemplative more than active men. It has then placed the highest good in humility, abjectness, and contempt of things human". "If our religion asks that you have strength in yourself, it wishes you to be capable more of suffering than of doing something strong. **This mode of life thus seems to have rendered the world weak and given it in prey to criminal men**, who can manage it securely, seeing that the collectivity of men, so as to go to paradise, think more of enduring their beatings than of avenging them." He adds, with Machiavellian politeness, that this comes "more from the cowardice of the men who have interpreted our religion according to idleness and not according to virtue".

## 2. Strauss on Machiavelli

- **"The old-fashioned and simple opinion"** is that Machiavelli was "a teacher of evil". What else could describe one who teaches that "princes ought to exterminate the families of rulers whose territory they wish to possess securely"? The Athenians at Melos and Callicles said such things "behind closed doors", in the mouths of characters. Machiavelli "alone has dared to utter the evil doctrine in a book and in his own name". See [[Thucydides on Human Nature and Power]].
- **Against the softer readings.**
  - He was not merely a patriot: "he is more concerned with the salvation of his fatherland than with the salvation of his soul". "Patriotism as Machiavelli understood it is collective selfishness". "Love of one's own is inferior to love of what is both one's own and good."
  - Nor was he a value-free scientist. "The indispensable condition of 'scientific' analysis is then moral obtuseness."
  - Scholars praise his "praise of religion" without noticing that it is "only the reverse side of… his complete indifference to the truth of religion". They miss this "because they are pupils of Machiavelli".
- **A fallen angel.** "Even if… he himself [is] a devil, we are forced to remember the profound theological truth that the devil is a fallen angel." He has "a perverted nobility of a very high order". Marlowe's line fits: "I hold there is no sin but ignorance."
- **The lowered goal** (*Natural Right and History*). The "realistic revolt" led to "the substitution of patriotism or merely political virtue for human excellence… for moral virtue and the contemplative life". "The goal was lowered in order to increase the probability of its attainment."
- **Justice is rooted in crime.** "All legitimacy has its root in illegitimacy… The founder of the most renowned of all commonwealths was a fratricide." Machiavelli "takes his bearings… by the extreme case". "The root or the efficient cause takes the place of the end or of the purpose." He was "that greater Columbus" who discovered the continent on which Hobbes built.

## 3. Hobbes: Leviathan

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) translated Thucydides in 1629 to warn England against democracy. He fled the Civil War to Paris, and published *Leviathan* in 1651.

**The artificial man (Introduction).** "Nature (the Art whereby God hath made and governes the World) is by the Art of man… imitated". The commonwealth is "an Artificiall Man", the sovereign its "Artificiall Soul".

**Religion defined (ch. 6).** "Feare of power invisible, feigned by the mind, or imagined from tales publiquely allowed, RELIGION; not allowed, SUPERSTITION. And when the power imagined, is truly such as we imagine, TRUE RELIGION." The *state's* allowance distinguishes religion from superstition.

**No highest good (ch. 11)**
- "There is no such Finis ultimus, (utmost ayme,) nor Summum Bonum, (greatest Good,) as is spoken of in the Books of the old Morall Philosophers." "Felicity is a continuall progresse of the desire, from one object to another".
- "I put for a generall inclination of all mankind, **a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death**." This is not because men always want more delight, "but because he cannot assure the power and means to live well, which he hath present, without the acquisition of more".

**The natural condition (ch. 13)**
- Men are roughly **equal**: the weakest can kill the strongest "by secret machination". So "if any two men desire the same thing… they become enemies".
- **Three causes of quarrel.** "First, Competition; Secondly, Diffidence; Thirdly, Glory. The first, maketh men invade for Gain; the second, for Safety; and the third, for Reputation". The third is "for trifles, as a word, a smile, a different opinion". These are Thucydides' interest, fear and honour, rearranged.
- Without "a common Power to keep them all in awe", men are in "such a warre, as is of every man, against every man". War is "a tract of time" with the known disposition to fight, "as it is in the nature of Weather".
- Then there is "no Industry… no Arts; no Letters; no Society; and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; **And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.**"
- **The test from experience.** Do you not lock your doors and chests? "Does he not there as much accuse mankind by his actions, as I do by my words? But neither of us accuse mans nature in it. The Desires, and other Passions of man, are in themselves no Sin."
- **No injustice by nature.** "Where there is no common Power, there is no Law: where no Law, no Injustice. **Force, and Fraud, are in warre the two Cardinall vertues.**"

**Right and law of nature (ch. 14)**
- The *right* of nature is liberty to use one's power "for the preservation of his own Nature". In the state of nature "every man has a Right to every thing; even to one anothers body".
- The *first law*: "to seek Peace, and follow it". The *second*: to lay down one's right to all things "when others are so too" and be "contented with so much liberty against other men, as he would allow other men against himselfe". "This is that Law of the Gospell; Whatsoever you require that others should do to you, that do ye to them."
- **Injustice is like absurdity**: "voluntarily to undo that, which from the beginning he had voluntarily done".

**The Fool (ch. 15)**
- "The Foole hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice". Why keep covenants when breaking them pays? "Successfull wickednesse hath obtained the name of Vertue".
- Hobbes answers that it is not rational. The man who breaks faith can be received into no society "but by the errour of them that receive him", and he cannot count on such errors. Justice is "keeping of valid Covenants", and covenants are valid only once "a Civill Power, sufficient to compell" exists.

**The Mortal God (ch. 17).** Each covenants with each: "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him". "This is the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more reverently) of that **Mortall God**, to which wee owe under the Immortal God, our peace and defence." The name comes from Job 41:34, the beast that is "king over all the children of pride".

**Christianity reduced (Parts III–IV)**
- **What is necessary (ch. 43).** "All that is NECESSARY to Salvation, is contained in two Vertues, Faith in Christ, and Obedience to Laws." The only necessary article is that **Jesus is the Christ**. Christ "hath not given us new Laws, but Counsell to observe those wee are subject to; that is to say, the Laws of Nature, and the Laws of our severall Soveraigns". "The Laws of God therefore are none but the Laws of Nature, whereof the principall is… to obey our Civill Soveraigns."
- **The limit.** Where obedience would mean damnation, Christ's counsel holds: "Fear not those that kill the body, but cannot kill the soule" (Matt 10:28). Hobbes quotes it himself. But the sovereign is the supreme pastor and the authorised interpreter of Scripture. In ch. 42 he lets a Christian, like Naaman, outwardly obey a command to deny Christ.
- **The Kingdom of Darkness (ch. 47).** "The Papacy, is no other, than the **Ghost of the deceased Romane Empire**, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof". Latin is "the Ghost of the Old Romane Language". The clergy are like fairies whose king is Beelzebub.

## 4. Strauss on Hobbes

- **The founder of modern political philosophy.** Hobbes ("that imprudent, impish, and iconoclastic extremist, that first plebeian philosopher") agrees with Socrates that political philosophy is about natural right. But he takes from Epicurus the premise that man is "by nature… an a-political and even an a-social animal" and that the good is the pleasant. He gives hedonism "a political meaning", and so is "the creator of **political hedonism**". With it comes **political atheism**, which is "a distinctly modern phenomenon". Burke saw it: atheists "formerly like the old Epicureans, rather an unenterprising race… of late they are grown active, designing, turbulent".
- **Knowledge as making.** "We understand only what we make." The universe is unintelligible, so man can be sovereign only "because he is absolutely a stranger in the universe". "The vision of the City of Man to be erected on the ruins of the City of God is an unsupported hope." *Scientia propter potentiam*: knowledge for the sake of power.
- **Death in place of the telos.** Natural law must be built on the most powerful passion, "the fear of violent death at the hands of others: not nature but 'that terrible enemy of nature, death'". "Death takes the place of the telos."
- **Rights before duties.** "The fundamental moral fact is not a duty but a right". All duties are conditional. "If we may call liberalism that political doctrine which regards as the fundamental political fact the rights, as distinguished from the duties, of man… we must say that **the founder of liberalism was Hobbes**." Rights are easier to rely on because "the premises already are in the passions" (Burke: "the little catechism of the rights of men is soon learned").
- **The state of nature replaces the state of grace.** Before Hobbes, "state of nature" belonged to *theology*: pure nature, fallen nature, and grace. "Hobbes dropped the subdivision and replaced the state of grace by the state of civil society. He thus denied… the importance of the Fall", asserting that what remedies man's condition "is, not divine grace, but the right kind of human government".
- **Vanity and fear** (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes*). Beneath the mechanism lies a *moral* antithesis. Vanity, the pride that "makes men blind", is "fundamentally unjust". Fear of violent death, which "makes men see", is "fundamentally just". Strauss notes this looks like "the 'secularized' form of the traditional antithesis between spiritual pride and fear of God (or humility)", with the almighty God replaced by deadly enemies and then by "the over-mighty State, 'the Mortall God'". Because vanity rules public life, Hobbes prefers **monarchy**, where publicity is weakest. That preference runs from his Thucydides preface onward.

## 5. Comparison

| question | **Classical (Plato, Aristotle)** | **Christian (Fathers)** | **Machiavelli** | **Hobbes** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| starting point | how one ought to live | God, the Fall, grace | how men do live; the extreme case | the passions; fear of violent death |
| the good | virtue, contemplation | communion with God | glory, the safety of the fatherland | no *summum bonum*; peace, self-preservation |
| human nature | political animal | image of God, fallen | "ungrateful, fickle… eager for gain" | restless desire of power; equal and dangerous |
| justice | natural, prior to the city | God's law written on the heart | rooted in crime, made by founders | made by covenant and the sword |
| religion | civic piety and true theology | the truth itself | a useful instrument, "even if false" | fear of invisible power as allowed by the state |
| the remedy | education in virtue | grace, repentance, the Church | the armed prophet; *virtù* against fortune | the Leviathan |

## 6. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What they saw**
- Both men describe fallen man accurately. Machiavelli's men "ungrateful, fickle… eager for gain" and Hobbes's "restless desire of Power after power" are no harsher than Scripture: "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually" (Gen 6:5). Augustine's *libido dominandi*, the lust to rule that "dominates even those who dominate", is the same thing seen from inside. So is Maximus' *philautia*, the self-love from which every passion grows. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]] and [[Augustine's City of God]].
- Hobbes's endless desire is Augustine's **restless heart** with God removed. Augustine: "our heart is restless until it rests in Thee". Hobbes keeps the restlessness and denies the rest: "there is no such Finis ultimus". Desire that has lost its true end does not stop; it turns into the pursuit of power. That is the most exact diagnosis in *Leviathan*, and Hobbes draws the wrong conclusion from it. See [[Augustine's Confessions: Memory, Time and the Restless Heart]].

**Against "learning to be able not to be good"**
- The principle of *The Prince* is the one Paul names and condemns: "Let us do evil, that good may come? whose damnation is just" (Rom 3:8). Doing evil "at a stroke" for the sake of the state is still murder. The Fathers' rule for those who must use force is the reverse of Machiavelli's: the *Basis of the Social Concept* points to the St George icon, where the struggle with evil must not share in it. See [[Orthodox Political Theology: Symphonia, Autocracy or Democracy?]].
- **To seem religious without being so** is what Christ calls hypocrisy: "ye are like unto whited sepulchres" (Matt 23:27). It is also the third temptation. Machiavelli's prince accepts the kingdoms of the world on the devil's terms. Christ answered: "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve" (Matt 4:10).

**On "Christianity has made the world weak"**
- This is the most serious charge, and Nietzsche will repeat it. Machiavelli is right that the Gospel prefers suffering wrong to doing it (1 Cor 6:7; Matt 5:39). But he misreads what that does. The martyrs, who would neither avenge nor obey, defeated the Roman Empire that Machiavelli admired. "My strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Cor 12:9). Christian peoples also defended their fatherlands. St Cyril's answer to the Saracens distinguishes bearing *private* injury from defending *one's neighbour*, which is the Orthodox position (see the *Basis*). The Church never taught the "idleness" Machiavelli ascribes to it.
- His **"armed prophet"** is answered by Christ, the unarmed prophet who was "ruined" and rose again. Christ's kingdom has outlasted every armed founder Machiavelli names. Moses, whom Machiavelli puts among the founders, did not found by fraud. He feared God and was "very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth" (Num 12:3).

**On Hobbes**
- **Fear of violent death** as the foundation of morality is answered by Hobbes's own quotation: "Fear not them which kill the body" (Matt 10:28). The martyr breaks Hobbes's system. He does not fear violent death more than God, and so the Leviathan cannot finally command him. That is why every Leviathan-state persecutes the Church: it cannot bear a subject who fears something more than it.
- **Strauss is right that Hobbes abolished the Fall** and put government where grace had been. Orthodoxy holds the opposite. The state restrains sin (Rom 13:4), but only grace heals it. A politics that promises to remedy the human condition by the right constitution becomes, sooner or later, an idol. See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]] and [[The Fall of Humanity and Its Consequences]].
- **The name "Leviathan"** is chosen with dark wit. In Job 41 the Leviathan is "king over all the children of pride". The Fathers, from Gregory the Great's *Moralia* onward, read it as the devil. The Orthodox Church, at the Great Blessing of the Waters, praises the One who "crushed the heads of the dragons" in the Jordan (Ps 74:13–14). A Christian cannot give the title "Mortal God" to anything.
- **The one-article creed.** Reducing faith to "Jesus is the Christ" plus obedience to the sovereign makes the state the Church's bishop. That is Erastianism. It is close to what Peter the Great imposed on the Russian Church, which Orthodox history counts among its worst wounds.
- **Rights before duties.** Strauss's genealogy explains the instinct of documents like the *Basis*: rights cut loose from duties become "the protection of self-will". The Orthodox answer is not to discard rights. It is to root them again in the image of God and to bind them to the vocation to become like Him.
