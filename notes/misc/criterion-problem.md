The **criterion problem** (or **problem of the criterion**) is a fundamental issue in epistemology concerning the justification of knowledge. It arises when trying to determine:

1. **What we know** (a set of true beliefs).
2. **How we know it** (the criteria for determining truth).

The problem is that these two questions are **circularly dependent**:

- To identify what counts as knowledge, we need a criterion for knowledge.
- But to justify the criterion itself, we need to already have knowledge.

## Example of the Dilemma

Suppose someone asks, *“How do you know reality exists?”* You might answer, *“Because my senses confirm it.”* But if they then ask, *“How do you know your senses are reliable?”* you might say, *“Because they have always worked.”*  
This response **assumes** that past experiences establish truth, but that assumption itself needs justification, leading to an infinite regress or circular reasoning.

## Responses to the Problem

1. **Skepticism** – Rejects the possibility of certainty in knowledge.
2. **Coherentism** – Justifies knowledge within a self-supporting system of beliefs.
3. **Foundationalism** – Argues that some basic beliefs are self-evident and need no further justification.

The criterion problem challenges how we justify **any** claim without falling into circularity, infinite regress, or arbitrary assumptions.
