title: The Fifth Ecumenical Council

---

The Fifth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople II, 553) primarily dealt with Christological controversies arising from misunderstandings of Chalcedon and aimed to reconcile differing factions within the Church. While the council did not explicitly address the issue of Christ's wills (which would come later at the Sixth Ecumenical Council), it is significant for reaffirming the unity of Christ’s person and the complete integrity of His two natures, as taught by the Council of Chalcedon.

The Fifth Council strongly upheld the teaching that there is **one person (hypostasis)** of the Word, who is both fully God and fully man. It condemned any attempt to separate or confuse Christ's two natures. By doing so, the council laid the groundwork for the later affirmation of **two wills (dyothelitism)** at the Sixth Council, because if Christ has two complete natures, He must also have the properties of each nature, including two wills.

The council also condemned the writings and persons associated with Nestorianism, which implied a division of Christ into two persons, and further clarified that Christ’s divine and human operations are united in the one divine hypostasis. This clarification was essential for maintaining that Christ’s actions—whether divine (e.g., miracles) or human (e.g., eating, suffering)—are always the actions of the same person, the incarnate Word.

While the Fifth Council did not explicitly address monothelitism, its teachings implicitly reject any doctrine that denies the completeness of Christ’s humanity, which would include a human will. It prepared the theological groundwork for the Sixth Ecumenical Council (681), which would definitively affirm dyothelitism as the correct understanding of Christ’s wills. Thus, the Fifth Council is part of the broader trajectory of affirming the fullness of both Christ’s divinity and humanity in one person.
