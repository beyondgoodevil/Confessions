The *Phaedrus* is the only dialogue where Socrates leaves Athens for the countryside. He spends a summer afternoon under a plane tree by the Ilissus with a young man who loves speeches. In the first half there are three speeches about love: two against the lover and one, the famous **palinode**, in praise of him. The palinode contains the image of the soul as a **charioteer with two winged horses**. The second half drops love and asks what makes a speech good or bad. It ends with the myth of **Theuth** and an attack on **writing**, which is strange coming from the greatest prose writer in Greek. Readers have argued since antiquity about whether these halves belong together. This note goes through the dialogue in order, quoting Nehamas and Woodruff's translation, and then turns to that question with Cobb, Nicholson, Rosen and the Neoplatonist **Hermias**.

### 1. The Setting: A Book Under the Cloak

Phaedrus has spent the morning with **Lysias**, the speechwriter, son of Cephalus (the old man of *Republic* I). He is going for a walk outside the walls to practise a speech Lysias has written. Socrates calls himself "sick with passion for hearing speeches" (228b) and spots the book hidden in Phaedrus' left hand under his cloak (228d–e). They wade barefoot along the Ilissus and stop under a tall plane tree beside a spring sacred to Achelous and the Nymphs, with the cicadas singing (230b–c).

Two things happen on the way that matter later. Phaedrus asks if Socrates believes the story of Boreas carrying off Orithuia. Socrates says that explaining myths away rationally is work for a clever man he does not envy. He has no time for it, because "I am still unable, as the Delphic inscription orders, to know myself" (229e–230a). He would rather ask whether he is "a beast more complicated and savage than Typhon" or something tamer with a share in a divine nature (230a). Cobb points out that Socrates is already *using* a myth here: Typhon becomes a question about his own soul. That is what Socrates means by believing a myth, and the palinode will work the same way (Cobb, pp. 142–3).

The second thing is Socrates' explanation of why he never leaves town: "landscapes and trees have nothing to teach me—only the people in the city can do that" (230d). Phaedrus has lured him out the way you lead a hungry animal with a branch, by waving a book at him (230d–e). So the dialogue starts with Socrates out of place and in the grip of a written text, and it ends with a critique of written texts.

### 2. Lysias' Speech: The Case for the Non-Lover

Lysias' speech (230e–234c) argues that a boy should give his favours to a man who is *not* in love with him. The lover will regret his generosity when his desire fades. He is jealous, cuts the boy off from friends, praises him only to flatter him, and is "more sick than sound in the head". The non-lover is in control of himself, keeps no accounts, and can be discreet. It is a cold, clever argument, and it is really a seduction. Hermias, and Rosen after him, read it as the speech of a lover pretending not to be one.

Socrates says the speech put him in a "Bacchic frenzy", which is ironic. His real complaint is that Lysias says the same thing two or three times (235a). Sappho or Anacreon, he says, did better, and he feels "filled, like an empty jar" with something better from another source (235c–d). Phaedrus swears by the plane tree that he will not read Socrates another speech unless Socrates gives one himself (236d–e).

### 3. Socrates' First Speech, With His Head Covered

Socrates speaks with his head covered for shame (237a). His speech is better built than Lysias'. It begins with a **definition**, because you cannot advise someone about a thing until you agree what it is (237b–d). In each of us there are two ruling principles: an "inborn desire for pleasures" and an "acquired judgment that pursues what is best" (237d). When desire drags us irrationally toward pleasure, it is called *hubris*. When it is desire for bodily beauty, it is called *erōs*, from *rhōmē*, "force" (238c). The lover will therefore keep his boy weak, soft, ignorant and cut off from family, money and "divine philosophy", and will break his promises once his love cools (238e–241c). The speech ends: "Do wolves love lambs? That's how lovers befriend a boy!" (241d).

Cobb thinks this is "good rhetoric with a bad content" (p. 146). The form is what the later discussion of rhetoric will praise. But the psychology, where desire overpowers judgment, is not the usual Socratic one, and Socrates soon calls the speech "foolish, and close to being impious" (242d).

### 4. The Daimonion and the Palinode

As he is about to cross the river, Socrates' **divine sign** stops him (242b–c). He realises that both speeches insulted Love, who is the son of Aphrodite and a god, and so "can't be bad in any way" (242d–e). He must purify himself the way the poet **Stesichorus** did. Stesichorus was struck blind for slandering Helen, and he got his sight back by writing a recantation, a *palinode*: "There's no truth to that story" (243a–b). Socrates will now speak "with my head bare, no longer covered in shame" (243b). He says that the speech is by Stesichorus son of Euphemus of Himera. The translators note that this means roughly "son of Good Speaker, from the Land of Desire" (244a).

Cobb notices that in the *Symposium* Diotima makes Eros a *daimōn*, something between god and human, while here he is simply a god. Rosen builds his whole reading on this difference (see section 18).

### 5. Four Kinds of Divine Madness

The first two speeches assumed that madness is bad and self-control good. Socrates replies: "the best things we have come from madness, when it is given as a gift of the god" (244a). He lists four kinds, and later (265b) assigns each to a god:

| Kind | God | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prophetic | Apollo | The priestesses at Delphi and Dodona, who do good "out of their minds" and little or nothing when sane (244b) |
| Ritual / purificatory | Dionysus | Rites that release families from guilt for ancient crimes (244d–e) |
| Poetic | The Muses | Whoever tries to be a poet by skill alone "will fail, and his self-controlled verses will be eclipsed by the poetry of men who have been driven out of their minds" (245a) |
| Erotic | Aphrodite and Eros | "the madness of love is the best" (265b) |

To support the first kind, Socrates adds a joke etymology: the old word for prophecy was *manikē*, "madness", and people later inserted a *t* to make it *mantikē* (244c).

The praise of poetic madness is surprising after *Republic* X, and Cobb notes that the poet is ranked only sixth in the list of lives later in the speech (p. 149). The real question for Cobb is what "madness" means here. Does it mean irrational, or only that the lover departs from normal behaviour because he sees something higher? Socrates later describes the divine kind as "a divinely inspired release from normally accepted behavior" (265a). That supports the second reading: the madman is out of step with convention, not with reason.

### 6. The Self-Mover: A Proof of Immortality

Before the myth Socrates gives a short, dry argument, a proof that "convinces the wise if not the clever" (245c). "Every soul is immortal" (the translators note it may mean "All soul"). Whatever is moved by something else stops living when it stops moving. Only what moves itself never stops, and it is the source of motion for everything else. A source has no beginning, since otherwise it would not be the source. And it cannot be destroyed, or "all heaven and everything that has been started up would collapse, come to a stop" (245c–e). Since being self-moved is the essence of soul, soul has neither birth nor death.

Cobb points out two oddities (pp. 150–1). First, the argument does not fit the myth that surrounds it, since nothing later depends on it. Second, *psuchē* in Greek is the principle of life in general, so if the argument works it proves that the souls of animals and even plants are immortal. This argument is also different from the four in the *Phaedo*. It is closer to the self-moving soul of *Laws* X, and the Neoplatonists made it central.

### 7. The Charioteer and the Two Horses

To say what the soul *is* would be "a task for a god", but "to say what it is like is humanly possible" (246a). So Socrates gives an image. The soul is like "the natural union of a team of winged horses and their charioteer". The gods' horses are all good. Ours are mixed: one is "beautiful and good", the other its opposite, "so chariot-driving in our case is inevitably a painfully difficult business" (246b).

Later (253d–e) the horses are described in full. The good horse is upright, white with black eyes, "a lover of honor with modesty and self-control", and is guided "by verbal commands alone". The bad one is "a crooked great jumble of limbs" with a bull-neck and bloodshot eyes, "deaf as a post", and barely yields to whip and goad together.

This looks like the three-part soul of the *Republic*: reason (the charioteer), spirit (the good horse) and appetite (the bad horse). Cobb notes two differences (p. 152). The good horse never opposes the charioteer, and the *whole* soul, horses included, is immortal here, while the *Republic* and *Timaeus* tie the lower parts to the body. He adds that the image is poetry and should not be pressed into a theory of action.

### 8. The Procession of the Gods and the Place Beyond Heaven

The soul's wings carry heavy things upward to where the gods live, and they are nourished by "beauty, wisdom, goodness" and shrink at ugliness (246d–e). **Zeus** leads a procession of gods and spirits in eleven companies. Hestia stays at home, and anyone who wants may follow, "since jealousy has no place in the gods' chorus" (247a). When they go to feast, they climb to the rim of heaven. The gods' chariots manage easily, but ours are dragged down by the heavy horse.

At the top the souls stand on the back of heaven and are carried round, looking at what lies outside it:

> What is in this place is without color and without shape and without solidity, a being that really is what it is, the subject of all true knowledge, visible only to intelligence, the soul's steersman. (247c)

There the soul sees **Justice**, **Self-control** and **Knowledge**, "not the knowledge that is close to change" but knowledge "of what really is what it is" (247d–e). This is one of the few places where Plato describes the Forms as a *place*, and Socrates admits that no poet has ever praised it enough.

### 9. The Fall, the Nine Lives and Recollection

Most souls cannot keep up. They trample and kick one another below the surface, many are crippled and break their wings, and "they will depend on what they think is nourishment—their own opinions" (248b). A soul that loses its wings falls to earth and takes a body. The law of Destiny ranks the first human lives by how much the soul saw (248d–e):

1. a lover of wisdom or of beauty, "cultivated in the arts and prone to erotic love"
2. a lawful king or warlike commander
3. a statesman, household manager or financier
4. a trainer or doctor
5. a prophet or priest of the mysteries
6. a poet or other representational artist
7. a manual labourer or farmer
8. a sophist or demagogue
9. a tyrant

A soul normally needs ten thousand years to regrow its wings. A philosopher, or someone who "loves boys philosophically", who chooses that life three times running is free after three thousand (248e–249a).

Only a soul that has seen the truth can become human, "since a human being must understand speech in terms of general forms, proceeding to bring many perceptions together into a reasoned unity". That is **recollection** of what the soul saw while travelling with a god (249b–c). This is the same doctrine as in the *Meno* and *Phaedo*, but here it also explains ordinary language: we can use general words at all because we once saw the Forms. The philosopher lives close to those memories, so "ordinary people think he is disturbed and rebuke him for this, unaware that he is possessed by god" (249d). That is the fourth madness.

### 10. Beauty and the Growing of Wings

Why is love the way back? Justice and self-control do not shine through their earthly copies. But "beauty was radiant" (250b). Sight is the sharpest sense, though it "does not see wisdom". If wisdom showed itself as clearly as beauty, it would arouse a terrible love, but "beauty alone has this privilege, to be the most clearly visible and the most loved" (250d–e). Back then, before falling, we saw the vision in pure light, not yet "locked in" the body "like an oyster in its shell" (250c).

Someone who has forgotten responds to beauty like a beast, "eager to make babies" (250e). A recent initiate who sees "a godlike face" shudders, feels awe, and would sacrifice to the boy "as if he were the image of a god" (251a). Then come the most physical pages Plato wrote. The stream of beauty enters through the eyes, warms the soul and softens the hard places where the wings once grew. The feathers swell and push through, so the whole soul "seethes and throbs" like a child's gums when teeth are coming in (251b–c). Apart from the boy it aches. In his presence it is relieved. The lover forgets "mother and brothers and friends", does not care if he loses his money, and will "sleep like a slave" as near the beloved as he can, because the boy is "the only doctor" for his pain (252a–b).

Each lover chooses a beloved who resembles the god he once followed. Followers of Zeus look for a boy with "a talent for philosophy". Then, with "no envy", they try to draw him into being like themselves and their god (252c–253c). Plato's lover wants to *make the beloved better*, not to possess him. This is the same idea as Diotima's "begetting in beauty", told as myth instead of as a ladder.

### 11. The Lovers: Taming the Bad Horse

When the charioteer sees the beloved's face, the bad horse lunges forward, wanting sex. The other two resist and then give way. Then:

> When the charioteer sees that face, his memory is carried back to the real nature of Beauty, and he sees it again where it stands on the sacred pedestal next to Self-control. (254b)

He falls back in awe and wrenches the reins so hard that both horses sit down on their haunches. The bad horse curses them as cowards and tries again. Each time the charioteer yanks the bit until he "bloodies its foul-speaking tongue and jaws", until finally the horse "dies of fright" when it sees the boy (254c–e). The violence is the point: in this image, self-control comes from beauty and memory.

The beloved, in turn, is struck by the overflow of beauty coming back to him "as in a mirror". He loves without knowing what he loves and calls it friendship, though it is really "backlove" (*anterōs*) (255c–e). Two outcomes follow:

- If "the better elements" win, the pair live by "the assigned regimen of philosophy" without consummating their love. They grow wings at death and win "the first of three rounds in these, the true Olympic Contests" (256a–b).
- If they live by "ambition in place of philosophy", they give in now and then, "sparingly", because they do not approve with their whole minds. They still stay friends for life and will grow wings together in time (256c–e).

The non-lover's friendship, by contrast, is "diluted by human self-control" and pays only "cheap, human dividends" (256e). Cobb is struck by how harshly Socrates treats sex in a speech that is otherwise so openly erotic (pp. 153–5). The *Symposium* never says this so clearly, though Socrates' refusal of Alcibiades points the same way.

The speech ends with a prayer to Love: may Lysias be turned to philosophy like his brother **Polemarchus**, so that Phaedrus can "devote his life to Love through philosophical discussions" (257b).

### 12. The Cicadas

Phaedrus says politicians are ashamed to be called "speech writers". Socrates points out that every decree begins with the names of its admirers ("Resolved by the Council...") and concludes that "It's not speaking or writing well that's shameful" but doing it badly (258d). Then he notices the noon heat and tells the **myth of the cicadas** (258e–259d). The cicadas were once humans who lived before the Muses were born. When song was invented, they were so enchanted that they forgot to eat and died. The Muses let them sing without food until death, and afterwards they report to each Muse who honoured her. To **Calliope** and **Urania**, the eldest, who sing of heaven and of all speech, "they report those who honor their special kind of music by leading a philosophical life" (259d). So philosophy is the highest music, and the two men should keep talking instead of dozing like sheep.

### 13. Rhetoric as Leading the Soul

The question now is what makes a speech good. Phaedrus has heard that an orator needs to know only what will *seem* just to the crowd, not what *is* just (260a). Socrates answers with a joke. Suppose neither of us knew what a horse was, and I praised the donkey to you as a horse that is useful in war. That would be ridiculous. It is far worse when an orator who does not know good from bad persuades a city to do evil (260b–d). Rhetoric is "a way of directing the soul by means of speech" (*psuchagōgia*), in private as much as in court (261a).

The argument against the cynics is clever. Deceiving someone means leading him in small steps through similarities from the truth to its opposite. Zeno, the "Eleatic Palamedes", can make the same things look like and unlike, one and many (261d). But you can only lead someone through similarities if you know what the things really are. So even a liar needs the truth, and "the art of a speaker who doesn't know the truth and chases opinions instead is likely to be a ridiculous thing—not an art at all!" (262c).

Socrates then examines the three speeches. Words like "iron" and "silver" mean the same to everyone, but "just" and "good" pull us in different directions, so rhetoric works best on disputed things, love among them (263a–c). Lysias failed to define love. He also begins from the end, so that his speech swims "upstream on its back" (264a), and his parts could be shuffled like the verses on Midas' tomb. The rule is: "Every speech must be put together like a living creature, with a body of its own" (264c). Readers have used this sentence against the *Phaedrus* itself ever since.

### 14. Collection and Division

Looking back at his two speeches, Socrates finds in them two procedures that were not just play (265c–266b):

- **Collection**: "seeing together things that are scattered about everywhere and collecting them into one kind", so that a definition makes the subject clear.
- **Division**: being able "to cut up each kind according to its species along its natural joints, and to try not to splinter any part, as a bad butcher might do".

Both speeches put madness into one kind and then cut it in two, like a body with left and right sides. The first followed the left-hand side down to a "left-handed" love and condemned it. The second followed the right-hand side and found a divine love with the same name. Socrates says: "I am myself a lover of these divisions and collections, so that I may be able to think and to speak" (266b), and the people who can do this he calls **dialecticians**.

Cobb thinks this is the most solid teaching in the dialogue: "nothing is a less ambiguous candidate than this notion of the principles of dialectic" (p. 161). He also argues against Hackforth that it is not a rigid method of splitting everything in two. It is a general habit of looking for unity and difference, the method the late dialogues (*Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Philebus*) go on to practise at length.

### 15. The True Rhetoric: Knowing Souls

Phaedrus lists what the rhetoric handbooks contain: Preamble, Statement of Facts, Proof, Refutation, the tricks of Theodorus, Evenus, Polus and Thrasymachus, and the teaching of Tisias and Gorgias that "what is likely must be held in higher honor than what is true" (266d–267d). Socrates says these are only the *preliminaries* of the art. A man who knows how to make a body hot or cold is not a doctor if he does not know when to do it. Someone who can write long and short speeches is not Sophocles. Knowing the highest and lowest notes is not harmony (268a–269c).

The real model is **medicine**: "In both cases we need to determine the nature of something—of the body in medicine, of the soul in rhetoric" (270b). So the true orator must (271a–272b):

1. know whether the soul is simple or complex;
2. know what it acts on and is acted on by;
3. classify the kinds of soul and the kinds of speech, and match them;
4. recognise in real life which kind of person is standing in front of him;
5. know the right moment to speak and to keep silent.

"Since the nature of speech is in fact to direct the soul, whoever intends to be a rhetorician must know how many kinds of soul there are" (271d). This shows why the palinode was really about the soul: it is the psychology a true rhetoric would need. And the reason to make this huge effort is "not in order to speak and act among human beings" but to please the gods, our masters, who are "wholly good" (273e–274a).

### 16. Theuth and Thamus: The Critique of Writing

The last question is about writing. Socrates tells a story from Egypt (274c–275b). The god **Theuth** invented number, geometry, astronomy, draughts and dice, and above all writing. He brought them to King **Thamus** and called writing "a potion for memory and for wisdom". The word is *pharmakon*, which means both drug and poison. Thamus replied that the inventor of an art is not the best judge of it:

> You have not discovered a potion for remembering, but for reminding; you provide your students with the appearance of wisdom, not with its reality. (275a)

Phaedrus remarks that Socrates can make up stories from Egypt whenever he likes. Socrates answers that people once listened to an oak at Dodona if it spoke the truth, so why should it matter who is speaking (275b–c)?

Then he gives the critique proper (275d–e). Writing is like painting: its offspring "stand there as if they are alive, but if anyone asks them anything, they remain most solemnly silent". If you question a written text, "it continues to signify just that very same thing forever". It roams everywhere, reaches the wrong readers as well as the right ones, and when attacked "it always needs its father's support". Its "legitimate brother" is a discourse "written down, with knowledge, in the soul of the listener", which can defend itself and knows when to be silent. Phaedrus calls it "the living, breathing discourse of the man who knows" (276a).

A sensible farmer does not plant his serious seeds in a "garden of Adonis", a pot forced to bloom within seven days for a festival. The man who knows justice will likewise "sow gardens of letters for the sake of amusing himself", storing up reminders for his old age (276b–d). His serious work is dialectic: planting words in a fit soul, where they produce seed that makes the word "forever immortal" (276e–277a). Whoever writes a law believing it holds clear and lasting knowledge deserves reproach, "even if the crowd praises it with one voice" (277d–e).

### 17. Plato Writing Against Writing

So the greatest writer in Greek wrote, in a polished written work, that no writing deserves serious attention. Nicholson collects the main ways out (ch. 4, pp. 75–88):

- **He meant it.** The *Seventh Letter* says the most important things cannot be put into words like other subjects. They come suddenly in the soul, like light from a spark, after long shared study, and Plato says there is and will be no writing of his on them (341c–e). This fits the Tübingen school's idea of an **unwritten doctrine** taught orally in the Academy, which Aristotle seems to refer to (*Physics* 209b). Nicholson is cautious about it: having an oral practice does not mean having a secret second philosophy (pp. 81–4).
- **Dialogue is the exception.** Ronna Burger and Charles Griswold argue that the critique hits treatises and speeches, not dialogues. A dialogue, with its characters, irony and lack of stated conclusions, does not just keep saying the same thing. It forces the reader to answer. Cobb agrees that the dialogue form is a "compromise between the written and the living forms of speech" (p. 167). Nicholson objects that the Theuth story attacks writing as such (pp. 79–80).
- **Derrida.** In "Plato's Pharmacy" Derrida argues that the *pharmakon* undoes Plato's attempt to put speech above writing, because the word means both remedy and poison. Nicholson thinks the essay wanders far from the dialogue but admits that its starting point, the awkwardness of a "logocentric" philosophy in written form, is fair (pp. 80–1).

Nicholson's own suggestion is the neatest. Socrates' final test (278c) says the writer earns the name *philosopher* if he can defend his writing and show that it is worth less than living speech. The *Phaedrus* passes that test: it is a written text that argues for the inferiority of written texts (p. 79). And Socrates calls all of it, the hymn to Love included, "play" (265c, 278b). Cobb warns that we may miss "the playfulness of this claim about the playfulness of written documents" (p. 167).

### 18. What Is the Dialogue About?

The ancients already asked this. Hermias, writing in the fifth century AD, reports the candidates: rhetoric (judging from the beginning and end), the soul (because of the immortality proof), love (because of the first half), theology, the Good, or primary Beauty. Each, he says, takes a part for the whole. Since Socrates himself demands that a speech be "like a single living thing", there must be one aim, and following **Iamblichus**, Hermias says the aim "concerns beauty of every kind" (Hermias 9–12 Couvreur). The dialogue climbs from the visible beauty of Phaedrus, to the beauty of Lysias' speech, to the beauty of souls and sciences, to the gods and beauty itself, and then comes back down through division to beautiful speeches, "joining the end to the beginning". Hermias even reads the scenery this way: the tall plane tree, the chaste-tree and the grass are levels of the ascent. He also explains why Eros is a god here and a *daimōn* in the *Symposium*: there love itself was the subject, here love is only the power that leads everything up to the beautiful.

Modern scholars give different answers (Cobb, pp. 139–41):

- **Hackforth**: the dialogue defends philosophy and proposes a reformed rhetoric based on collection and division. The love between teacher and pupil is a shared love of truth.
- **Rowe**: the lover and beloved of the palinode are a picture of the philosophical teacher and pupil. Rowe adds that a split dialogue would be "intolerable in a work in which Socrates specifically complains about an author (Lysias) for failing to put together an organic composition".
- **Griswold**: the theme is **self-knowledge**, which is what Socrates says at 229e he lacks and what Phaedrus never has.
- **Arieti**: none of the speeches about love is meant seriously. Even the palinode shows how a clever speaker can make bad ideas sound good.

**Rosen** starts from the *Symposium*. In that dialogue the speakers try to explain love "from within the horizon of genesis", from becoming itself, and they fail. Even Diotima's ladder needs a guide who has already seen the top. The *Phaedrus* answers with a revelation from above: "the way up is not the same as, and in fact is posterior to, the way down" (ch. 5). That is why Eros is promoted from *daimōn* to god, and why the decisive thing in the *Phaedrus* is *madness*, which comes from outside, rather than *erōs* alone. In Rosen's reading the non-lover and the "concealed lover" of the first two speeches show what happens when intelligence is made the servant of the body. Calculation becomes mere efficiency, and the sober man ends up a slave to his desires.

These answers fit together reasonably well. Rhetoric is "directing the soul", so a true rhetoric needs a true account of the soul, and the palinode supplies one (in images, since a literal account is "a task for a god"). The lover who wants to make his beloved like his god is the model of the good rhetorician, and Socrates spends the whole afternoon trying to do the same with Phaedrus.

### 19. The Prayer to Pan

The heat dies down, and before leaving Socrates prays to the local gods:

> O dear Pan and all the other gods of this place, grant that I may be beautiful inside. Let all my external possessions be in friendly harmony with what is within. May I consider the wise man rich. As for gold, let me have as much as a moderate man could bear and carry with him. (279b–c)

Phaedrus asks him to make the prayer for him too: "Friends have everything in common." The first half also ended with a prayer, to Love. It is fitting that the end is a prayer for inner beauty, given what Hermias said the dialogue is about. The last words also bring back friendship, the theme Cobb sees running from the opening question (where has Phaedrus come from, and where is he going?) to the end (p. 141).

### Conclusion

The *Phaedrus* is two things at once. It is Plato's richest account of love: a madness sent by the gods that begins with a beautiful face and, if the charioteer holds the reins, regrows the soul's wings. It is also his most careful account of speech, from the empty tricks of the handbooks to a true rhetoric that knows its subject and its listener's soul. What links them is the soul. Love moves it toward beauty, and speech leads it to the truth or away from it. The final critique of writing is less a contradiction than a warning to readers of this very book not to take the dialogue for the knowledge it points to. Hermias' reading still seems right to me: the dialogue climbs from a pretty speech to beauty itself and comes back down to show how one should talk.
