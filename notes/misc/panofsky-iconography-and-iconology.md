What does a painting **mean**? Erwin Panofsky's answer, set out in the introduction to *Studies in Iconology* (1939), is that meaning comes in **three layers**, and each layer needs a different kind of knowledge and a different historical check. The method became the standard toolkit of art history: identify what's shown, then what story or allegory it is, then what it reveals about the mind of its age. Here's how it works:

### 1. The Man Lifting His Hat

Panofsky starts not with a painting but with the street. An acquaintance greets him by removing his hat (pp. 3–5).

1. **Formally**, all I see is a change in a pattern of colours, lines and volumes.
2. **Factual meaning.** As soon as I recognise "an object (gentleman)" and "an event (hat-removing)," I've passed beyond pure form. I know this from practical experience.
3. **Expressional meaning.** From the *way* he does it I can sense whether he's in a good mood, friendly or hostile. This comes by "empathy," but it's still everyday experience. Factual plus expressional = **primary or natural** meaning.
4. **Conventional meaning.** Knowing that hat-lifting is a **greeting** is different. It's a Western custom, a leftover of medieval knights removing helmets to show peaceful intent. An outsider to the culture, or an ancient Greek, wouldn't get it. This is **secondary or conventional** meaning: "intelligible instead of being sensible," and consciously intended by the man.
5. **Intrinsic meaning.** Finally, the gesture can reveal his whole **personality**: a twentieth-century man of a certain nation, class and education, with his own way of seeing the world. No single gesture shows this fully, but every gesture shows it "symptomatically." This is **intrinsic meaning or content**, and it lies as far above conscious intention as the expressional meaning lies beneath it.

### 2. The Three Strata in a Work of Art

Transferred to art (pp. 5–8):

| | Object of interpretation | Act of interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Primary or natural subject matter (factual, expressional): the world of **motifs** | Pre-iconographical description |
| **II** | Secondary or conventional subject matter: the world of **images, stories and allegories** | Iconographical analysis |
| **III** | Intrinsic meaning or content: the world of **"symbolical" values** | Iconographical interpretation in a deeper sense (iconology) |

1. **Motifs.** Lines and colours read as people, animals, houses, tools; their relations as events; and expressional qualities such as "the mournful character of a pose." Listing these is a **pre-iconographical description**.
2. **Images.** Realising that "a male figure with a knife represents St. Bartholomew," a woman with a peach is Veracity, thirteen men at a table in a certain arrangement are the **Last Supper**. Here motifs are connected with **themes or concepts**. This is iconography in the narrow sense, and it presupposes the first step: if the knife is really a corkscrew, it's not St Bartholomew (pp. 6–7).
3. **Intrinsic meaning.** The "underlying principles which reveal the basic attitude of a nation, a period, a class, a religious or philosophical persuasion," condensed into one work. His example: from about 1310 the Nativity with the Virgin **lying in bed** is often replaced by the Virgin **kneeling** before the Child. Compositionally that's a triangle replacing a rectangle; iconographically it's a new theme from texts like Pseudo-Bonaventure and St Bridget; but at the deepest level it reveals "a new emotional attitude" of the late Middle Ages (p. 7).

> [!definition] Iconology
> Treating a work not as a thing with its own properties but as a **symptom** of "something else" (Leonardo's personality, the High Renaissance, a religious attitude) that also shows itself in countless other symptoms. These "symbolical" values, in Ernst Cassirer's sense, are "generally unknown to the artist himself" (p. 8).

- **A note on the word.** In the 1939 table the third level is still "iconographical interpretation in a deeper sense." The book's title and the later reprint of the essay in *Meaning in the Visual Arts* (1955) call it **iconology**. Hatt and Klonk note the word itself goes back to Cesare Ripa's emblem book *Iconologia* (1613), where allegorical images came with explanatory texts (p. 98).

### 3. Every Level Needs a Historical Check

Panofsky's key move is that our equipment at each level can mislead us if applied "indiscriminately." So each level gets a **controlling principle** (pp. 9–16):

| Equipment | Controlling principle |
|---|---|
| Practical experience | **History of style**: how objects and events were expressed by forms |
| Knowledge of literary sources | **History of types**: how themes and concepts were expressed by objects and events |
| Synthetic intuition | **History of cultural symptoms**: how the essential tendencies of the human mind were expressed by themes and concepts |

1. **Style: what's floating?** In Roger van der Weyden's *Three Magi*, a child hovers in the sky, so we read it as an **apparition**. But in an Ottonian miniature from the *Gospels of Otto III*, the whole city of Nain hangs in empty space, and it's not a miracle at all: around 1000, the background just wasn't thought of as real three-dimensional space. Even the "simplest" description depends on knowing a picture's historical "locus" (pp. 9–11).
2. **Types: Judith or Salome?** Francesco Maffei painted a young woman with a **sword** in one hand and a man's head on a **charger** in the other. The Bible gives Salome the charger but not the sword, and Judith the sword but a sack, not a charger. Texts alone can't decide. But there are several sixteenth-century pictures of "Judith with a charger" and not one Salome with a sword, so it's **Judith**. Why the transfer went one way: the sword was an honourable attribute (of Judith, martyrs, Justice) and couldn't go to "a lascivious girl," while the Baptist's head on a charger had become a popular devotional image (pp. 12–14).
3. **Symptoms: intuition under control.** For intrinsic meaning there's no single text, so we need something like a diagnostician's "synthetic intuition," which "may be better developed in a talented layman than in an erudite scholar" (p. 15). Because it's the most subjective faculty, it needs the most control: checking the meaning we find against politics, poetry, religion and philosophy of the same period. That's where "the various humanistic disciplines meet on a common plane" (p. 16).

- **The circle.** Interpreting single works by a history of style that is itself built from single works looks circular. Panofsky admits it is a circle, "though not a vicious, but a methodical one" (p. 11, n. 3). Compare Heidegger's circle of artist, work and art in [[Heidegger's Origin of the Work of Art and the Quarrel Over Van Gogh's Shoes]].
- **One process.** The table makes three neat steps, but in practice they "merge with each other into one organic and indivisible process" (p. 17).

### 4. The Renaissance: Reuniting Motif and Theme

The second half of the introduction applies the method to the Renaissance's own question, the rebirth of antiquity (pp. 18–31).

- **The medieval split.** At the height of the Middle Ages, **classical motifs** were used for Christian themes, and **classical themes** were drawn with non-classical figures, but almost never together. On St Mark's in Venice a thirteenth-century carver copied a Roman relief of Hercules carrying the boar, but turned it into an allegory of salvation. Meanwhile illuminators showed Aeneas and Dido as a fashionable couple playing chess, and Laocoön as a bald old man in contemporary dress (pp. 18–20).
- **Why.** Partly because myths reached the Middle Ages through **texts** (Servius, Fulgentius, the *Mythographus III*, the moralized Ovids, where Pyramus is Christ and Thisbe the soul), while motifs came through copied images. But Panofsky shows it happened even when a visual tradition existed: Carolingian copies of classical gods were dropped by the thirteenth century, and Mercury became a bishop and Jupiter a judge with gloves (pp. 21–26). (On the moralized gods, see [[How Philosophers Saved Homer: Allegory from the Stoics to the Byzantines]].)
- **No sense of distance.** Antiquity was "too far removed and at the same time too strongly present" to be seen historically. Just as the Middle Ages couldn't build perspective, which needs a fixed distance between eye and object, they couldn't build the modern idea of history, which needs an intellectual distance between present and past (pp. 27–28).
- **Emotional disparity.** Classical art showed body and soul as a unity; for the Christian Middle Ages, classical formulas of bodily beauty and passion were only acceptable when given a more-than-natural meaning. Compare a fourteenth-century *Ovide moralisé* Europa, sitting on her bull "like a young lady taking a morning ride," with Dürer's drawing after Poliziano's verses, all fluttering hair and fear (pp. 28–30).
- **Significance:** The Renaissance reintegrated classical themes with classical motifs "after what might be called a zero hour" (p. 27). But it couldn't simply go back. It made something new, "related and indebted to both" (p. 31).

### 5. Case Studies: Father Time, Blind Cupid, Twin Venuses

The rest of the book shows the method at work. Panofsky calls the key process **pseudomorphosis**: a Renaissance figure looks classical but carries a meaning its classical prototype never had, picked up on its way through the Middle Ages (pp. 70–71).

1. **Father Time.** Old, winged, with a **scythe** and **hourglass**: none of these combinations exist in ancient art. Classical Time was either *Kairos*, the fleeting decisive moment (Opportunity), or a cosmic figure of power and fertility, never decay. The sickle and old age came from **Kronos** (Saturn), the old agricultural god, because his name sounded like *Chronos*, time. Plutarch is the earliest writer to state the identity (pp. 71–73). Saturn's gloomy planetary character then brought in destruction and death.
2. **Blind Cupid.** Classical literature often calls love "blind" (*caecus amor*), but in classical art Cupid is **never** blindfolded (p. 95). The bandage is a medieval symbol for blindness, first seen on **Night** around 975, then on the "benighted" **Synagogue**, on Infidelity and on **Death**. So Blind Cupid "started his career in rather terrifying company" (pp. 110–111). For traditional iconography his blindness put him "on the wrong side of the moral world," opposed to the clear-sighted love of the philosophical poets (p. 109).
3. **Titian's *Sacred and Profane Love*.** Panofsky reads it through Ficino's **"Twin Venuses"** from Plato's *Symposium*. The **Celestial Venus**, motherless daughter of Uranus, stands for eternal intelligible beauty (mother = *mater* = matter, so no mother means no matter); the **Earthly Venus** for the generative force that makes visible beauty. The nude is the celestial one. Cupid, between them, stirs the water in a fountain that is an ancient **sarcophagus** turned into a spring of life (pp. 142, 151–152). Its correct title, he says, would be *Geminae Veneres*. (See [[Emergence of Magic and Neoplatonism in the Renaissance]] and, for the Platonic source, [[Begetting in Beauty: Diotima's Pregnant Souls and Plotinus' On Beauty]].)

### 6. Melencolia I: The Method in Full

Hatt and Klonk walk through Panofsky's famous reading of Dürer's 1514 engraving (pp. 109–114):

1. **Pre-iconographical.** A gloomy winged woman sits with a compass among tools, a polyhedron, a sphere, a sleeping dog and a scribbling child, under a comet and rainbow. Even this description already assumes a place in the history of style (unified Renaissance space, northern engraving).
2. **Iconographical.** The grid on the wall is a **magic square**, a talisman against melancholy. "Melancholia" means the black-bile **humour** of the four temperaments, which medieval art showed as a lazy miser close to *acedia*. Dürer **fuses** this type with the *Typus Geometriae*, the personification of geometry, so we get "Geometry gone melancholy," an **Artist's Melancholy**.
3. **Iconological.** Ficino's Neoplatonism linked melancholy with **genius**. Agrippa of Nettesheim distinguished three stages of inspired melancholy; the first, ruled by **imagination**, belongs to artists and craftsmen, hence "I." So the print is both a general philosophy and "a spiritual self-portrait of Albrecht Dürer."

### 7. Criticisms

Hatt and Klonk group the objections under three heads (pp. 114–118):

1. **Over-intellectualising.** Not that Panofsky ignores emotion, but that the image gets **absorbed into language**. That's why he favours periods when art was tied to theological and literary programmes. W. J. T. Mitchell even says modernism makes sense "as a resistance to Panofsky's iconology."
2. **No real social history.** History for Panofsky is the story of changing **mind–world relations**, not people in conflict. Dürer's turn to Lutheranism appears only as a mental struggle. Panofsky himself saw the danger that iconology might behave "like astrology as opposed to astrography."
3. **Homogenised cultures.** He assumes a period has one coherent world-view the work condenses. Other interpreters with different values find other coherences (Hoffmann's pious, medieval Dürer; Rona Goffen's feminist reading of Titian). Walter Benjamin had already read *Melencolia I* as a field of **fragments** that don't add up.

- **Bann on the method.** Stephen Bann notes that Panofsky's founding example isn't a work of art at all but a street encounter, and that the "innocent eye" of pure form is itself a nineteenth-century idea (Crary). At the iconological level it's unclear whether the work condenses the **artist's** inner life or **society's** character. His test case is the *Allegory of Prudence*: after a long hunt for the three-headed Prudence through reliefs, manuscripts and coins, Panofsky suddenly suggests the heads are Titian, his son and a young relative. Bann's question is how much any of this depends on its being an **oil painting** by a great colourist (Preziosi, pp. 256–259).
- **Still the standard.** Hatt and Klonk conclude that even if we don't share Panofsky's humanist outlook, its coherence "forces us to test our own viewpoints," and that he has been for twentieth-century art history what Hegel was for the nineteenth (p. 118).

### 8. Assessment

- **What's lasting.** The distinction between **seeing**, **identifying** and **interpreting** is genuinely useful, and the controls are the best part: the floating city and the Judith with a charger show how easily practical experience and textual knowledge mislead. Any reading of an old picture starts with Panofsky's first two levels.
- **Where it's weakest.** The third level. "Synthetic intuition" is honest about being subjective, but the critics are right that it tends to find the same humanist struggle everywhere.
- **An Orthodox angle.** In Orthodox usage "iconography" means something different: not the scholar's decoding but the **painting** of icons according to fixed **types**. Panofsky's "history of types" is, for the icon painter, the tradition itself. An icon isn't meant to be a symptom of the painter's personality at all; the painter is supposed to disappear into the type (see [[Theology of the Icon]]). And Panofsky's footnote on the blind Synagogue (p. 27, n. 26) is a reminder that iconography also preserved polemics.

### Conclusion

Panofsky's method reads a picture in three layers: **motifs** we recognise from experience, **images, stories and allegories** we identify from texts, and an **intrinsic meaning** that shows the mind of an age and is usually unknown to the artist. Each layer is checked by its own history (of style, of types, of cultural symptoms). Applied to the Renaissance, it shows how classical motifs and classical themes, split apart in the Middle Ages, were reunited, and how figures like Father Time and Blind Cupid carry medieval meanings in classical dress. Its critics say it turns images into texts and periods into single minds, but nobody reads an old picture without his first two levels.
