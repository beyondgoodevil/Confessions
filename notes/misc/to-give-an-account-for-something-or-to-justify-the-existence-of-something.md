To **give an account for something** or to **justify the existence of something** means to provide a rational explanation or defense of its existence or validity. This typically involves presenting reasons, evidence, or arguments that demonstrate why something is the case or why it exists in the way that it does.

## Example: Justifying the Existence of Logic

In the case of logic, to justify its existence is to explain why logical principles (such as the law of non-contradiction or the law of identity) hold true universally and how they are grounded. This would typically involve showing how logic is indispensable for coherent thought, communication, and reasoning about the world, and how it applies universally, not just in human thinking but in the very structure of reality.

For example, **justifying the existence of logic** might involve the following steps:

- **Explaining its necessity**: Logic is essential for making valid inferences, avoiding contradictions, and organizing thought coherently. Without it, no rational discourse or reasoning would be possible.
- **Exploring its universality**: Logic applies universally and isn't dependent on specific contexts or subjective perceptions. Its principles hold for all rational beings, in all times and places.
- **Grounding its validity**: One could argue that logic is grounded in the nature of reality itself, such that it reflects the way things truly are. For instance, the principle of non-contradiction, which states that something cannot both be and not be at the same time and in the same respect, could be seen as reflecting the very structure of reality.

## Why Saying "We Use Our Brain for Logic" Is Insufficient

Saying "we use our brain for logic" is insufficient because it only describes the **mechanism** by which logic is applied but doesn't explain **why** logic itself exists or why its principles are universally valid.

1. **Descriptive, not explanatory**: Stating that we use our brain for logic only describes an observable fact (that we use cognitive processes to reason) but does not account for why logic itself is necessary or valid. The brain may be involved in the application of logic, but that doesn’t answer the question of **why** logic has the structure it does, or **why** the brain’s processing follows logical laws.
2. **Subjectivity**: If we say that we use our brains for logic, it may suggest that logic is merely a product of human cognition, implying that logic is a subjective construct rather than a universal and objective framework. This would fail to explain why logic applies beyond individual thought or why it is consistent across different cultures, times, and contexts.
3. **Circular reasoning**: Simply saying "we use our brain for logic" could be seen as circular reasoning: it explains the use of logic by pointing to the brain, but it doesn't answer the deeper philosophical question about the **origin** or **necessity** of logical principles themselves. Why do we apply logic in the first place, and why does logic work consistently across all forms of reasoning and reality?

In sum, the justification for logic requires explaining its **necessity**, **universality**, and **grounding**, rather than merely describing the biological process through which it is applied. To give a full account, one must show how and why logical principles are not just human conventions but reflect something fundamental about how we think and how the world is structured.
