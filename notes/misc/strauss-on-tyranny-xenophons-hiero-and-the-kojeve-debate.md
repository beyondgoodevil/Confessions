*On Tyranny* began as a commentary on one of the shortest works of Xenophon, the *Hiero*, a conversation between a Greek tyrant and a visiting poet. Strauss published it in 1948. Alexandre Kojève, the Hegelian who had taught the *Phenomenology* to a generation of Paris intellectuals, reviewed it at length, and Strauss replied. The French edition of 1954 put the three together, and the 2000 Chicago edition adds the surviving letters between the two men from 1932 to 1965, with an introduction by Victor Gourevitch and Michael Roth. The book is therefore four things at once: a translation of Xenophon, a model of close reading, a public debate about whether philosophy can or should rule, and a private friendship between the two thinkers each of whom thought the other held the only serious alternative to his own view.

The note follows that order. Section 1 is the *Hiero* itself. Sections 2–8 are Strauss's study. Sections 9–12 are Kojève's review, and 13–16 Strauss's Restatement. Section 17 covers the letters and section 18 the editors' introduction and the final comparison.

### 1. The Hiero

The dialogue opens with sixteen words of narration: "Simonides the poet came once upon a time to Hiero the tyrant" (1.1). When both have leisure, Simonides asks Hiero to explain something he must know better than the poet. Hiero has lived as a private man and as a tyrant, so he must know how the two lives differ "in human joys and pains" (1.2).

Simonides then lists the pleasures: sights, sounds, smells, food and drink, sex, what the whole body feels, the good and bad things felt by the soul, and sleep (1.4–6). He asserts that the tyrant's pleasure "is multiplied many times over" and his pains far fewer (1.8). Hiero denies it, and the first seven chapters are his case.

- **Sights.** The tyrant cannot travel to festivals; it is not safe for him to go where he is not stronger than those present (1.12).
- **Sounds.** Simonides says tyrants always hear praise, "the sweetest sound". Hiero answers that he knows the silent men think ill of him and suspects the praise is flattery. Simonides concedes that "the sweetest praise comes from those who are free in the highest degree" (1.14–16).
- **Food.** Tyrants are sated and lose the pleasure of hope. Their contrived sour dishes are the desires of "a soft and sick soul" (1.17–25).
- **Sex and love.** Hiero names his one real grievance. He loves the boy Dailochus and wants his favours given freely, but "it is never possible for the tyrant to trust that he is loved" (1.31–37).
- **Peace, friendship, trust, fatherland.** Tyrants live as if in perpetual war with their own subjects. Kin have killed tyrants. The cities put up statues of tyrannicides in their temples (2.7–4.5).
- **The good men.** Hiero fears the brave, "because they might dare something for the sake of freedom; the wise, because they might contrive something; and the just, because the multitude might desire to be ruled by them". He is left with the unjust, the incontinent and the slavish (5.1–2).

In chapter 7 Simonides shifts ground. Honour is what separates real men (*andres*) from other animals, "For no human pleasure seems to come closer to what is divine than the joy connected with honors" (7.3–4). Hiero replies that honours given in fear are no honours, and the tyrant lives "night and day as one condemned by all human beings to die for his injustice" (7.10). Asked why he does not give it up, he says no tyrant can repay what he has taken, and "if it profit any man, Simonides, to hang himself, know ... that I myself find this most profits the tyrant" (7.12–13).

Chapters 8–11 belong to Simonides. A ruler's small kindnesses please more than a private man's (8.1–7). He should do the gratifying things himself and leave the punishing to others, as the archon gives the prizes while others drill the chorus (9.1–4). Prizes for farming, commerce and invention would make the city prosper (9.5–11). The mercenaries should act as a bodyguard for all the citizens (10.4–8). He should spend his own wealth on the city and stop competing with private men in the chariot races (11.1–6). His real contest is against other rulers, for the happiest city: "Consider the fatherland to be your estate, the citizens your comrades, friends your own children, your sons the same as your life" (11.14). The last words promise that "while being happy, you will not be envied for being happy" (11.15). Hiero says nothing.

### 2. Why Read a Forgotten Dialogue

Strauss's introduction starts from the present. "Tyranny is a danger coeval with political life" (p. 22). The classical analysis of it was clear enough to be remembered by generations who never met a tyrant. Yet "when we were brought face to face with tyranny—with a kind of tyranny that surpassed the boldest imagination of the most powerful thinkers of the past—our political science failed to recognize it" (p. 23).

He gives two reasons. Modern tyranny differs from ancient tyranny because it has "technology" and "ideologies" at its disposal, and both presuppose a science aimed at "the conquest of nature" and meant to be popularized (p. 23). The classics knew such a science was possible and refused it. The other reason is the rule against "value judgments". A political scientist bound by it may speak of dictatorship, totalitarianism and the mass state, and may condemn them as a citizen, but as a scientist he must treat the word tyranny as "mythical" (p. 23).

Machiavelli stands behind both. The *Prince* is marked by "deliberate indifference to the distinction between king and tyrant", and the only earlier work on tyranny Machiavelli refers to emphatically is the *Hiero* (p. 24). Strauss's claim is that the teaching of the *Hiero* "comes as near to the teaching of the Prince as the teaching of any Socratic could possibly come", so that the dialogue "marks the point of closest contact between premodern and modern political science" (pp. 24–25). A note sets out the difference: the classics took their bearings by how men ought to live and left the realization of the best order to chance, while Machiavelli took his bearings by how men live and tried to control chance. The result was a lowering of standards and the rise of the "philosophy of history" (p. 106 n. 5).

On method, Strauss rejects historicism, the assumption that all thought belongs to its time. A historicist claims to understand the past better than it understood itself before he has understood it as it understood itself (p. 25). Strauss says he tried to read Xenophon exactly and relied as little as possible on outside information, and adds: "It goes without saying that I never believed that my mind was moving in a larger 'circle of ideas' than Xenophon's mind" (p. 26).

Xenophon was called "the orator" in the manuscripts, and his eclipse in the nineteenth century came with a decline in understanding rhetoric. His rhetoric is Socratic rhetoric, whose most perfect product is the dialogue. It leads the fit to philosophy and keeps the unfit away, on the premise "that not all truths are always harmless. Society will always try to tyrannize thought" (p. 27). The modern danger is a tyranny that, thanks to "the conquest of nature' and in particular of human nature", could become "perpetual and universal" (p. 27).

### 3. Title, Form and Setting

Strauss reads the *Hiero* the way he says a dialogue must be read, with "the greatest possible attention ... to every detail, and especially to the unthematic details" (p. 27).

**The two parts.** Chapters 1–7, about five sevenths of the work, prove the tyrant miserable. Chapters 8–11 prove the beneficent tyrant could be happiest. Read on the surface, the dialogue recommends beneficent tyranny. But Simonides is not Xenophon, and his advice is given to a disheartened tyrant whom he has reason to comfort (pp. 29–30).

**The title.** *Hiero or Tyrannicus* is the only Xenophontic title that joins a proper name to an adjective. The second part matches the technical works, *Hipparchicus*, *Oeconomicus*, *Cynegeticus*, so the work teaches the tyrannical art (p. 31). Its only parallel with an alternative title, *Ways and Means*, shows the Athenian democracy how to stop acting unjustly without changing its regime. Both works correct a faulty regime without replacing it (pp. 31–32). The *Hiero* is the only work in which Xenophon never says "I", and *Ways and Means* the only one that opens with an emphatic "I" (p. 32).

**Why Simonides.** Xenophon could not give the "tyrannical" teaching to Socrates without undoing his own defence of him against the charge of teaching the young to be tyrannical. The *Oeconomicus*, where Socrates teaches the royal art of household management, is the pair: a Basilicus to a Tyrannicus. Socrates was perfectly self-controlled about wealth. Simonides was famous for greed (pp. 32–33). And the form leaves open whether the lessons bore fruit. The tyrant's misery rests on experience, but the beneficent tyrant's happiness "is merely promised—by a poet" (p. 34).

**The opening.** Simonides comes to Hiero, not the reverse, since tyrants avoid travel and the wise wait at the doors of the rich (p. 36). He presents himself as a pupil and proposes the one comprehensive topic on which a wise man could plausibly defer to an unwise tyrant (p. 37). His real purpose, on Strauss's reading, is to make Hiero receptive to advice, and no one rebukes a tyrant to his face. So Simonides adopts the vulgar opinion that the tyrant's life is the most pleasant. Hiero, eager to show that he knows better, refutes it and proves his own misery. "Yet his victory is his defeat" (p. 40).

**Why Hiero takes it seriously.** The tyrant fears the wise, but he does not know what they want. The vulgar think wisdom is the skill of becoming and staying a tyrant. A wise man who praises tyranny looks like a rival or the adviser of one (pp. 41–44). Hiero's indictment is therefore aimed at dissuading Simonides, and it is overstated. Xenophon's own *Hellenica* records tyrants revered by their subjects and tyrannicides honoured only "in most of the Greek cities", and Hiero forgets his own victory at Cumae (pp. 45–47). Simonides plainly thinks Hiero's deepest grievance is the loss of the pleasures of love, which Simonides calls minor (p. 46). Strauss's summary: "by choosing a conversational setting in which the strongest possible indictment of tyranny becomes necessary, he intimates the limited validity of that indictment" (p. 48).

### 4. Silence and the Peripeteia

Strauss divides the conversation into four sections and counts the words. In the first, on bodily pleasures, Simonides speaks about 218 of 1,058 words, and the exchange about food is the only real elenchus in the dialogue, with Hiero in the role of Socrates (pp. 50, 52). In the second, Simonides first dismisses bodily pleasures as trivial, since real men aim at power and wealth, and then falls silent: 28 words out of about 2,000 (p. 52). Hiero fills the gap with the lofty topics Simonides never raised: peace, friendship, trust, fatherland, the company of good men.

The silence works. When Simonides finally speaks, he praises honour and passes over every crime Hiero has just confessed. He mentions the toils and dangers but ignores the moral depravity. That reveals, without a word, "a complete lack of scruple", which both overwhelms Hiero and convinces him that this man could advise a tyrant (pp. 55–56). Strauss draws the lesson:

> Even a perfectly just man who wants to give advice to a tyrant has to present himself to his pupil as an utterly unscrupulous man. (p. 56)

The greatest imitator of the *Hiero* was Machiavelli, and his most shocking sentences may come from understanding this lesson. But Xenophon's teacher shows his freedom from morality "not by speech but by silence", not by preaching immoral principles but by failing to notice moral ones. Xenophon "is more 'politic' than Machiavelli; he refuses to separate 'moderation' (prudence) from 'wisdom' (insight)" (p. 56).

Hiero then says hanging would most profit the tyrant. His excuse for not abdicating, that he cannot make amends, is "manifestly hypocritical"; he could simply flee, as other tyrants have, but he cannot bear to live as a stranger. That attachment to his city is what the wandering poet appeals to (p. 57). The turning point is what Simonides does next. A wise man who can do anything with an interlocutor could have talked Hiero into suicide. Instead he chooses to use "the obedience of a living man" (p. 59). Having shown that he could contrive anything, he shows that he will not. This breaks Hiero's distrust. Hiero answers "at once", the only time either speaker does anything "straightway", and asks his one question, about the mercenaries (pp. 61–63).

Simonides' advice then has two layers. The comfort, the bright picture of happy tyranny, belongs to the conversation, and Strauss judges it "even more rhetorical than Hiero's indictment", since neither the *Hiero* nor the rest of Xenophon names a single happy tyrant (pp. 59–60). The advice itself is Xenophon's. Its centre is a transfer from sexual love to love in general. He who wants to be loved must love first, which is "exactly the same advice which Socrates explicitly gives his companions, nay, which Virtue herself explicitly gives to Heracles" (p. 61). The dialogue ends with "the polite silence in which a Greek tyrant, old in crime and martial glory, could listen to a siren-song of virtue" (p. 64).

The vocabulary bears this out (pp. 64–65). "King" never appears, as "tiranno" never appears in the *Prince*. Simonides never says "law", mentions justice only once, for business dealings, and never mentions courage. "Tyrant" occurs 83 times in the first part and 7 in the second, and "ruling" 4 and 12 times. Words of pleasure and pain drop from 93 to 6, and "noble" and "base" rise from 9 to 15. By his last remark Hiero speaks of a "ruler", not a tyrant.

### 5. The Teaching on Tyranny

The teaching has a pathology and a therapy (p. 66). Several features of the setting are necessary. The pupil must be an actual tyrant, since teaching a potential tyrant would mean teaching injustice, while an actual one can be shown "a way toward lesser injustice" (p. 67). Plato took the opposite path. He gave the indictment of tyranny to Socrates and its praise to unwise men, Polus, Callicles and Thrasymachus, who praise its injustice openly. Xenophon gives the praise to a wise man. No dialogue can treat tyranny without some inconvenience, and both chose so that "the wise man ought to have the last word" (p. 68).

Xenophon's Socrates defines kingship as rule over willing subjects in accordance with law, and tyranny as rule over unwilling subjects according to the ruler's will (*Memorabilia* IV 6.12). Simonides' corrected tyranny is rule over willing subjects, but still without law, so tyranny "is essentially rule without laws, or, more precisely, monarchic rule without laws" (pp. 68–69).

Strauss lists its merits and its limits. The tyrant may be virtuous and happy, the subjects are not disarmed, and the policies on prizes and farming are ones Xenophon approved elsewhere (p. 69). But Simonides avoids "freedom" as carefully as "law": "no laws, no liberty" (p. 69). Treating citizens as comrades does not make them free, since slaves can be companions. Treating the fatherland as one's estate means the subjects have no property rights against the tyrant. Leaving punishment to others is a Machiavellian device, and lavish prizes keep subjects busy with private concerns. The subjects cannot have "equality of honor" (p. 70).

As for virtue, the tyrant rewards "prowess in war" and "justice in contractual relations", not courage and justice simply (9.6). Strauss suggests that under a good tyrant moderation produced by fear takes the place that courage holds in republican virtue. He does not think this need be lower in dignity. Xenophon's two lists of Socrates' virtues leave out courage, and Xenophon admired the younger Cyrus while calling him a "slave" (pp. 71–72).

### 6. Law and Legitimacy

Why would a pupil of Socrates find anything good in tyranny? Socrates identified the just with the legal. But laws depend on the regime that makes them, and the identification cannot tell just from unjust laws. Only Alcibiades in Xenophon raises the Socratic question "What is law?", but Socrates' own conduct toward the Thirty shows he did not hold the identity strictly (pp. 73–74). Some justice exceeds law: ingratitude is unjust but legal. Xenophon offers a fuller definition, on which the just man harms no one and helps everyone who deals with him. If justice is beneficence, the rule of a man who knows how to rule may be more just than law, since he is "a seeing law" and law is blind (p. 74).

Legitimacy fares no better. Only knowledge, not force, fraud, election or inheritance, makes a man a ruler. A tyrant who listens to the wise is "essentially more legitimate" than elected magistrates who do not, and Xenophon's Socrates calls such advisers the tyrant's "allies" (pp. 74–75).

In practice Xenophon thought good tyranny could hardly be realized. He names no actual case. Even the best tyrant needs a teacher, lacks unquestioned authority, and depends on a bodyguard loyal to him rather than the city. So for practical purposes Socrates and Xenophon preferred the rule of law and treated the just as the legal (pp. 75–76). The "tyrannical" teaching "has then a purely theoretical meaning. It is not more than a most forceful expression of the problem of law and legitimacy" (p. 76).

Even so, holding this thesis made its holders imperfect democrats, which helps explain why Socrates was condemned and Xenophon exiled (p. 76). That is why the praise was given to a stranger. Hiero indicts tyranny because at bottom he is a citizen. Socrates was a citizen-philosopher and could never be shown praising tyranny. Simonides plays in Xenophon the part the Eleatic Stranger plays in Plato, who discusses the problem of the rule of law while Plato's Socrates stays silent (pp. 76–77).

### 7. The Two Ways of Life

The dialogue's real subject, Strauss argues, is the old Socratic question of how to live. Since Hiero ends up speaking of "ruling" and Simonides is no ordinary private man, the comparison becomes one between "the political life and the life devoted to wisdom" (p. 79).

**The last sentence.** Simonides promises that the beneficent ruler will be happy without being envied. Strauss first takes this to favour political life: the wise man, Socrates above all, was envied, while the ruler can pay for his superiority by serving all (pp. 82–83). Then he turns it over. Political power, however beneficent, never escapes envy. And if per impossibile the ruler did escape the envy of the many, "he would begin to be envied by the wise. He would be envied for not being envied" (pp. 83–84). Simonides could become dangerous to Hiero only if Hiero followed his advice. The ruler has to serve all his subjects, while Socrates talks only with those he likes: "The wise man alone is free" (p. 84).

**Possession and good.** Simonides calls happiness without envy "the most noble and most blessed possession to be met with among human beings", not the greatest good. For Socrates the greatest good is wisdom, the greatest good for human beings is education, and the best possession is a good friend (pp. 84–85). In the one passage where Simonides sketches the relation of teaching to ruling, the ruler's job is to honour those who carry out what the wise teach: "The wise man is the ruler of rulers" (p. 86). The action shows the same thing. In the *Oeconomicus* the wise man hands the lead to the ruler Ischomachus. In the *Hiero* the ruler hands it to the wise man (pp. 86–87).

**Love and honour.** Simonides praises honour. Hiero wants above all to be loved. Hiero's desire is "fundamentally the same as the erotic desire for the common people" that Plato's Socrates finds in Callicles, and only such a desire makes a man willing to serve all his subjects (p. 88). The wise man wants admiration from those "free in the highest degree". Strauss then sets out the difference:

- The beneficent ruler is loved by his subjects but admired even by his enemies. "The range of love is more limited than that of admiration or praise."
- Love needs proximity in space and time. A man is admired long after those who loved him are dead.
- Love's limits usually match the city's borders. Admiration of excellence has none.
- Love is roused by benefits. Admiration is "less mercenary".
- "Admiration is as much superior to love as the man of excellence is to one's benefactor as such." Love "has no criterion of its relevance outside itself, but admiration has" (p. 89).

The wise man is admired for what he is, not for services, and is "as self-sufficient as is humanly possible" (p. 90). Even the detail that Simonides cares for food and Hiero for sex fits: one does not need other people to enjoy food (p. 90). The ruler needs enemies, and Hiero puts the pleasure of killing enemies at the centre of his list of war's pleasures. Simonides shows no delight in war. Because the wise man does not need men as the ruler does, his attitude toward them is free and not passionate. So "the wise man alone is capable of justice in the highest sense", a transpolitical justice that harms no one (p. 91).

### 8. Pleasure, Virtue and Piety

**Pleasure.** Compared with Isocrates' *To Nicocles*, which urges every virtue on the young king, the *Hiero* contains almost no moral exhortation (p. 93). Simonides recommends virtue as a means to pleasure. The historical Simonides wrote that not even the life of gods is to be envied without pleasure, advised play over seriousness, and took money for his wisdom, so Xenophon's choice fits him (pp. 93–94). Yet he is not a hedonist in the strict sense. His opening list distinguishes the good from the pleasant, and his standard is the pleasure that suits the nature of real men, a level set "by the hierarchy of beings" (pp. 95–96).

**Friendship and fatherland.** Hiero ranks friendship above the city. The fatherland is "worth very much" for safety, which is the condition of every pleasure but not itself pleasant, and the city cannot exist without compulsion (pp. 96–97). Simonides agrees in deed by living as a stranger. Xenophon agreed too: he left Athens to join Cyrus, an enemy of Athens, wanted to found a city "in some barbarian place", and marched with Agesilaus against Athens (p. 98). Strauss's explanation is the core of the book:

> We have stated that according to that teaching beneficent tyranny is theoretically superior and practically inferior to rule of laws and legitimate government. (p. 99)

A pupil of Socrates must believe "that nothing which is practically false can be theoretically true". So the thesis does not solve the problem of the best regime. It serves "the purpose ... of bringing to light the nature of political things": legal justice is imperfect and blind, and legitimate government is almost never government by the wise. Hence "the best city is morally and intellectually on a lower plane than the best individual". This "individualism" lies at the bottom of Xenophon's cosmopolitanism (p. 99).

**Socrates and hedonism.** Socrates distinguished common or political virtue, whose ends are wealth and honour, from true virtue, which is self-sufficient wisdom. In his last conversation he says the highest pleasure is the awareness of one's progress in wisdom. "No man can be simply wise; therefore, not wisdom, but progress toward wisdom is the highest good for man" (p. 101). That awareness is a reasonable self-admiration. So Simonides and Socrates differ mainly in that Socrates does not care to be admired by others (pp. 101–102). The *Hiero* poses the problem of pleasure and virtue in its most radical form: whether virtue can be reduced to the desire for the highest pleasure (p. 102).

**Piety.** *Ways and Means* ends with the advice to consult Dodona and Delphi. The *Hiero* never mentions piety. Since Socrates defined piety as knowledge of the laws about the gods, a rule without laws has no place for it, though Xenophon also knows a piety that comes from contemplating the purposive order of nature (pp. 103–104). Where Ischomachus in the *Oeconomicus* speaks of the god and the law working together, Hiero speaks of nature and the law. For Ischomachus the law praises; for Hiero it compels. "The law assumes a higher dignity if the universe is of divine origin" (p. 105).

### 9. Kojève: Simonides the Intellectual

Kojève begins by saying the book matters for the problem it raises, not for what it shows about Xenophon. Strauss reads like a detective, but the book "cannot end as detective novels do, with the unmasked 'criminal's' confession" (p. 136 n.).

His first criticism is that Simonides gave useless advice. He never told Hiero how to undo his unpopular measures without endangering his life and his state. A tyrant always has "current business" that cannot be dropped, and some of it may take centuries to finish (p. 137). By painting an ideal with no first step, Simonides behaved "not so much like a wise man as like a typical 'Intellectual'" who offers a utopia (p. 137). Hiero's silence is that of a "liberal" statesman who lets the poet speak and leave in peace (p. 138).

Second, the utopia has come true. The prizes have become "Stakhanovite" emulation in farming, industry and commerce. The bodyguard has become a state police and a standing army, with compulsory service, and the tyrant builds public works rather than palaces (pp. 138–139). Xenophon never saw tyrannies in the service of "truly revolutionary political, social, or economic ideas", national, racial, imperial or humanitarian. But modern tyrannies have realized the ideal, "by Salazar, for example" (p. 139).

### 10. Kojève: Recognition

Simonides' praise of honour is, for Kojève, the attitude Hegel calls the Master's, aristocratic and pagan, against the Slave's, Judeo-Christian and bourgeois (p. 140). It is half the truth. The other half is the joy of work itself. A child making sand-patties alone on a beach, a painter covering the cliffs of a desert island, act without any thought of glory. A "bourgeois" statesman may justify his power as the condition of his "labour" (pp. 140–141). But as soon as there are onlookers and rivals, emulation and honour come back (p. 141).

Hiero's complaint is the tragedy of the Master in the *Phenomenology*: the recognition of a slave who submits out of fear is worthless to the victor (p. 142). Kojève prefers Hegel's terms to Xenophon's. Love, affection and happiness involve things that have nothing to do with politics. What political men want is recognition and the satisfaction it brings:

> the desire to be "recognized" in one's eminent human reality and dignity (by those whom one "recognizes" in return) effectively is, I believe, the ultimate motive of all emulation among men, and hence of all political struggle, including the struggle that leads to tyranny. (p. 143)

This is a theory of authority, which means being obeyed without constraint. Pure terror is impossible. By force alone one man can dominate children, the old, some women, "at the outside two or three adults", so every ruler rests on some authority (p. 144). Tyranny in a morally neutral sense exists when a fraction, majority or minority, imposes its ideas on the rest without compromise (p. 145). Hiero is unsatisfied because his authority is recognized by some and not by all whom he counts as citizens. Since the desire for recognition has no limit, the statesman will be fully satisfied only at the head of a state that is universal and "politically and socially homogeneous". There he will want to free slaves, emancipate women and raise everyone's cultural level so that more can recognize him (pp. 145–146). The new Simonides must avoid utopia and tell the tyrant how to reach that state from the present one (p. 147).

### 11. Kojève: The Philosopher and the Garden

Can a philosopher rule, and does he want to? Kojève thinks he can. He is better at dialectic, freer of prejudice, and closer to the concrete, since he knows when he is abstracting (pp. 148–150). A footnote explains that "concrete" means seeing the whole: a tree abstracted from earth, air and sun is an abstraction, and so is a purely "national" politics (p. 148 n.).

The difficulty is time. The philosopher seems to need all of it, and the "Epicurean" answer is to withdraw: the aristocrat into a "garden", the poor bourgeois intellectual into the "Republic of Letters" Bayle described, each asking only to be left alone (pp. 150–151). Kojève rejects this on three grounds.

1. **Being.** Withdrawal makes sense only if Being is eternal and Truth fully revealed for all time. If, with Hegel, Being creates itself in history, the philosopher must frequent "the citizens of the City", not "trees and cicadas" (p. 152).
2. **Madness.** The solitary thinker relies on the feeling of evidence, the criterion "accepted by all 'rationalist' philosophers from Plato to Husserl". But madmen are certain too. The man who thinks he is made of glass is called mad only because no one, not even other madmen, shares his belief. So "the Epicurean philosopher, living strictly isolated in his 'garden,' could never know whether he has attained Wisdom or sunk into madness" (p. 153).
3. **The cloister.** Gardens and academies admit friends and so escape madness, but they breed prejudice. An elite chosen for its doctrine will hold on to the doctrine. The philosopher should live "in the 'market place' or 'in the street,' like Socrates" (pp. 154–155). A note recalls Queneau's quip that the philosopher is a *voyou*.

Kojève then reverses Strauss on love. With Goethe and Hegel, he holds that we are loved for what we are, as a mother loves her son despite his faults, and recognized for what we do. So it is Simonides, who wants his perfection admired, who seeks recognition, and he seeks it through the work of perfecting himself. Statesman and philosopher both seek recognition and both want to deserve it (pp. 155–156). There is no a priori reason to limit the philosopher's disciples, and an elite that hears only its admirers comes to think only its admirers worthy (pp. 157–158).

As for Socrates, the joy of knowledge, the satisfaction of duty and the pleasure of self-admiration cannot be separated by any experiment. A footnote adds that intellectuals are vainer than men of action, because the success of a book is nothing but its recognition by others, while a bridge either stands or falls (p. 162 n.). These distinctions make sense only before a God who sees the heart:

> as long as a man is alone in knowing something, he can never be sure that he truly knows it. If, as a consistent atheist, one replaces God ... by Society (the State) and History, one has to say that whatever is, in fact, beyond the range of social and historical verification, is forever relegated to the realm of opinion (doxa). (p. 161)

### 12. Kojève: History as the Test

Because discussion is his criterion of truth, the philosopher must be a teacher, and he will not limit his teaching. But the state is a teaching institution too, so the philosopher collides with the tyrant, as in the charge against Socrates of corrupting the young (pp. 162–163). Hence Plato's journeys to Syracuse and Spinoza's work with De Witt (p. 163).

Advice given in spare hours has failed. Philosophers in a hurry are drawn to tyrants, and a philosopher in power would act like one, as Alcibiades did (pp. 164–165). Had Plato stayed in Syracuse, he would have faced "cases of conscience" and reached the limits of his knowledge, and resigned (p. 166). The philosopher's conflict with the tyrant is the intellectual's conflict with action, which for Hegel is the one authentic tragedy of the bourgeois world, "the tragedy of Hamlet and of Faust" (p. 166).

Two thousand years of discussion have settled nothing, so Kojève turns to "historical verification" (p. 167). Truth emerges only when history ends in the universal and homogeneous state, which leaves nothing to negate (p. 168). History shows that philosophers have been heard after all. Alexander, the pupil of Aristotle, pupil of Plato, pupil of Socrates, wanted an empire that dissolved Macedon and Greece and mixed conquerors with conquered. Only a disciple of Greek philosophy could have based a state on a common human "essence", the Logos (pp. 169–171). Ikhnaton and St Paul offered religious versions, and Paul added equality through conversion, a homogeneity the Greeks could not conceive. Modern philosophy secularized it. "The tyrant who here initiates the real political movement toward homogeneity consciously followed the teaching of the intellectual who deliberately transformed the idea of the philosopher" (p. 173), The names are left out; the chain presumably runs from Hegel through Marx to Stalin.

Kojève concludes that philosophers, intellectuals and tyrants have all behaved reasonably. The philosopher stays at the level of ideas, intellectuals bridge them to current affairs, and tyrants act when the advice is concrete. "The emergence of a reforming tyrant is not conceivable without the prior existence of the philosopher" (p. 175). It would be unreasonable for the philosopher to condemn tyranny "on principle", since it can be judged only in a concrete situation. History judges by success (p. 176).

### 13. The Restatement: Caesarism and Machiavelli

Strauss opens with a sentence he could have used for the whole book: "A social science that cannot speak of tyranny with the same confidence with which medicine speaks, for example, of cancer, cannot understand social phenomena as what they are" (p. 177). The classics knew a science of conquering nature and of popular enlightenment was possible, and rejected both as "unnatural", that is, "destructive of humanity" (p. 178).

**Voegelin.** Eric Voegelin had argued that the classical concept of tyranny was too narrow for Caesarism, absolute rule after a constitutional order has collapsed. Strauss grants that genuine Caesarism is not tyranny, but places it inside the classical frame as a kind of absolute monarchy. It is justified by necessity, and "Necessity excuses: what is justified by necessity is in need of excuse". Caesarism belongs to a corrupt people and is just in the way deserved punishment is just (p. 179). The classics kept quiet about it for a reason: "The true doctrine of the legitimacy of Caesarism is a dangerous doctrine", and it is better that people regard the potential Caesar as a potential tyrant. "Caesars can take care of themselves" (p. 180).

**Cyrus.** The *Cyropaedia* is not a model. Cyrus turns a healthy aristocracy into an unstable "Oriental despotism". His first step is to persuade the Persian nobles to practise virtue for its rewards. This matches the myth of Er, where a man virtuous by habit without philosophy chooses "the greatest tyranny" (pp. 181–182). Still, Cyrus remains legitimate, and the difference between him and a reformed Hiero is like that between William III and Cromwell. Xenophon "appreciated too well the charms, nay, the blessings of legitimacy" (p. 182). To historicism Strauss answers: "After the experience of our generation, the burden of proof would seem to rest on those who assert rather than on those who deny that we have progressed beyond the classics" (p. 183).

**Machiavelli.** His armed prophets are Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus, and the stress falls on "armed": the unarmed prophet is Savonarola, and behind him "the most famous of all unarmed prophets" (p. 183). The tradition Machiavelli radicalizes is the Averroist one, not Joachim of Fiore. Rejecting the contemplative ideal, "Machiavelli separates wisdom from moderation", and the *Hiero* comes close to the *Prince* because Simonides' wisdom, with its love of the table, comes close to a wisdom without moderation (pp. 184–185). Readers raised on modern literature need "a second education" to see "the noble reserve and the quiet grandeur of the classics", and those who prefer Jane Austen to Dostoevsky have a head start (p. 185).

### 14. The Restatement: Honour, Work and Synthesis

Strauss is shocked by Kojève's "more than Machiavellian bluntness" about atheism and tyranny, but not for long, since Kojève "belongs to the very few who know how to think and who love to think. ... In a word, Kojeve is a philosopher and not an intellectual" (pp. 185–186). He would not compare Simonides and Hiero to Stefan George or Thomas Mann and Hitler, since the *Hiero* supposes a tyrant who might be taught (p. 186).

**Utopia.** A utopia of the best tyranny simply spells out what every sensible reform aims at. Its key step, shifting power from mercenaries to citizens, would be safe only in an emergency no sensible man would create (pp. 187–188). Simonides' real achievement may be small, getting Hiero to stop competing at Olympia. "Today such little actions like that of Simonides are not taken seriously because we are in the habit of expecting too much" (p. 188).

**Salazar and Stalin.** "One swallow does not make a summer" (p. 188). Kojève means all modern tyrants are good tyrants, Stalin included. But Stalin would meet Simonides' standard only if Stakhanovism had come with a decline of the NKVD and the camps. Could Stalin travel outside the Iron Curtain without fear, as Simonides promises (11.10)? (pp. 188–189)

**Honour.** Kojève dropped the word "seems" from Simonides' praise of honour, and ignored that it is the passion of *andres*, not of the highest type. Xenophon's highest type is the wise man. Socrates is called a *kalos te kagathos anthrōpos*, not *anēr*, and courage is missing from both lists of his virtues (pp. 189–190). The classics already had Kojève's "joy of work": they called it the pleasure of noble activity. A safecracker also enjoys a job well done, so what matters is whether the job is noble (pp. 190–191). Since one cannot become a tyrant without base acts, and every well-bred child knows not to do evil for good results, the would-be tyrant must be blinded by passion, most charitably by love of honour (p. 191).

**Synthesis.** "Syntheses effect miracles." Hegel's synthesis of Biblical and classical morality produces "an amazingly lax morality out of two moralities both of which made very strict demands on self-restraint" (p. 191). Hegel continues the modern tradition from Machiavelli through Hobbes and Adam Smith, which emancipated the passions. His master and slave rest on Hobbes's state of nature, and both doctrines assume "that man as man is thinkable as a being that lacks awareness of sacred restraints or as a being that is guided by nothing but a desire for recognition" (p. 192).

**Aristocracy.** If the wise do not desire to rule, the unwise will never give them absolute power, and the wise man's first measure would be to send everyone over ten out of the city (*Republic* 540e). A universal state needs agreement on fundamentals, which only wisdom gives, and "Every faith that lays claim to universality ... of necessity provokes a counter-faith". The best one could expect is the unwise ruling half the globe. So the unwise should rule under law, interpreted by gentlemen, and the alternative to aristocracy is "permanent revolution". Strauss adds:

> It would not be difficult to show that the classical argument cannot be disposed of as easily as is now generally thought, and that liberal or constitutional democracy comes closer to what the classics demanded than any alternative that is viable in our age. (p. 194)

### 15. The Restatement: Philosophy, Sect and City

Strauss accepts Kojève's point about certainty. The classics knew the individual mind is weak, which is why their account of the philosophic life is an account of friendship. But friends agree on opinions, which yields sects (p. 194). The Republic of Letters avoids narrowness by not taking any position seriously: it "prefers agreement to truth or to the quest for truth". The mass party is worse: "a mass party is nothing but a sect with a disproportionately long tail". "If we must choose between the sect and the party, we must choose the sect" (p. 195).

But philosophy need not be sectarian, because it is not a set of certainties:

> Philosophy as such is nothing but genuine awareness of the problems, i.e., of the fundamental and comprehensive problems. ... the philosopher ceases to be a philosopher at the moment at which the "subjective certainty" of a solution becomes stronger than his awareness of the problematic character of that solution. At that moment the sectarian is born. (p. 196)

Socrates never belonged to or founded a sect (p. 196).

**Love and honour again.** Strauss restates the difference between the two lives. The philosopher's passion is for the eternal order. Seen from there, human things look "paltry and ephemeral", so "the philosopher is the only man who is truly ambitious" (p. 198). The political man cannot accept that depreciation. He is "consumed by erotic desire ... for the demos" and wants to be loved by all regardless of their quality (p. 198). Kojève's mother does not love her son "because he is" but because he is her own (p. 199). The philosopher is still bound to others: philosophy needs a division of labour, and man is a social animal. Being free of the desire to have more, "the philosopher will not hurt anyone", will not start revolutions, and will mitigate the evils of the human condition where he can (pp. 199–200).

Why does he teach? A well-ordered soul reflects the eternal order more than a chaotic one. The philosopher is pleased by such souls and pained by disordered ones without regard to his own benefit, and he wants the promising young to acquire good order, which is philosophizing. So he educates "simply because he cannot help loving well-ordered souls" (pp. 200–201). Strauss leaves open whether this can be explained without assuming that the soul is akin to the eternal order, since Democritus philosophized without that assumption (p. 201). The ruler's love, by contrast, is mercenary. Xenophon's Cyrus is "a cold or unerotic nature" (p. 202). More men can judge a ruler's greatness than a philosopher's, because philosophy requires freedom from "unqualified attachment to human things", and nature returns however often it is driven out with a pitchfork (p. 203). A philosopher who becomes concerned with recognition "turns into a sophist" (p. 203).

**Philosophic politics.** Strauss agrees with Kojève's conclusion that the philosopher must go to the market place, "to fish there for potential philosophers", and so will be accused of corrupting the young (p. 205). But defending philosophy is not the same as trying to set up the best regime. Plato ranked Sparta above Athens while knowing Athens let Socrates teach until seventy, whereas in Sparta he would have been exposed at birth. Philosophic politics means satisfying the city that philosophers are not atheists, revere what it reveres, and are the best of citizens (pp. 205–206). Plato, Cicero, Farabi and Maimonides did this with great success. "One sometimes wonders whether it has not been too successful" (p. 206). For the classics the conflict of philosopher and city is not tragic. Xenophon saw it in the light of Socrates and Xanthippe, and it is "as little tragic as the death of Socrates" (p. 206).

### 16. The Restatement: The Universal and Homogeneous State

Everything depends on whether the universal and homogeneous state is the best order. Strauss uses Kojève's own *Introduction to the Reading of Hegel* against it.

- Only the Chief of State is "really satisfied" and "truly free". Strauss asks whether the end state is "merely a planetary Oriental despotism" (p. 208).
- Its citizens are said to be warrior-workers, but war and revolution have become impossible and work has nearly ended. Yet for Kojève it is struggle and work that raise man above the brutes. The end state is therefore the state "in which man loses his humanity. It is the state of Nietzsche's 'last man'". Kojève thus "confirms the classical view that unlimited technological progress ... [is] destructive of humanity" (p. 208).
- If so, history is tragic. Mankind has laboured toward a goal that returns it to the prehuman. "Vanitas vanitatum. Recognitio recognitionum" (p. 209).
- As long as "sun and man still generate man", there will be men who revolt, perhaps nihilistically, against a state with no room for noble deeds, and the cycle would start again. Strauss prefers that to an inhuman end: "Warriors and workers of all countries, unite, while there is still time, to prevent the coming of 'the realm of freedom'" (p. 209).
- If wisdom rather than recognition is the end, everyone must be capable of it. Kojève himself calls the ordinary citizens only "potentially satisfied", which is why his end state remains a state and does not wither away (p. 210).

The contrast between the two traditions is then summed up:

> The classical solution is utopian in the sense that its actualization is improbable. The modern solution is utopian in the sense that its actualization is impossible. The classical solution supplies a stable standard by which to judge of any actual order. The modern solution eventually destroys the very idea of a standard that is independent of actual situations. (pp. 210–211)

The Universal and Final Tyrant will be unwise, since the wise do not want to rule. He must forbid any teaching that there are natural differences among men, and he will present himself as "the executor and hangman authorized by the only true philosophy". In the past persecuted philosophers wrote exoterically and could flee abroad. "From the Universal Tyrant however there is no escape." Kojève is right, "although for the wrong reason: the coming of the universal and homogeneous state will be the end of philosophy on earth" (p. 211).

The last paragraph names the root of the disagreement. Classical philosophy presupposes an eternal order unaffected by history. Kojève holds that Being creates itself in history. On the classical view, the philosopher "must not be absolutely at home on earth, he must be a citizen of the whole" (p. 212). Both men turned from Being to tyranny because those who lacked the courage to face tyranny, "who therefore *et humiliter serviebant et superbe dominabantur*, were forced to evade the issue of Being as well, precisely because they did nothing but talk of Being" (p. 212). The Latin, adapted from Livy, means they were humbly servile and haughtily domineering.

### 17. The Correspondence

The letters run from December 1932, when Strauss invites "Mr. Kochevnikoff" over for the evening in Paris, to June 1965. Gourevitch's prefatory note gives the background: both born around 1900, Strauss in Kirchhain and Kojève in Moscow, they met in Berlin in the 1920s while both studied religious thought. Kojève took over Koyré's Hegel seminar in 1933 and ran it until 1939. After the war he joined the French Ministry of Economic Affairs, helped write the GATT treaty and build the European Economic Community, while Strauss taught at Chicago (pp. 218–219). Each "regarded the other's position as perhaps the only significant philosophical alternative to his own" (p. 219).

**The 1930s.** Strauss writes from London that he has become "a real Hobbes philologist" and may have found Hobbes's first writing (p. 225). He outlines his thesis: the young Hobbes moved from aristocratic honour to the antithesis of vanity and fear, and "the fear of (violent) death becomes the moral principle" (pp. 228–229). Kojève replies in 1936 that Hegel starts from Hobbes but sees what Hobbes missed, the value of work. Fear of death alone leads only to religion. The slave reaches freedom by working for the master, and the end of history synthesizes master and slave in the citizen-soldier (pp. 231–233). One postscript from 1934 encloses a picture of Hitler, who looks "very congenial and 'cozy'", which "explains a great deal" (p. 227).

**The 1948 letter.** After reading Kojève's *Introduction*, Strauss writes that apart from Heidegger no contemporary had written "as comprehensive and at the same time as intelligent a book" (p. 236). He then puts the objections later made public. Hegel's system needs a philosophy of nature, which Kojève discards. Only a teleological nature can guarantee that history is unique, and that would rule out atheism. Recognition cannot satisfy; only wisdom can, and if most people never become wise, the end state means the loss of their humanity. He ends with Nietzsche's "last men" (pp. 236–239). The same letter asks Kojève to review *On Tyranny*, because he is one of the few who will understand: "I am one of those who refuse to go through open doors when one can enter just as well through a keyhole" (p. 236).

**After the debate.** Kojève's letter of 19 September 1950 accepts Strauss's conclusion as the real issue. In the end state the "healthy" automata will be satisfied with sport, art and eroticism, the "sick" locked up, the dissatisfied will become philosophers and so "gods", and the tyrant "becomes an administrator, a cog in the 'machine' fashioned by automata for automata" (p. 255). He came to Hegel by way of the question of criteria. There are three choices: Plato's and Husserl's intuition of essences, relativism, or Hegel's circularity, and about nature one can only be silent (p. 256). In 1953 he answers *Natural Right and History*: if there is a human nature, Strauss is right in everything, but animals have natures and are only healthy or sick, so "it is precisely ancient anthropology that would lead to mass-training and eugenics" (p. 262).

**Plato, 1957.** The longest exchange is about Plato. Kojève reads the *Theaetetus* portrait of the philosopher as ironic and sides with the laughing Thracian maid. He argues that the *Sophist* and *Statesman* attack Eudoxus and Aristotle, and that late Platonists like Julian and Damascius were ironic writers in the manner Strauss described, "first-class Voltaires" (pp. 265–273). Strauss replies: "I do not believe in the possibility of a conversation of Socrates with the people ... the relation of the philosopher to the people is mediated by a certain kind of rhetoricians who arouse fear of punishment after death; the philosopher can guide these rhetoricians but can not do their work (this is the meaning of the Gorgias)" (p. 275). Later he objects to Kojève's method: Plato must be interpreted dialogue by dialogue, with as little outside information as possible (pp. 276–277). His final answer comes down to the old disagreement: "you are convinced of the truth of Hegel (Marx) and I am not", and Kojève never answered the questions of the last man and the philosophy of nature (p. 291).

**Late letters.** In 1962 Kojève turns down a Hegel congress at Heidelberg. A lecture to three hundred young people in Saint-Germain, who calmly wrote down his most shocking paradoxes, left him feeling like an "old gent": "I am becoming more and more 'platonic.' One should address the few, not the many" (p. 308). Strauss answers that he is glad to see from the title of Kojève's history of "pagan" philosophy that "you have returned to the faith of your fathers" (p. 309). In the last letter, of 1965, Strauss is pleased that Kojève is "just as critical of U.S. liberals" as he is, and that Kojève's Festschrift essay on the emperor Julian "shows that persecution and the art of writing are not some fancy" (p. 313).

### 18. The Editors' Introduction and the Two Positions

Gourevitch and Roth frame the debate as a choice about modernity. Both men agree that philosophy and society are in conflict and that wisdom ranks highest. They disagree about whether the conflict can and should be resolved: Strauss keeps the two apart, Kojève works for their reconciliation (p. xi). For Strauss "it is primarily the manner of philosophy that is political. For Kojeve it is just as much its matter" (p. xix).

The editors add several points the texts leave implicit.

- **Kojève's change.** After the war, perhaps in response to Strauss's 1948 letter, Kojève gave up his "heroic Hegelianism" and became "an ironic culture critic" who placed the end of history in the past, at Jena in 1806 (pp. xiv–xv). His famous note says that America has in a sense already reached the "final stage of Marxist 'communism'", and that post-historical men keep their humanity only through formal rituals like the tea ceremony or Noh theatre (pp. xv–xvi).
- **Kojève's deeds.** He did not "like a man in a storm, seek shelter behind a wall". His work for the European community was meant as pressure on the Soviet Union to liberalize (pp. xvi–xvii).
- **Skepticism and relativism.** Both men reject the "decapitated Hegelianism" that accepts history but denies it can end, Kojève for the sake of the complete account, Strauss for the sake of zetetic skepticism (p. xvii).
- **Nature.** Kojève's complete account would have to deduce man and therefore nature, but he denies any discursive account of nature is possible. Mastery of nature presupposes an anthropocentric providence, which neither man accepts (pp. xix–xx).
- **Brutality.** In the 1953 letter Kojève defended Stalin's and Mao's collectivizations. The editors note unconfirmed press reports from 1999 linking him to Soviet intelligence (p. xxi).
- **Liberal democracy.** Strauss's remark that liberal democracy comes closest to the classical demand is best read as "a suggestion for a radical revision of our conception of liberal democracy", since attempts to reconcile classical philosophy with it end in concessions he never made (p. xxii).
- **Heidegger.** Never named in the debate, he is present throughout. The Livy line that closes the Restatement seems aimed at the one great thinker who joined the Nazis in the name of his teaching (p. xxii). The letters bear this out. Strauss is glad that someone at last states the modern position intelligently and "without Heidegger's cowardly vagueness" (p. 244), and reads Heidegger as "the last refuge of nationalism", with only language left after the state and culture are done with (pp. 251–252).

| Question | Strauss | Kojève |
|---|---|---|
| What is philosophy? | Awareness of the permanent problems; quest, not wisdom | A stage on the way to wisdom, which arrives at the end of history |
| Criterion of truth | Evidence of the problems; friends correct each other | Recognition by others, finally by history |
| Being | Eternal order; history takes place within it | Being becomes; Truth = Man = History |
| Motive of the ruler | Love, an erotic desire for the demos | Recognition, as for everyone |
| Motive of the philosopher | Love of truth and of well-ordered souls; honour is secondary | Recognition, and the wish to deserve it |
| Philosopher and city | Conflict permanent and not tragic; defend philosophy through "philosophic politics" | Conflict tragic for the individual, resolved by history through tyrants |
| Good tyranny | Possible only by chance; the thesis exposes the problem of law | Realized in modern states (Salazar) |
| End state | The last man; a universal tyranny with no escape | Satisfaction of all, wisdom for the few who want it |
| Best practical regime | Rule of law by gentlemen; liberal democracy as the nearest viable form | The universal and homogeneous state |

### Conclusion

The *Hiero* is a short dialogue about a tyrant who complains and a poet who flatters him into listening. Strauss shows that almost everything in it carries meaning: who visits whom, who speaks and who stays silent, which words are used or avoided. Read that way, the dialogue shows that tyranny may in theory be better than law, because law is blind and legitimacy is not wisdom. Strauss argues that this is a statement of the problem of law, not a policy. It also shows that the ruler, who needs to be loved by all, ranks below the wise man, who wants only the admiration of a few and can therefore be just to everyone.

Kojève accepts the reading and rejects the conclusion. For him the wise man is an intellectual until he finds a way from the ideal to the present. Recognition, not love or honour, moves all politics, and a philosopher alone cannot tell wisdom from madness. Philosophy is tested by history, and history has been the work of tyrants who learned, at several removes, from philosophers. Strauss's reply grants that philosophy needs others and must defend itself before the city. He denies that it needs to rule or to be universally recognized, and he calls the end of history the age of the last man and the end of philosophy.

The letters show that neither man moved. Strauss's last word in their Plato exchange is that Kojève believes in Hegel and Marx and he does not. Gourevitch and Roth close by saying that the disagreement, left unreconciled, shows more clearly than any compromise could "the temptations and the risks of the most basic alternatives before us" (p. xxii).
