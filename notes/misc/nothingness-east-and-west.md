> "Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear." (Heb 11:3)

> "Let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me. For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: but whosoever will lose his life for my sake, the same shall save it." (Luke 9:23–24)

The **Kyoto School** (Nishida Kitarō, Tanabe Hajime, Nishitani Keiji, later Abe Masao and Ueda Shizuteru) is the one modern philosophy that set out to answer the West in its own language from the standpoint of Buddhism. Its key word is **absolute nothingness** (*zettai mu*), or in Nishitani, **śūnyatā** (emptiness). Its thesis, roughly: Western thought from Plato to Hegel puts *being* first, and modern nihilism is what happens when that being (God) is lost. Buddhism alone has a "nothing" deep enough to pass *through* nihilism and come out the other side. Christianity is not its enemy here but its most serious partner, because the Kyoto thinkers saw in kenosis, the Cross and apophatic theology the places where the West came closest.

## 1. Nishitani: Religion and Nothingness

Nishitani (1900–1990) studied under Nishida and with Heidegger at Freiburg (1937–39). He said his youth was "absolutely without hope", lived "in the grips of nihility and despair", and that he took up philosophy as "a matter of life and death". *Religion and Nothingness* (1961) is six essays, not a system.

**"What is religion?"** (ch. 1)
- You cannot ask what religion is *for*. Learning and art are needed to live *well*; food is needed to live; religion is needed precisely by the person who thinks he doesn't need it. The real question is turned back on the asker: **"For what purpose do I myself exist?"**
- It arises when death, nihility or sin become personal: an illness, the loss of someone loved, a ruined life's work. Then "a void appears here that nothing in the world can fill". But the abyss is "always just underfoot". We carry death with us from birth.
- Normally our busy engagements hide it. When it opens, life "takes a step back". In the Zen phrase, it turns "the light to what is directly underfoot".
- **Reality.** He wants the "self-awareness of reality": reality *realising itself* in us, and our realising it, in both senses of "realise". His example is Dostoevsky in *The House of the Dead*, carrying bricks by the Irtysh, suddenly seeing the steppe, a bird and a flower and weeping. For a moment the prisoner forgets his "wretched self" and sees "God's world".
- **The Cartesian ego.** We usually see from "within the citadel of the self", on the field of *consciousness*, where subject and object are split. Descartes made this the foundation: the ego certain of itself and a dead, mechanical world of extension around it, "a lonely but well fortified island floating on a sea of dead matter". His fault was thinking about the *cogito* only from inside the *cogito*. The ego is "self in a state of **self-attachment**".
- **The Great Doubt.** Descartes' doubt was methodical, a tool of the ego. The Zen "Great Doubt" is the opposite: the self and all things become a single question mark, until "the self becomes the Great Doubt". He quotes the Zen teacher Takusui: doubt "what is the subject that hears", "becoming completely like a dead man", until suddenly you wake "as if, having been completely dead, you had suddenly revived". Hence Zen's sayings: "Beneath the Great Death, the Great Enlightenment"; "In the Great Death heaven and earth become new".
- **Sin.** Evil is not something "the self commits" as a separate act. It is a "Great Reality" at the root of the self, shared with all humanity: Kant's radical evil, Kierkegaard's original sin, Buddhist karma and *avidyā* (ignorance). On Barth versus Brunner (is there a "point of contact" for grace in fallen man?), he gives a striking answer. The point of contact is the **awareness of total corruption itself**. When the self knows it is nothing and powerless, that nothingness becomes a *locus* (like Plato's *chōra* in the *Timaeus*) for "receiving redeeming love". It matches the **kenosis** of Christ. Faith is the self's acceptance of that love, "the sword that kills man" and "the sword that gives man life" (Matt 10:34; 16:25).
- **Sartre.** Atheist existentialism stands on nothingness but treats it as "a springboard underfoot of the ego". It is still a nothing *for* the ego, and so the ego becomes "a vast and desolate cave": in Zen terms, "living in the Demon's Cavern", "the emptiness perversely clung to". Sartre's freedom "is rather the deepest bondage".
- **Creation ex nihilo.** Here is his most interesting move on Christianity. Augustine: "heaven and earth… cry out that they have been created" (*Conf.* XI.4). If so, each thing also says "I am not God". So at the bottom of everything we meet "the nihility of the *creatio ex nihilo*", an "**iron wall**" between creature and God. Yet things *are*, so in the same place we meet God's grace giving being. "In our very inability to encounter God wherever we turn in the world, we encounter God no matter where we turn." The Christian should be able to pick up a pebble or a blade of grass and feel the "fear and trembling" Moses felt at the bush. The *nihil* is "more immanent in that thing than the very being of that thing". He calls this God's "absolute immanence" as "absolute transcendence".
- Even a sneeze, or listening to the radio, brings one before the nihility of the self and the omnipotence of God. A man who says he has no time for nihility "encounters nihility in his way of not encountering it".

**The personal and impersonal** (ch. 2)
- Christ's command, "love your enemies… that ye may be the children of your Father… for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good" (Matt 5:44–45), shows a love that is **non-differentiating**. It is not nature's cold indifference but "the indifference of love".
- Its form is **self-emptying**: Christ's *ekkenōsis* (Phil 2:7). Nishitani goes further: "What is *ekkenosis* for the Son is *kenosis* for the Father", contained "in his original perfection". In the East this is called *anātman*, non-ego. "It means denying eros and turning to agape, denying ego and turning to non-ego."
- So the "personal" God who chooses Israel, commands and punishes is one side. The perfection of God that shines on all alike is "**personally impersonal**", deeper than the personal. Christianity has stressed only the first.
- **Eckhart** is the Western witness. He distinguished God from the **Godhead** (*Gottheit*), which is "absolute nothingness", beyond even Creator and Love. The soul "breaks through" God into the "desert of the godhead". "The ground of God is the ground of my soul… The eye with which I see God is the eye with which God sees me." For Nishitani, Eckhart reaches "the absolute near side".

**Nihility and śūnyatā** (ch. 3)
- Science sees the laws of nature most purely in the **machine**. But at the extreme an inversion happens: man is mechanised, and at the same time becomes "a subject in pursuit of its desires" standing on nihility. These are two sides of one coin. He finds it in mass amusements ("crypto-nihilism"), in Communist totalitarianism and in liberal consumerism alike.
- Religions spin "on a life-oriented axis" (ascending to soul, spirit, personality, God). Science runs on "a death-oriented axis" (reducing all to matter, ending in meaninglessness). Śūnyatā is where both cross. Every thing is a "double exposure" of life and death, "life-*sive*-death".
- **Nihility versus emptiness.** Even Heidegger's being "held out into the nothing" keeps a trace of treating nothingness as "some 'thing'", an abyss *outside* the self. Śūnyatā "empties itself" even of that. "Form is emptiness; emptiness is form" (the *Heart Sutra*). It is the negation of the negation. As a valley lies within the endless sky, "emptiness may be said to be an abyss even for that abyss of nihility". "Heaven would also have to reach beneath the bottomless pit of hell."
- On nihility, even my closest friend is ultimately "a stranger, an 'unknown'", and the flower in my garden is separated from me by an abyss. On emptiness, the same absolute separation becomes "a most intimate encounter". He quotes Daitō Kokushi: "Separated from one another by a hundred million kalpas, yet not apart a single moment".
- **The 90°, 180° and 360° turns.** Plato's Ideas and the Christian God of revelation are each "a 90° turn": heaven above earth. Plato goes from earth upward (*eros*), Christianity from heaven downward (*agape*); either way it is a "far side" on the same canvas. Nihilism is a 90° turn downward. Emptiness is a 180° turn, turning the whole canvas over. Emptiness emptied is 360°, where "0° means 360°" and everything stands in its "suchness".

**The standpoint of śūnyatā** (ch. 4)
- "Fire does not burn fire"; "the eye does not see the eye". A thing on its "home-ground" is in *samādhi*-being, settled in itself. Reason's substance-concepts cannot reach it. Only a "knowing of non-knowing" can.
- All things are "master and servant to one another": each thing is itself only by being upheld by all the others, and upholds them in turn. He borrows the Trinitarian word for this: the world is a "system of **circuminsession**", possible "only on the field of emptiness".
- Chs. 5–6 extend this to time and history. Buddhist "beginningless" time, faced existentially, becomes the "fullness of time at each and every moment". He criticises the Christian line from creation to judgement, both for leaving no room for selflessness and as the parent of the secular idea of progress.

## 2. Tanabe: Metanoetics

Tanabe (1885–1962), Nishida's successor and critic, came at nothingness from the opposite direction: not insight but **repentance**.
- **The occasion** (Preface, written 1944–45). As Japan's war was being lost, he was torn between speaking out against the government and keeping silent so as not to divide the nation in wartime. He couldn't decide, and concluded "I was not fit to engage in the sublime task of philosophy". At that moment "something astonishing happened": he "let go and surrendered myself humbly to my own inability", and his "penitent confession, metanoesis (*zange*)" turned him inward.
- The result is "a philosophy that is not a philosophy". "It is no longer I who pursue philosophy, but rather *zange* that thinks through me."
- **Self-power and Other-power.** He borrows these from Shinran's Pure Land Buddhism. *Jiriki* (self-power) is abandoned in despair. *Tariki* (Other-power) "turns me in a completely new direction". *Zange* is "my own act" and yet "cannot be my own act": it is "prompted by a Power outside of myself".
- **"Nothingness-qua-love."** The absolute is "the negation and transformation, that is, conversion, of everything relative", so "it may be defined as absolute nothingness". It is experienced not as a void but as "**the Great Nay as the Great Compassion**". "I have died to philosophy and been resurrected by zange… I no longer live of myself" (compare Gal 2:20).
- *Zange* must be **continual**. If he grows complacent and thinks himself wise he falls back into despair. "Only through continual *zange* can we achieve the faith and witness of continuous resurrection."
- The translator notes that he read *zange* through the Greek *metanoia*, as more than remorse: a "voluntary despair" (against "rebellious despair") that lets go of the self. He was reading Schweitzer's *Mysticism of Paul* at the time and later wrote *The Dialectics of Christianity* (1948).
- Against Nishida, Tanabe objected that "intuition" of absolute nothingness is still contemplation, still the self looking. Only *action and faith* under Other-power really pass through death.

## 3. Abe: Non-Being and Mu

Abe (1915–2006) put the contrast most sharply, in "Non-Being and Mu: The Metaphysical Nature of Negativity in the East and the West".
- He starts from **Tillich**: "Being precedes nonbeing in ontological validity"; "Being 'embraces' itself and non-being". This, he says, is *the* Western assumption.
- **Greeks**: non-being is privation, like darkness where light is lacking (*mē on*). Plato rejects absolute non-being (*ouk on*) as unthinkable. Being is form and good; non-being is matter and the source of evil.
- **Christians**: *creatio ex nihilo* makes the *nihil* absolute (*ouk on*) and personal: creatures face "the abyss of nihility", and nothingness becomes the root of sin as rebellion. This is "more existential", but still being has priority.
- So in the West, overcoming the opposition of being/non-being, life/death, good/evil means moving *toward* Being, Eternal Life, the Supreme Good.
- **East.** Laozi: "Being is the product of Non-being". [[Zhuangzi: Perspectives, the Butterfly Dream and the Useless Tree|Zhuangzi]]: a time "before that, when even nothing did not exist". Buddhism denies a permanent self (*anātman*) and teaches dependent origination (*pratītya-samutpāda*). Nāgārjuna's **emptiness** rejects both the "eternalist" and the "nihilistic" view. It is a "negation of the negation", so "absolute negation is absolute affirmation", and "true Emptiness is wondrous Being".
- **U and mu.** In Japanese, being (*u*) and non-being (*mu*) are of "completely equal force". Neither has priority. The ultimate is not a Being slanted toward *u* but a formless Emptiness beyond both, which must itself be emptied, or it becomes a third "thing".
- **Ethics.** The same goes for life and death and for good and evil. Buddhism doesn't deny that good *should* conquer evil, but holds that good "is not strong enough to overcome evil", because they are equal and opposed. So salvation is not the victory of good but liberation from the antinomy itself: "to be master of, rather than enslaved by, good and evil". He calls the Western view "idealistic", and the Buddhist one "radically realistic with regard to human nature".
- **Western exceptions.** He grants that the West has its negative strand: Dionysius' *Mystical Theology* (God beyond affirmation and negation), Eckhart's *Nichts*, Boehme's *Ungrund*, Nietzsche and Heidegger.

## 4. Heidegger: "The Nothing Itself Nihilates"

The Kyoto School read Heidegger's 1929 Freiburg lecture closely.
- Science "wants to know nothing of the nothing", yet to say what it is, it must say what it is *not*.
- The nothing is not reached by logical negation. It is revealed in a mood: **anxiety** (*Angst*), where beings as a whole slip away and "we hover". Afterwards we say that what we were anxious about was "nothing", and "indeed, nothing itself, as such, was there".
- "No-thing nihilates of its own" (*das Nichts selbst nichtet*). It is neither annihilation nor negation.
- Old metaphysics said *ex nihilo nihil fit*. Christian dogmatics turned this into *ex nihilo fit ens creatum*, where nothing is simply the opposite of God, the *summum ens*. Heidegger offers instead: ***ex nihilo omne ens qua ens fit***, every being as being comes from the nothing, because beings first show themselves as beings to a Dasein "held out into the nothing". Hence wonder and the question "Why are there beings at all, and not rather nothing?"
- The 1943 Postscript adds that this "nothing" is really **Being** itself, which is not a being.

Nishitani's verdict: Heidegger came closest of the Westerners, but his abyss is still an abyss *for* the self, "some 'thing' that is nothingness".

## 5. The Christian Nihil

The Kyoto writers mostly know Christianity through Augustine, Eckhart, Luther, Kierkegaard and Barth. The Greek Fathers give a different shape.

**Three "nothings" that must not be confused**
1. ***Mē on***: relative non-being, Plato's and Aristotle's matter, what has not yet received form. Lossky: Greek "being" means ordered existence, and matter is "non-being, a pure potentiality", but "not the *ouk on*, which is absolute nothingness".
2. ***Ouk on***: absolute nothing, the *nihil* of creation. It is not a stuff or a place. "God made them of things that were not" (2 Macc 7:28; compare Rom 4:17).
3. **The "nothing" of apophatic theology**: God is "not" any being because He is *beyond* being (*hyperousios*), not because He lacks it. Dionysius calls God "no thing among things", and also not non-being.

**Philaret and Lossky on created nothingness**
- Metropolitan Philaret of Moscow: "All creatures are balanced upon the creative word of God, as if upon a bridge of diamond; above them is the abyss of the divine infinitude, below them that of their own nothingness."
- Lossky: "The nothingness of creatures is as mysterious and unimaginable as the divine Nothingness of apophatic theology." To say nothingness exists is a contradiction. Yet by creation God "makes room" for something wholly outside Himself, "not by place but by nature".
- And against Eckhart, explicitly: Eastern apophaticism "is not an impersonal mysticism, an experience of the absolute, divine nothingness in which both the human person and God as person are swallowed up". Its goal "is not a nature or an essence, nor is it a person; it is something which transcends all notion both of nature and of person: it is **the Trinity**". In the West, theology that begins with the nature before the persons "runs the risk… of becoming a mysticism of 'the divine abyss', as in the *Gottheit* of Meister Eckhart". See [[The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church]] and [[Apophatic and Cataphatic Theology]].

**Athanasius: sin as the slide back to nothing**
- "The human being is by nature mortal, having come into being from nothing." He stays in being only by grace, by the likeness to the Word. The transgression "returned them to the natural state, so that, just as they, not being, came to be, so also they might rightly endure in time the corruption unto non-being" (*Inc.* 4). And plainly: "evil is non-being, the good is being". Evil is not a rival principle but a *privation*, a return toward the nothing.
- So the Incarnation is the Word entering the creature at the edge of the abyss, taking on the body that was sliding toward non-being, and filling it with life. See [[On the Incarnation: Main Argument]] and [[Creation out of Nothing]].

## 7. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What the Kyoto School gets right**
- The diagnosis of the modern self is excellent. The Cartesian ego "shut up within itself", the dead mechanical world around it, the twin outcome of mechanised man and desire-driven man, the nihility under mass entertainment: this is Pascal's *divertissement* and Fr. Seraphim Rose's nihilism seen from Kyoto. The Fathers would call it *philautia*, self-love, the root of the passions (Maximus). See [[Descartes: The Cogito and Mind-Body Dualism]], [[Nietzsche on Nihilism and Its Overcoming]] and [[The Underground Man and the Death of God: Dostoevsky, Shestov, Girard]].
- Nishitani on sin is closer to Orthodoxy than to Western legalism. Sin is not first a list of acts but a condition at the root of the self, shared by all, "the evil of all men within the evil of the self". That is the Eastern reading of Adam: a disease of nature we all inherit, not inherited guilt. And his answer to Barth and Brunner, that the "point of contact" is the awareness of one's own nothingness, is what the ascetic Fathers call **humility**. "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit" (Ps 51:17).
- Tanabe's *zange* is the most Christian thing in the school. A professor driven to despair by his own cowardice, letting go, and finding that "it is no longer I who pursue philosophy": this is the Publican, and the Prodigal "when he came to himself" (Luke 15:17). Climacus calls repentance "the daughter of hope and the refusal to despair". Tanabe's "continual zange" is the Jesus Prayer's "have mercy on me" said to the end of life. See [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]] and [[The Ladder of Divine Ascent]].
- "Beneath the Great Death, the Great Enlightenment" has a Christian form: "Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone" (John 12:24); "ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God" (Col 3:3); baptism as burial (Rom 6:4). The monastic tradition says "die before you die".
- Nishitani's pebble and blade of grass that should make the Christian tremble as Moses did is just the Orthodox sense of creation as burning bush, where every *logos* of every thing is from God. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].

**Where they go wrong: three confusions**
- **They read the Christian God as "Being", and the Fathers didn't.** Abe's chart, with everything slanting toward capital-B Being, fits Tillich and Thomism fairly well. It does not fit Dionysius, for whom God is *beyond* being and non-being alike: "It is neither one nor oneness, divinity nor goodness"; it falls "neither within the predicate of nonbeing nor of being" (*MT* 5). Formally that is Abe's double negation. The difference is what lies beyond the negations. For Abe it is "Emptiness"; for Dionysius it is the **superabundant Good**, the first divine name, ahead even of Being (*DN* IV–V), and finally the Trinity. So the real disagreement is not "Being versus Nothing" but "Goodness and Persons" versus "Emptiness". See [[Heidegger and the God Beyond Being]].
- **They read Eckhart as the summit of Christian apophaticism, and the East reads him as a warning.** Eckhart's godhead beyond the Trinity is just what Lossky names as the Western danger: a nature before the persons, an "impersonal apophaticism of the divine nothingness prior to the Trinity". It is no accident that Nishitani finds Eckhart nearest to Buddhism. The Orthodox answer is the **essence/energies distinction**. God is wholly unknowable in essence, so the apophatic is real, but He is truly present in His uncreated energies, and He is always the Father, Son and Spirit, never a faceless ground behind them. See [[Essence-Energies Distinction]].
- **They make good and evil equal, and that is not realism but the end of ethics.** Abe says good "is not strong enough to overcome evil" and wants to be "master of good and evil". Christianity agrees that *our* good is not strong enough; that is the whole point of grace (Rom 7). But evil is not an equal force. It is privation, a parasite on good with no being of its own (Athanasius, Dionysius *DN* IV.19–35). And good *has* overcome it, not in an idea but in an event: "Be of good cheer; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33). The history of the school is a sober footnote here. Nishitani and others lent their "standpoint beyond good and evil" to Japan's wartime ideology in the 1942 "Overcoming Modernity" symposium (see *Nothingness in the Heart of Empire*, in the library). Tanabe's metanoetics was in part a confession of exactly that.

**Kenosis and circuminsession: borrowed words**
- Nishitani's best Christian insight is kenosis: love empties itself. But he turns the Son's free act in the economy into a property of the divine nature ("*kenosis* for the Father… in his original perfection"). The Fathers say the opposite: the Word emptied Himself *while remaining what He was* (Cyril). Kenosis is the act of a **Person** who has something to give, not the emptiness of a nature with nothing in it. "Though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor" (2 Cor 8:9). You cannot empty yourself if there is no self.
- His "circuminsession" of all things, each "master and servant" to all, is a lovely picture of the world. But *perichōrēsis* in the Fathers is the mutual indwelling of **Persons** who remain distinct ("without confusion"), or of natures in Christ. Applied to the cosmos it is Maximus' vision of all things united in the Logos, each keeping its own *logos*. The Orthodox can take Nishitani's picture, but only with Persons at its centre. See [[Being as Communion]].

**"Personally impersonal" and the sun on the just and unjust**
- Nishitani is right that God's love does not pick favourites and that "the personal" in the modern sense of a choosing ego is too small for God. But the answer is not to go *beneath* the personal into an impersonal ground. It is to see that the **person** is not the ego. The Cartesian "I" is closed in on itself. The person in the Fathers is ecstatic, in communion, the image of the Trinity. Nishitani's critique hits Descartes and Sartre, not the Cappadocians. See [[Nature and Person Distinction in Christology]].
- The sun rising on evil and good alike is, in Isaac the Syrian, the same love received differently: as light by the saints, as torment by those who hate it. God's love is "non-differentiating" in Nishitani's sense, and yet judgement is real. See [[Hell as the River of Fire]].

**Heidegger and the nihil**
- Heidegger's *ex nihilo omne ens qua ens fit* is a fine phrase for the experience of wonder: things show up as *being at all* only against the background of their possible non-being. Christianity agrees and adds who stands behind it. The nothing does not "nihilate" by itself. It is the place where the creature hears the Word say "Let there be". Anxiety before the nothing is real (Kierkegaard knew it), but the end is not dread but **thanksgiving**: "O Lord, how manifold are thy works!" (Ps 104:24). That is a eucharistic answer to the question "Why is there something rather than nothing?" The answer is a gift.

**What I take from it**
- Three abysses, not one: the abyss of our created nothingness *below* (Philaret), the abyss of divine infinity *above*, and the abyss of sin that we dig ourselves, which is a turning toward the first. The Kyoto School is right that the way through is death to the self. It is wrong that what we find beyond is an emptiness. We find a Face. "For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face" (1 Cor 13:12).
- Nishitani says Christian love, like Plato's eros, is still a "90° turn", one directional. The Fathers' answer, Dionysius' circle of divine eros going out and returning, is closer to his 360° than he knew. But the circle runs between Persons, which is why it is love and not just emptiness. See [[Eros and Agape: Plato, Nygren, Dionysius and the Fathers]].
