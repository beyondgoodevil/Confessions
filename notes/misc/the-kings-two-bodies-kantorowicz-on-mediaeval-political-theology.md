Ernst Kantorowicz published *The King's Two Bodies* in 1957. Its starting point is a doctrine of the Elizabethan law courts: the king has two bodies. One is a natural body that ages and dies. The other is a "body politic" that cannot be seen, cannot die, and passes to each new king. Maitland had called this "a marvelous display of metaphysical — or we might say metaphysiological — nonsense" (p. 3). Kantorowicz agrees that it looks absurd. He sets out to show that it was not a whim of Tudor lawyers. It was the end of a long chain of ideas running back through medieval law, theology and liturgy, and those ideas helped make the modern state.

The book's own account of its aim is modest. It wants "to make problems visible rather than to solve them" (p. xxxvi), and it traces how "certain axioms of a political theology which mutatis mutandis was to remain valid until the twentieth century, began to be developed during the later Middle Ages" (p. xxxiv). The chapters follow a sequence of models of kingship: Christ-centred, law-centred, polity-centred, and finally, in Dante, man-centred. Between them come long chapters on continuity: how a mortal ruler could be tied to something that does not die.

The note follows the book. Sections 1–2 cover the Tudor doctrine and *Richard II*. Sections 3–4 cover Christ-centred kingship, sections 5–8 law-centred kingship, and sections 9–10 the polity and the fatherland. Sections 11–14 are the problem of continuity, section 15 is Dante and section 16 the Epilogue. Section 17 gives the two modern assessments printed with the book.

### 1. The problem: Plowden's Reports

The doctrine appears in its fullest form in Edmund Plowden's reports of cases argued under Elizabeth. In the case of the Duchy of Lancaster the judges said that "the King has in him two Bodies, viz., a Body natural, and a Body politic." The body natural is "a Body mortal, subject to all Infirmities that come by Nature or Accident." The body politic is "a Body that cannot be seen or handled, consisting of Policy and Government, and constituted for the Direction of the People, and the Management of the public weal, and this Body is utterly void of Infancy, and old Age" (p. 7). Because the body politic cannot err, acts done by the king in that capacity cannot be undone on the ground that he was a minor.

The two bodies are not separate. "The Body politic includes the Body natural, but the Body natural is the lesser," and "these two Bodies are incorporated in one Person, and make one Body and not divers" (p. 9). The greater body cleans the lesser. In *Willion v. Berkley* the judges said that "the Body politic wipes away every Imperfection of the other Body" (p. 11); this is why an attainted man who took the crown, as Henry VII did in 1485, was cleared of the attainder by taking it. When the king dies, the law does not call it death but "the Demise of the King": a "Separation of the two Bodies," in which the body politic is "conveyed over" to a new body natural (p. 13).

The doctrine reached into odd places. In *Hales v. Petit* a suicide was held to have offended "against the King in that hereby he has lost a Subject, and (as Brown termed it) he being the Head has lost one of his mystic Members" (p. 15). Coke, in *Calvin's Case*, said that the politic capacity "is invisible and immortal, nay, the politic body hath no soul, for it is framed by the policy of man" (p. 15).

Kantorowicz's first point is that these formulas look like theology, because they are built the way theology is built. Maitland had said the lawyers were making "a creed of royalty which shall take no shame if set beside the Athanasian symbol" (p. 17). Kantorowicz goes further. The relation of the king's two bodies was argued with the same tools the Church used for the two natures of Christ. Chalcedon's terms — unconfounded, unchanged, undivided, unseparated — have their legal echoes. The judges had to steer between positions that look like the old heresies. They avoided a "Nestorian" split by always stressing the unity of the two bodies. But their indifference to the mortal man in whom the body politic happened to dwell has "a touch of 'Monophysitism'," and the Puritan cry "We fight the king to defend the King" "clearly points in the monophysitic direction" (pp. 17–18). In his words, "the fiction of the King's Two Bodies produced interpretations and definitions which perforce would resemble those produced in view of the Two Natures of the God-man" (pp. 18–19).

The doctrine had a practical edge. In 1642 Parliament raised armies in the name of the king's body politic against Charles I in his body natural. The Declaration of 27 May 1642 said that the king's acts of justice and protection "are not exercised in his own Person, nor depend upon his pleasure," and so what Parliament did "hath the stamp of Royal Authority, although His Majesty ... do in his own Person oppose" it (p. 21). Kantorowicz notes the result. Parliament could execute "solely the king's body natural" without harming the King, unlike the French in 1793, who abolished the kingship along with the king (p. 23).

### 2. Shakespeare's Richard II

Kantorowicz gives a whole chapter to Shakespeare because "the Tragedy of King Richard II is the tragedy of the King's Two Bodies" (p. 26). The lawyers' language was common talk by the 1590s; one play of the period has a character say, "I have plodded in Plowden, and can find no law" (p. 25).

He reads the play as three scenes of doubling, each one falling lower than the last. At the coast of Wales, Richard is the King. At Flint Castle he is the Fool. At Westminster he is a God-like figure in his passion. The three dissolve in the mirror scene, and as Richard says in prison, "Thus play I in one person many people" (p. 27).

In Wales Richard begins with the doctrine at full strength: "The breath of worldly man cannot depose / The deputy elected by the Lord" (p. 27). Within the same scene, as bad news arrives, it breaks apart. He moves from "Is not the king's name twenty thousand names?" (p. 29) to "Let's talk of graves, of worms and epitaphs" (p. 30), and to the hollow crown within which "Keeps Death his court." Kantorowicz's line is: "The king that 'never dies' here has been replaced by the king that always dies" (p. 30). Richard's "I live with bread like you ... How can you say to me, I am a king?" (p. 31) states the body natural without its partner.

At Westminster the un-kinging is staged as a rite turned inside out. "With mine own tears I wash away my balm" (pp. 35–37) undoes the anointing. Richard compares his betrayers to Judas and Pilate. He then admits, "I find myself a traitor with the rest" (IV.i.244): he is a traitor to his own body politic. Kantorowicz sees this as the case of 1649 played out in advance (pp. 38–39). In the mirror he sees a face with none of its kingship left: "a physis now void of any metaphysis whatsoever. It is both less and more than Death. It is the demise of Richard" (pp. 39–40).

The play was political. The deposition scene was not printed under Elizabeth. Essex's followers paid for a performance on the eve of his rising in 1601, and Elizabeth is reported to have said, "I am Richard II, know ye not that?" (pp. 40–41).

### 3. Christ-centred kingship: the Norman Anonymous

To find where the two bodies came from, Kantorowicz goes back to about 1100, to a set of anti-Gregorian tracts by an unknown Norman cleric, the "Norman Anonymous." Here the king is a *gemina persona*, a twin person: "one descending from nature, the other from grace ... by grace, a Christus, that is, a God-man" (p. 46). The king is not a twin by birth. He becomes one when he is anointed and the Spirit "leaps" into him. "The power of the king is the power of God. This power, namely, is God's by nature, and the king's by grace. Hence, the king, too, is God and Christ, but by grace" (p. 48).

The parallel with Christ is close, and Kantorowicz notes that it was dogmatically unsafe. Orthodox doctrine speaks of one person in two natures, not of a "twin person." The Spanish councils had warned that a *geminata persona* would add "a Quaternity to the Trinity" (p. 50). The Anonymous knew those councils, and his king who becomes divine sits close to adoptionist language (p. 52).

Two of his arguments look forward. In one he separates office from person. On the tribute coin, Christ told men to render to the power, not to the person: "Iniquitous is Tiberius, but good is the Caesar" (p. 54). Kantorowicz calls this "the strangest chiasmus imaginable": the pagan's office is haloed while Christ, in his humanity, stands in servitude without a halo (p. 55). In the other he divides the two natures between the two orders. The king images Christ's divinity and the priest his humanity, so the king outranks the bishop (p. 56). For Kantorowicz the Anonymous's doubling is "not founded in law or constitution, but in theology" (p. 58). It had no practical effect. The Reform Papacy took the sacred to itself, and the new states were built on law. He "actually sums up the political ideas of the tenth and eleventh centuries" (p. 60), like "a kind of mirror that magnifies, and thereby slightly distorts" (p. 61).

### 4. The Aachen Gospels and the halo of perpetuity

The same idea appears in a picture: the frontispiece of the Aachen Gospels (c. 975). An Ottonian emperor sits enthroned in a mandorla, crowned by the Hand of God. The four beasts of the Evangelists hold a white band across his chest. His head and shoulders are above the band, in heaven, and his feet rest on the earth goddess Tellus (pp. 62–66). Kantorowicz reads the band as the veil of the tabernacle, the sky that divides earth from heaven, and the figure as an emperor in the pose of Christ. The source is Augustine on Psalm 90, which pictures Christ with his head in heaven and his feet on earth. One of Kantorowicz's own phrases sums it up: "pedes in terra, caput in coelo" (p. 73). The model is two-natured Christ, so the Ottonian idea of rule "was not theocentric: it was decisively christocentric," a "liturgical kingship" (p. 78).

A short section on the halo connects this to the theme of time. In late antique art a halo marked something that continued beyond the individual. Provinces in the *Notitia dignitatum* wear halos, and so do Rome and the emperor's genius. To put a halo on a figure moved it "from tempus to aevum, from Time to Sempiternity" (p. 84). Aevum, the time of ideas and angels, will come back in section 11. Here it already explains why the Tudor body politic resembles "the holy sprites and angels."

### 5. From liturgy to law

The next model is law-centred kingship. Kantorowicz follows a slow change in titles. In the earlier Middle Ages the king was the image and vicar of Christ. The Reform Papacy then claimed "vicar of Christ" for the pope alone, and Huguccio could still say that every priest is a vicar of Christ except as to the plenitude of power (p. 91). The secular ruler became the vicar of God instead, with the civilians calling the emperor *deus in terris*. "Henceforth a papal Christus in terris was sided by an imperial deus in terris" (p. 92). The late medieval divine right of kings was "modelled after the Father in Heaven rather than after the Son on the Altar" (p. 93).

John of Salisbury's *Policraticus* (c. 1159) marks the turn. Its chapter heading states that the prince, "although he be not bound by the ties of Law, is yet Law's servant as well as that of Equity; that he bears a public person" (p. 95). The doubling has moved. It now lies inside the prince's public person, which is at once "legibus solutus and legibus alligatus, ... at once lord and serf of the Law" (p. 96). John's prince is the image of equity, and his doubling is "mirrored by Law" rather than by the two natures (p. 97).

### 6. Frederick II: father and son of Justice

Frederick II's Constitutions of Melfi (1231) push the legal model to its height. A law on justice explains that the Roman people conferred power on the prince so that "from the very same person ... there might progress the origin of Justice, from whom also the defence of Justice proceeds." The conclusion is: "The Caesar, therefore, must be at once the Father and Son of Justice, her lord and her minister" (pp. 98–99). The model is Christ and the Virgin ("Vergine madre, figlia del tuo figlio"), not the two natures. The emperor is "et maior et minor se ipso," greater and less than himself (p. 100).

The jurists had a pair of texts for this. The *lex regia* made the prince free from law. The *lex digna* made him profess himself bound by it: "It is a word worthy of the majesty of the ruler that the Prince professes himself bound to the Law" (p. 104). Frederick held that his majesty, though free from all laws, "is nevertheless not altogether exalted above the judgment of Reason, herself the Mother of all Law" (p. 106). Kantorowicz notes the danger in this: *ratio* bound to the king and the fatherland leads toward reason of state (p. 107).

Justice became a goddess with her own cult. Lawyers read the opening of the *Digest*, "Deservedly may we be called of this art the priests: for we worship Justice" (p. 120), as if it were literal. The glossator Accursius wrote: "Just as the priests minister and confection things holy, so do we, since the laws are most sacred" (pp. 120–121). The judge was a "priest of justice," and the profession was a kind of knighthood. The emperor was *lex animata*, the living law. Justinian's Novel 105 said God sent him "down to men as the living Law" (p. 127). Aegidius Romanus put it in a formula: "the Law is a kind of inanimate prince; the prince, however, a kind of animate Law" (p. 134).

Kantorowicz sums up the change. The just king survived "at the price of being transferred from the altar to the bench, from the realm of Grace to that of Jurisprudence," and Maitland's joke fits: the judge became "a priest for ever after the order of Ulpian" (p. 140). "Grace" now had no place. The pairing was "Law of Nature and laws of man" (p. 142). Justice was "the goddess of the age in which jurisprudence took the lead," and the prince was "the hypostasis of an immortal Idea ... both her incarnation and her Pontifex maximus" (p. 143).

### 7. Bracton: above and under the law

In England the same materials produced something more sober. "To accept domination by an abstract idea has never been a weakness of England, though a useful fiction might more readily be accepted" (p. 147). Bracton did not ask whether the king was father and son of Justice. He asked whether he was "above or under the Law." His answer was both. The king had rights that "make the Crown what it is" and could not be sued (p. 149), yet "Laws, and not the person, make the king" (p. 150).

Bracton read the Roman maxim "what pleases the prince has the force of law" in a constitutional way: what pleases the prince is "not what has been rashly presumed by the [personal] will of the king, but what has been rightly defined by the consilium of his magnates" (p. 152). The Italian jurists made the prince's councillors his mouth. Bracton made the king the mouth of the council (p. 154). His method Kantorowicz calls "exaltation through limitation": the king is "vicar of God only when and where he acts 'God-like' by submitting to the Law" (p. 155). Bracton grounds this in Christ, who chose "not the strength of power, but the maxim of Justice, and therefore he wished to be under the Law in order to redeem those under the Law" (p. 156).

England also shows "the king's impersonal alter ego" at work in government. In 1292 the judges said the king was by prerogative above the laws "for the common utility" and yet "the debtor of justice" (p. 163). A writ, perhaps a joke, was said to have King Henry III command King Henry III. Master Simon the Norman lost his post for refusing to seal a charter he judged *contra coronam*, against the Crown (pp. 163–164).

### 8. Christus and Fiscus

The strangest pairing in the book comes from fiscal law. Royal lands and rights set apart under Henry II as the "ancient demesne" were "a suprapersonal compound of rights and lands which was separate from the individual king and definitely not his private property" (p. 167). These things were *res quasi sacrae*, almost holy things, and no more alienable than church property. Time did not run against them: *nullum tempus currit contra regem*. As to time, then, the king was a *gemina persona*, bound by time in some things and above it in others (p. 172). The line between them did not run between public king and private man. It ran between "a king feudal and a king fiscal": matters between lord and vassal on one side, matters "that touch all" on the other (p. 172).

In 1441 a judge, John Paston, said in court that "what is not snatched by Christus, is snatched by the Fiscus" (pp. 173–174). Alciati put the motto in an emblem book of 1531, and it spread across Europe. It came from Gratian, who took it from a sermon on tithes (p. 175). For the lawyers the pairing was natural, because Church and fisc shared one feature: both were inalienable and both "never died" (pp. 176–177). They called the fisc *sacratissimus*. They said it was everywhere at once, and Baldus concluded that "the fisc is omnipresent and in that, therefore, the fisc resembles God" (p. 184). Kantorowicz insists these were figures of speech — "sanctus Fiscus was never included in the official catalogue of saints" (p. 185) — but figures that did real work. By the end the fisc could be said to "represent State and Prince," and he asks "whether it is logic or irony of history that the solemn Roman cult of gods and public functions should be found at the root of modern deification and idolization of state mechanisms" (p. 189).

He closes the law-centred chapter with a summary. "The ancient idea of liturgical kingship gradually dissolved, and it gave way to a new pattern of kingship centered on the sphere of Law which was not wanting its own mysticism." The old pair of priesthood and kingship "was superseded by the new dichotomy of the King and the Law" (p. 192).

### 9. Polity-centred kingship: the corpus mysticum

The next stage takes its language from the Church. Kantorowicz first traces a reversal in terms. In Carolingian theology *corpus mysticum* meant the consecrated host, and the Church was the *corpus Christi* of St. Paul. After the eucharistic controversies of the eleventh century, the host came to be called the *corpus verum* or *corpus naturale*. From about 1150 *corpus mysticum* was transferred to the Church as an organised society: "the expression 'mystical body,' which originally had a liturgical or sacramental meaning, took on a connotation of sociological content" (p. 196). Boniface VIII's *Unam sanctam* (1302) defined the Church as "one mystical body, the head of which is Christ, and the head of Christ is God" (p. 194).

Around 1200 theologians began to speak of the Lord's two bodies. Simon of Tournai wrote: "Two are the bodies of Christ: the human material body which he assumed from the Virgin, and the spiritual collegiate body, the ecclesiastical college" (p. 198). This was not the old distinction of two natures. It was "a sociological distinction between an individual body and a collective body," and here, Kantorowicz says, "we seem to have found the precise precedent of the 'King's two Bodies'" (pp. 198–199). The mystical body soon lost much of its mystery. Aquinas could call head and members "quasi una persona mystica," and Sohm's phrase is quoted: "The corpus Christi has been changed into a corporation of Christ" (p. 202).

The term then passed to secular bodies. Vincent of Beauvais spoke of the *corpus reipublicae mysticum* (p. 208). Baldus defined a people as "men assembled into one mystical body," and Kantorowicz comments that the term "brought to the secular polity, as it were, a whiff of incense from another world" (p. 210). Aristotle supplied a second source. For Godfrey of Fontaines every man is by nature, not by grace, "part of a social community, and thereby also a member of some mystical body" (p. 211).

The jurists added a marriage. The bishop was married to his see, and Lucas de Penna transferred the image to the prince: "the Prince is the head of the realm, and the realm the body of the Prince"; "Just as Christ joined to himself an alien-born as his spouse, the Church of Gentiles ..., so has the Prince joined to himself as his sponsa the state, which is not his" (p. 216). The fisc was the dowry, and a husband could not alienate his wife's dowry. In France this became coronation ritual. From 1547 the rubric for the ring said the king "solemnly married his realm" (p. 222).

In England the image stayed organic. For Fortescue the kingdom issues from the people "as a corpus mysticum governed by one man as head," held together by law as a body is by its nerves (p. 224). A Speaker in 1401 compared king, Lords and Commons to a trinity and the session of Parliament to a mass (p. 227). Henry VIII told his Parliament in 1542 that "we at no time stand so highly in our estate royal as in the time of Parliament, wherein we as head and you as members are conjoined and knit together in one body politic" (p. 228). Kantorowicz's summary: "The continuity, first guaranteed by Christ, then by the Law, was now guaranteed by the corpus mysticum of the realm which, so to speak, never died" (p. 231).

### 10. Pro patria mori

If the realm was a mystical body like the Church, death for it could be like death for the faith. In the feudal age the fatherland was a local word, and men died "pro domino, not pro patria" (p. 234). Only the Church kept the old meaning, transferred to heaven. The Christian's true patria was the heavenly Jerusalem, and the martyr remained "the genuine model of civic self-sacrifice" (pp. 234–235).

Kantorowicz traces how the model came back to earth. Crusade taxes "for the defence of the Holy Land" became taxes "for the defence of the realm" (p. 235). Philip IV raised money "for the defense of the native fatherland" (p. 236). The crusader's promise of heaven was extended to men who died for the king. The Song of Roland has Archbishop Turpin say: "If so you die, you shall be holy martyrs" (p. 240). Geoffrey of Monmouth has a bishop tell Arthur's army that whoever "suffers death for his brothers, offers himself a living host to God" (p. 241). Tolomeo of Lucca, writing under Aquinas's name, said that "the amor patriae deserves a rank of honor above all other virtues" (p. 242). So "in the thirteenth century the crown of martyrdom began to descend on the war victims of the secular state" (p. 244).

The propaganda of Philip IV against Boniface VIII shows the system complete. A French sermon of 1302 declared that "those who die for the justice of the king and of the kingdom [of France] shall be crowned by God as martyrs" (p. 256). A royal pamphlet called any cleric who would not pay "a useless and quasi paralytic limb" (p. 258). "The corpus mysticum patriae was set over against the corpus mysticum ecclesiae" (p. 258). Kantorowicz sees "reason of state" begin "in the garb of justice" (p. 257). He has little patience for the humanists who took it further. Salutati wrote that for the fatherland it would be no crime to put the axe to one's father's head. Kantorowicz calls this "scholarly blood-lust and overheated desk patriotism" (p. 246), and adds that "horrors justified by the names of God or patria are as old as they are new" (p. 246).

Then comes a turn. Kantorowicz asks whether the two bodies of Christ simply produced the two bodies of the king, and answers no. The organic image made the king the head of a body. But "nowhere do we find, merely on the basis of the organic concept of the state, the idea expressed that the king as the head of the body politic has two bodies" (p. 269). To say "there is one body of the king which is he himself, and another body of which he is the head" would be "a definition without consequence ... amounting to nothing" (p. 270). The missing piece was eternity. Christ was eternal by nature; the king's immortality had to come from somewhere else. It came from "the universitas 'which never dies'," from "the perpetuity of an immortal people, polity, or patria" (p. 272).

### 11. Continuity: aevum and the angels

"Undoubtedly the concept of the 'king's two bodies' camouflaged a problem of continuity" (p. 273). Kantorowicz's chapter on continuity is the philosophical centre of the book. For Augustine, time was created, finite and morally low; *temporalis* meant passing. The thirteenth century recovered Aristotle's doctrine that the world is eternal, and the Averroists pushed it far. The Church condemned their theses, but something shifted anyway. Time became "a vivifying element, a symbol of endless duration, of Life" (p. 277). Fame gained new force as a "secular substitute for the immortal beatitude" of the other world (p. 278).

Scholastic philosophy already had a category for endless time with motion in it: *aevum*, which Aquinas placed "in the middle between aeternitas and tempus" (p. 280). God has eternity, man has time, and the angels have aevum. Aquinas also taught that each angel is its own species. That gives Kantorowicz his key move. The lawyers' corporations were immortal species with no body, so they "displayed all the features otherwise attributed to angels" (p. 282). In his words, "the political and legal world of thought of the later Middle Ages began to be populated by immaterial angelic bodies, large and small: they were invisible, ageless, sempiternal, immortal, and sometimes even ubiquitous" (p. 283). Men did not come to believe the world was endless, "but one began to act as though it were endless" (p. 283).

Government practice moved the same way. Feudal taxes were tied to events: a ransom, a knighting, a dowry, an emergency. The "necessity of the realm" could be declared every year, and in the end taxation was "linked to the calendar, to the eternally rolling wheel of Time. The state had become permanent, and permanent were its emergencies and needs" (p. 286). The jurist Oldradus de Ponte distinguished an actual necessity from an "habitual" one. Kantorowicz compares this *perpetua necessitas* to the modern idea of "perpetual revolution" (pp. 286–287). Permanent envoys and annual registers tell the same story (pp. 290–291).

### 12. The corporation that never dies

The lawyers built continuity into bodies. "The Church never dies"; the empire was "forever." The *Glossa ordinaria* gave the classic image: "just as the [present] people of Bologna is the same that was a hundred years ago, even though all be dead now who then were quick," so a court, a legion, and a ship rebuilt plank by plank remain the same (p. 295). Baldus: "the commonweal cannot die," and for that reason "the commonweal has no heir, because in itself it lives for ever" (p. 299). As a Christian he would have confessed that the world was created. As a lawyer he needed an endless world. Kantorowicz calls this a lawyer's "double truth" (p. 300).

These corporations were not like the city goddesses of antiquity, who had visible bodies because they were gods. The cities of the jurists "were actually deprived of their visible body," and the lawyers made "angelomorphic" personifications rather than anthropomorphic ones (pp. 303–304). He coins a word for the abstraction: "Bononitas or 'Bolognity'," which, "like the angels," was species and individual at once (pp. 302–303). Innocent IV forbade the excommunication of a corporation in 1245 because a corporation is a name of law, not a person, and has neither soul nor body (pp. 305–306). Aquinas could still call fiction *figura veritatis*, and Baldus wrote that "fiction imitates nature" (p. 306).

What makes such a body immortal is succession. A corporation is not those "living together at the same moment" but a "plurality in succession, braced by Time" (p. 310). Once that was seen, the step to the head was short. One could build a corporation whose "members were echeloned longitudinally so that its cross-section at any given moment revealed one instead of many members" (pp. 312–313). This "curious concept" solved the problem of an immortal head (p. 313).

### 13. The king never dies: dynasty and Crown

Kantorowicz finds three strands in the Tudor "king that never dies": the dynasty, the Crown and the Dignity (p. 316).

*Dynasty.* Royal anointing lost its legal force. Innocent III moved the king's unction from the head to the arm and from chrism to plain oil, "a complete reversal of the former idea of Christ-like and Christ-centered kingship" (p. 319). The pope here was "the chief promoter of precisely that 'secularism' which in other respects the Holy See tended to fight" (pp. 320–321). In 1270 Philip III, and in 1272 Edward I, began to reign and count their years from their accession, while both were still abroad (pp. 328–329). Coke's later judgement fits: coronation is "but a royal ornament and solemnization of the royal descent, but no part of the title" (p. 317). Cranmer told Edward VI that if the oil "be wanting, that king is yet a perfect monarch notwithstanding" (p. 318). Grace moved from the sacrament into the blood: "the royal blood now appeared as a somewhat mysterious fluid" (p. 331). The old fiction of Christ as interrex during a vacancy — "while Christ rules expecting a king" (p. 335) — was no longer needed.

*Crown.* In England "Crown" belonged first to administration: pleas of the Crown, lands pertaining to the Crown (pp. 342–344). Kantorowicz traces the king's promise not to alienate Crown rights to canon law. A feudal oath was adopted by the papacy for bishops directly subject to Rome, with an added clause against alienating church property. It came back to England in 1216, when a papal legate crowned Henry III, the king being then a vassal of the pope. "That ecclesiastified, and now pseudo-feudal oath returned in a new guise to the secular state as an oath of office urging the king as well as his officers to protect an impersonal institution which 'never dies,' the Crown" (p. 354). Edward I told the pope he could do nothing "that touches the Diadem of this realm without having resorted to the counsel of prelates and magnates" (p. 362). A poem of the period says: "The leste lyge-man, with body and rent, / He is a parcel of the crowne" (p. 363).

The barons of 1308 tried to set the Crown against the king. They declared that homage is owed "more by reason of the Crown than by reason of the king's person," so that lieges could force the king back to reason (pp. 364–365). Kantorowicz calls it a false choice: the Crown without the king was incomplete. Bacon later answered that "it is one thing to make things distinct, another thing to make them separable" (p. 365). Judges under Edward II went the other way and called the Crown a perpetual minor against whom no time runs. When one slipped and said the king was always under age, Chief Justice Bereford answered that then "no deed which he makes, would bind him" (pp. 377–378). Kantorowicz's verdict on the Crown is that it was "rarely 'personified' but very often 'bodified'": a composite body of king and lords, and later Commons, not the abstract State of the Continent (p. 382).

### 14. Dignitas non moritur: the phoenix, the effigy and the tomb

The third strand came from canon law. Alexander III ruled that a commission given to "the Abbot of Winchester" passed to the next abbot. The canonist Damasus wrote: "The Dignity never perishes, although individuals die every day" (p. 385). Bernard of Parma's gloss gave the reason: "predecessor and successor are understood as one person, since the Dignity does not die" (p. 386). Bernard also compared such a name to the phoenix, a "singular" name (p. 388). Baldus drew the lesson: "The Phoenix is a unique and most singular bird in which the whole kind (genus) is conserved in the individual" (p. 389). The ancient poets had called the phoenix its own father and its own heir. In the jurists' hands, "there arose from the ashes of the Phoenix metaphor the prototype of that spectre called the 'Corporation sole'" (p. 394). Baldus distinguished in the king a *persona personalis* and a *persona idealis*, "which is the Dignity." The Dignity "miraculously perseveres forever, though not corporeally," so that the king "functions holding the place of two persons" (p. 400).

English practice took this up. Judges called an abbot a "body politic" and spoke of "that mystical body of the abbot which never dies" (p. 406). In 1461 Edward IV had Parliament turn the Duchy of Lancaster into a corporation, "THE DUCHY OF LANCASTER (one is inclined to add: LTD. or INC.)" (p. 404). In *Hill v. Grange* (1556–57) the judges said that "King is a name of continuance ... and in this the King never dies" (p. 407). The Tudor lawyers' own contribution was to replace "Dignity" with "Body politic" (p. 406).

Kantorowicz then turns to ceremony. The custom behind the cry "Le roi est mort! Vive le roi!" began at the funeral of Charles VI in 1422. The English regent, the Duke of Bedford, had a herald proclaim the infant Henry VI at the grave to forestall the Dauphin (pp. 410–411). The short form without names appears first in 1515 (p. 411). Kantorowicz keeps it apart from the lawyers' maxim that the king never dies, which has a different history (p. 410). At French royal funerals the presidents of the Parlement wore scarlet rather than mourning, "because the Crown and Justice never die" (p. 417). The banner of France was dipped and raised again, "car elle ne meurt jamais" (p. 419).

The effigy came from England, where it is recorded from the burial of Edward II in 1327 (p. 420). It was a figure dressed in coronation robes with crown, orb and sceptre: "a persona ficta — the effigy — impersonating a persona ficta — the Dignitas" (p. 421). In France it took on a life of its own. At the death of Francis I in 1547 the effigy lay in state in a room without mourning, and meals were served to it "as if he had been living" (p. 426). The new king could not appear in the procession, since he "could not at once wear mourning and not wear mourning" (p. 429). The double tombs of the fifteenth century show the same split. Archbishop Chichele at Canterbury lies twice: as a skeletal corpse within the tomb and in full pontificals on top (pp. 433–434). "The incumbent of a Dignity may decay, the Dignity itself is nonetheless forever" (pp. 435–436).

Kantorowicz finds a moral in this. The jurists "discovered the immortality of the Dignity; but by this very discovery they made the ephemeral nature of the mortal incumbent all the more tangible" (p. 436). It was the age of the dance of death, "where all Dignities danced with Death." Immortality, "vulgarized by the artifice of countless fictions," needed constant new mortal bearers: "The King could not die, was not allowed to die" (p. 437). Yet "since life becomes transparent only against the background of death, and death against the background of life, the bone-rattling vitality of the late Middle Ages appears not devoid of some deeper wisdom" (p. 437).

The last section of the chapter puts the pieces back together. The Tudor formula — the body corporate in the body natural and the body natural in the body corporate (p. 438) — goes back through Andreas of Isernia and Gratian to Cyprian's "The bishop is in the Church, and the Church in the bishop" (p. 440). Baldus called the Dignity "the principal" and the king's person "the instrumental" (pp. 441–442), borrowing Aquinas's teaching that the humanity of Christ is the living instrument of the Godhead (p. 443). The result was "the dogma of a political Incarnation," a secular copy of the hypostatic union (p. 445). Why only England? Kantorowicz points to Parliament, which "was never a persona ficta or persona repraesentata, but always a very real corpus repraesentans" (p. 447). The organic image of head and members stayed alive there long after it had faded on the Continent. The English lawyers fused the organic Crown with the successive Dignity, and out of that fusion came the king's body politic and the corporation sole (p. 449).

### 15. Man-centred kingship: Dante

The last stage is Dante. "It remained to the poet to establish an image of kingship which was merely human and of which MAN, pure and simple, was the center and standard" (p. 451). (Dante's case for a world monarch in *Monarchy* I is treated in [[The Case for One Ruler: Aquinas, Dante, Maistre, Kuehnelt-Leddihn]]. Kantorowicz's concern is Book III and the *Comedy*.)

Dante keeps office and person apart. He puts Boniface VIII in the hell of the simonists, yet at Anagni he sees "in his Vicar Christ made captive" (p. 454). In *Monarchy* III he argues that emperor and pope both hold office directly from God. As offices they are measured against God. As men they are measured against a human standard: "It is one thing to be man and another to be pope; and just so, it is one thing to be man and another to be emperor," and as men they must be reduced "to the best man (optimus homo), who is the standard of all others and, so to say, their Idea, whosoever he may be" (p. 459). Kantorowicz disagrees with Gilson here. Gilson made the best man a third order, the philosopher, beside pope and emperor. Kantorowicz reads two reference points: God for the office and the best man for the incumbent (pp. 461–462).

Behind this lies Dante's doctrine of two ends. Man, standing like "the horizon" between corruptible and incorruptible, has two goals: the happiness of this life, figured by the earthly paradise, and the happiness of eternal life, figured by the heavenly one (pp. 457–458). The emperor leads to the first by philosophy and the four cardinal virtues; the pope leads to the second by revelation and the three theological virtues. "Dante did not turn humanitas against Christianitas, but thoroughly separated the one from the other; he took the 'human' out of the Christian compound and isolated it as a value in its own right — perhaps Dante's most original accomplishment in the field of political theology" (p. 465). His human community includes Saladin, Avicenna and Averroes, and its best moment was under the pagan Augustus (pp. 465–466). Mankind as a whole has one intellect, actualised "at all times" and "all at the same time" only by the whole race, as a corporation (p. 472). "If ever there existed a secular 'mystical body,' it existed in Dante's humana civilitas," the body of Adam whose head was the emperor (p. 468).

Kantorowicz reads the *Purgatorio* as a baptism into this human body. Cato, the pagan suicide who embodies the four cardinal virtues, guards the shore (p. 486). The seven P's cut on Dante's forehead match the seven scrutinies of the catechumen (p. 488), and the wall of fire is a baptism by fire (p. 488). At the top of the mountain Vergil crowns and mitres him over himself. Baptism, the Fathers said, made every Christian a king and a priest. Dante's crowning is "natura, non gratia, an intellectual and moral 'baptism by transference'," with Cato as sponsor and Vergil as a Baptist "who this time unlocked to man not the heavens, but the paradise of Man" (p. 492). Dante is invested with "that almost objectified 'Dignity of Man' which 'never dies'" (p. 493). The lawyers' theories did not cross his mind, Kantorowicz says, "but the essence of the doctrine of the Two Bodies, of 'Man's Two Bodies,' was just as certainly present to his mind" (p. 493).

### 16. Epilogue: pagan or Christian?

The Epilogue asks whether the idea has pagan roots. Bacon's two maxims for kings are "Memento quod es homo, and Memento quod es Deus, or vice Dei." The first recalls the slave in the Roman triumph who whispered "Remember thou art a man" (p. 496). Kantorowicz lists the pagan parallels honestly. Alexander distinguished a friend of Alexander from a friend of the king (p. 498). Seneca wrote that the pilot of a ship has "two persons": a storm harms him as a passenger but not as a pilot (p. 498). The Neo-Pythagorean treatises on kingship made the king a god among men (pp. 498–500). Caligula's golden statue was dressed each day in the emperor's own clothes (p. 501), and men swore by the emperor's genius to be loyal to the emperor (p. 502). On coins of Constantine the Sun god takes Constantine's face (p. 503).

He weighs these and finds them short. The genius of an emperor was part of the man, not an office he served. Above all, there is nothing in pagan thought for a king having two *bodies*, and it "has a false ring" to call a Roman emperor a corporation sole (p. 505). The line runs from Paul's body of Christ to the mystical body of the Church, then to the mystical body of the commonwealth, then to the abbot as body politic, and so to the king. "The KING'S TWO BODIES is an offshoot of Christian theological thought and consequently stands as a landmark of Christian political theology" (p. 506).

### 17. The book and its readers: Leyser and Jordan

The Princeton Classics edition prints two assessments, one old and one new.

**William Chester Jordan (1997)** surveys the reception. Reviewers hailed it: one called it "perhaps the most important work in the history of medieval political thought," and another "a great book" (p. xxv). Barraclough praised the subtitle "political theology" for catching the reverent, metaphysical side of the subject. The objections came early too. Some reviewers disliked the same phrase because of its link with Carl Schmitt, and English historians wanted more on how the doctrine touched ordinary court cases. Smalley called it a "rich and muddled book"; Southern said reading it was "like walking in a strange country by night along unknown ways: the illumination is fitful, though sometimes spectacular" (pp. xxvi–xxvii). Jordan describes Kantorowicz as conservative "in the old European sense," a Jew who did not practise. He fought the communists in 1919, refused the oath to Hitler in 1934, and lost his Berkeley post in 1950 for refusing the loyalty oath (pp. xxviii–xxix). Jordan adds that he "worked from the footnotes, which were the repository of his learning" (p. xxx).

**Conrad Leyser (2016)** takes up the questions about the author and the book's purpose. Kantorowicz's preface tells of a talk with the lawyer Max Radin in 1945 and an offprint from "The Order of St. Benedict, Inc." (p. xxxiii). Leyser reports later research showing the book was sketched as early as 1935. He sets this against Kantorowicz's past: his place in the Stefan George circle and its "Secret Germany," his 1927 *Frederick II* (an English diarist in 1936 called him "one of Hitler's favourite authors"), and a 1933 letter to the new Nazi rector of Frankfurt showing a "fundamentally positive attitude towards a nationally geared Reich" (pp. xii–xiii). The preface denied that the book grew from "the horrifying experience of our own time," calling such thoughts "afterthoughts" (p. xxxiv). Leyser reads the subtitle as "in tense dialogue with Schmitt" and the whole book as "a shadow play about political culture in the modern age" (p. xv). He suggests the real problem behind it "is not the complex presence of power, but its absence": the empty throne of an interregnum (p. xxi). He also records the critics. It is "an airless book, with very little flesh or blood," and it tells us how the lawyers talked rather than how power was bargained over in practice. Philippe Buc says the book comes out of the very discourse it explains (pp. xviii–xx). Leyser then notes the book's long life in the history of the body, from Foucault to Natalie Zemon Davis (pp. xvii–xviii). He ends with the book's last line: Jerusalem, not Athens, as the source (p. xxii).

The two essays mostly agree. Both see a great and uneven book, and both stress its debt to the footnotes and its silence about the author's own past. They differ in emphasis. Jordan places the book among the scholarship of its time. Leyser reads it as a covert answer to the politics of the 1930s.

### Conclusion

Kantorowicz's thesis is that the modern state's claim to live forever was borrowed, piece by piece, from the Church. First the king copied Christ. Then he served Justice, which had taken over Christ's place. Then he headed a realm described in the words used for the Church. Finally he bore a Dignity that, like the Holy See, "never dies." At each step something holy was moved into the secular sphere and kept its glow there: the halo, the priesthood, the mystical body, martyrdom, inalienable property, the phoenix. The Tudor doctrine of the two bodies is where all this ends. It is the most worked-out version, and it existed only in England, because only England had a Parliament that made the body politic something one could see.

The book shows its argument more than it states it. Kantorowicz rarely claims that one idea caused another. He shows parallels, borrowings and shared language, and he repeats his own warnings against pushing them too far. The cost is the one his critics named: we learn how lawyers and theologians talked, much less how power was actually held. Its lasting gain is the picture of a culture that could see a king's corpse and his crowned effigy carried through Paris on the same day and think nothing of it. In that culture the body politic was not a metaphor.
