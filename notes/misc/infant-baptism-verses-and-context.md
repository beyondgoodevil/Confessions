### 1. Covenant Theology

- **Covenantal Continuity:** Just as circumcision was the sign of the covenant for Jewish infants, baptism is seen as the new sign of the covenant for all believers, including their children. In Colossians 2:11-12, Paul draws a parallel between circumcision and baptism, suggesting that both serve as markers of inclusion in God's covenant community.

### 2. Household Baptisms

- **Acts of the Apostles:** Several passages in the New Testament describe household baptisms, implying that children were included:
    - **Acts 16:15:** Lydia and her household were baptized.
    - **Acts 16:33:** The jailer and his entire household were baptized.
    - **1 Corinthians 1:16:** Paul mentions baptizing the household of Stephanas.

These instances suggest that if infants were present in these households, they would have been baptized as part of the family unit.

### 3. Jesus' Teaching on Children

- **Matthew 19:14:** Jesus explicitly welcomes children, saying, "Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven." This emphasizes the value and inclusion of children in the community of faith.

### 4. The Nature of Faith

- **Faith and the Community:** In the New Testament, faith is often viewed as communal. The faith of the parents can be seen as a basis for baptizing their infants, who are included in the faith community. This aligns with the understanding that baptism is not solely an individual act but a communal initiation into the body of Christ.

### 5. Symbolism of Baptism

- **Baptism as Cleansing:** In the New Testament, baptism symbolizes cleansing from sin (Acts 2:38). Infants, though not capable of personal sin, are seen as part of a fallen humanity. Baptism, therefore, signifies their inclusion in the grace of God, reflecting the Church's belief in original sin and the need for redemption.

### 6. Early Church Practice

- **Historical Evidence:** Early Christian writings, such as those from the Church Fathers, show that infant baptism was practiced and accepted in the early Church, indicating continuity with the apostolic tradition.
