Four writers, five centuries apart, all argue that government by **one** is best. They don't use the same argument, though. Aquinas argues from **unity**, Dante from the **end of mankind**, Maistre from **history and the nature of sovereignty**, and Kuehnelt-Leddihn from a point-by-point **comparison** with democracy.

## 1. Aquinas: Unity Makes Peace (*De Regno* I.1–7)

**Every multitude needs a ruler** (I.1). What is proper to each person divides people; what is common unites them. So besides what moves each person to his own good, there has to be something that moves the many to the **common good**. The same pattern shows up everywhere: the soul rules the body, reason rules the passions, the heart moves the members.

**Six forms** (I.2). A ruler's rule is just if it aims at the common good, unjust if at his own:

| | just | unjust |
|---|---|---|
| one | **king** | **tyrant** |
| few | aristocracy | oligarchy |
| many | polity | democracy ("the whole people will be as one tyrant") |

A king is "a shepherd, seeking the common good of the multitude and not his own" (Ezek 34:23, "I will set up one shepherd over them, even my servant David").

**Why one is best** (I.3):
- **From the end.** The welfare of a multitude is the unity of **peace**. A ruler doesn't deliberate whether to keep peace, any more than a doctor deliberates whether to heal. What is one brings about unity better than many, as what is hot is the best cause of heat.
- **Many must become "near one".** Several rulers can only rule if they agree; men pulling a ship must pull together. So one does better than several who approximate one.
- **From nature.** Every natural government is by one: the heart among the members, reason among the powers of the soul, one God over the universe. Art imitates nature.
- **From experience.** Cities without one ruler are "torn with dissensions": "Many pastors have destroyed my vineyard" (Jer 12:10).

**And so tyranny is the worst** (I.4). The same unity that makes a just government strong makes an unjust one more harmful. So among bad governments **democracy is the most tolerable** and tyranny the worst: "if the government should turn away from justice, it is more expedient that it be a government by many, so that it may be weaker".

**He admits the other side** (I.5). Rome after expelling its kings "waxed incredibly strong" (Sallust), because under consuls people treated the common good as their own instead of the king's. Israel suffered both ways: anarchy under the judges, then idolatry under the kings. "Danger thus lurks on either side."

**But polyarchy usually ends in tyranny anyway** (I.6). Many rulers quarrel, and one of them seizes power. "There has hardly ever been a polyarchy that did not end in tyranny": the Roman Republic ended in the emperors. A bad king harms individuals; quarrelling rulers destroy the peace itself.

**Safeguards** (I.7): choose a man unlikely to become a tyrant, "temper" his power so tyranny is hard, and plan in advance what to do if it happens anyway. On putting up with a tyrant, and why private tyrannicide is out, see I.7, [44]–[49].

## 2. Dante: One Monarch for the Whole World (*Monarchy* I)

Dante pushes Aquinas's argument from the city to **all mankind**. "Temporal monarchy, which men call 'empire'" is one authority over every other temporal authority.

**First principle** (I.iii–iv). Everything has its proper activity. The activity proper to humanity is to actualise the **potential intellect**: understanding, and then action as its extension. No single person or city can actualise all of it, so the whole human race is needed. It does this best in the "calm or tranquillity of peace". So **universal peace** is the first principle: "the message which rang out from on high to the shepherds" was peace (Luke 2:14).

**The arguments**, one per chapter:
- **v** Whatever is ordered to one end needs one director: reason in the person, the father in the household ("May you have an equal in your house" was a curse), one king in the kingdom, "Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation" (Matt 12:25). Mankind is ordered to one end, so it needs one ruler.
- **vi–vii** The order of parts to one head is better than the order of parts to each other, as an army's units are ordered to the commander. Mankind is in turn a part of the universe, which has one Ruler.
- **viii** Mankind is most like God "when it is most a unity": "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD" (Deut 6:4).
- **ix** Mankind is the "son of heaven", and heaven moves by one motion from one mover. So mankind should have one ruler and one law.
- **x** **The judge regress.** Two independent rulers can come into conflict; "an equal has no power over an equal", so someone above both must judge. Either that judge is the monarch, or you regress forever.
- **xi** **Justice** is strongest in the monarch. Justice is hindered by **greed** in the will and by lack of **power** to act. The monarch can't be greedy, "for his jurisdiction is bounded only by the ocean"; the kings of Castile and Aragon quarrel because each has a neighbour. Love sharpens justice, and men are closer to the monarch than to any lesser prince.
- **xii** **Freedom.** Free will is judgment not "pre-empted" by desire. Mankind exists "for its own sake" only under the monarch, because only then are perverted governments set right. "Citizens do not exist for the sake of consuls, nor the people for the sake of the king": the monarch is "the servant of all".
- **xiii** The best-disposed ruler disposes others best, since actions persuade more than words (Jacob's hands outweighed his voice).
- **xiv** What one can do, one should do. But this doesn't mean the monarch makes every local law. Nations need different laws: the Scythians in the cold north and the Garamantes at the equator. The monarch gives the common rule, as theoretical reason gives the major premise, and local rulers apply it, as Moses left lesser cases to the tribal leaders (Exod 18).
- **xv** Being, unity and goodness go together: unity is "the root of what it is to be good", plurality the root of evil. Concord is "a uniform movement of several wills", which needs one will to direct them.
- **xvi** History confirms it: the only time of universal peace was under Augustus, when Christ chose to be born, "the fulness of the time" (Gal 4:4). Since then the "seamless garment" of empire has been torn by greed.

Dante's earliest critic, Guido Vernani, objected that only **Christ** could have the qualities Dante gives his monarch (Shaw's note). Book III then argues that the emperor's authority comes **directly from God**, not through the Pope. The book was burnt in Bologna in 1329 and put on the Index in 1554.

## 3. Maistre: Sovereignty Is One, and Monarchy Is Natural (*Study on Sovereignty* II.1–4)

**All sovereignty is absolute** (II.1). "However we define and place sovereignty, always it is one, inviolable and absolute." In England King, Lords and Commons check each other, but once they agree "there is only one will that cannot be thwarted" (Blackstone).
- So the sovereign **can't be judged**: whoever judged him would be the real sovereign. "While I may be forced to agree that we have the right to massacre Nero, I would never admit that we have the right to judge him."
- The "division of powers" never divides sovereignty itself; the other powers are "counterweights or moderators". The real problem "is not to prevent the sovereign from willing invincibly... but to prevent it from willing unjustly".
- Against every sovereign there are only two courses: **obedience or insurrection**.

**Monarchy is the natural government** (II.2). "All men are born for monarchy": it is the oldest and most universal form, so natural that people speak of "kings" when they mean sovereignty.
- The threefold division of governments is "a Greek prejudice". Strictly, "men in general are ruled by kings", and republics are the exceptions.
- "History is experimental politics", and a hundred volumes of theory vanish before one experiment.

**How it works in practice:**
- Monarchy is "a **centralised aristocracy**". The aristocracy rules everywhere, under any form; in a monarchy it rules in the king's name and legally, so it offends the lower classes less. Every family can rise, and before the king all subjects are equal: "a kind of equality which dulls the inevitable pain of self-respect".
- The king's will doesn't really do everything: "religion, laws, customs, opinion, and class and corporate privileges constrain the sovereign". Kings are "much more often accused of lacking will than of abusing it".
- It's "precisely that which best does without the skill of the sovereign": a machine a mediocre man can work. But if the aristocracy loses its faith and pride, "the monarchy is dead, and its cadaver is left to the worms".
- **European** monarchy, as distinct from Asian, rests on six propositions "written only in the heart":
  1. the king is sovereign
  2. his person is inviolable
  3. he may not condemn anyone to death himself
  4. if he must imprison for reasons of state, he acts with great restraint and on the advice of a council
  5. he doesn't judge civil cases; magistrates do that in his name
  6. subjects may lawfully make their grievances known through bodies or assemblies
- The real complaint against monarchies is usually not too much power but **displaced** power, the "vizierate": "everyone is king except the king". The cure is to restore the king's "quality of a father" by direct communication with "the large family".
- "In all seditions... the people always begin by being right, and always end by being wrong."

**The other forms** (II.3–4):
- **Aristocracy** is "a monarchy whose throne is vacant": wise but less vigorous. Venice and Berne are his examples.
- **Pure democracy** is "an association of men without sovereignty". Its laws are "regulations, not laws". Justice in it is "sometimes weak and sometimes impassioned", and a foreigner can rarely get justice against a citizen.

## 4. Kuehnelt-Leddihn: Monarchy Compared Point by Point (*Liberty or Equality* ch. 4)

**His method is comparative.** Unlike Aquinas, he thinks government is needed only because of the **Fall**. So no form is perfect: the question is which **crutch** works better, since even "the perfect crutch" hurts the armpit. He compares not feudal monarchy but hereditary monarchy with an officialdom and local self-government, "the enlightened absolutism of Maria Theresa", with modern representative democracy. He lists about thirty points; the main ones:
- **Above parties.** "His Majesty's Most Loyal Opposition", whereas a president is always "a party man". Every election is "a solemn manifestation of division"; the monarchic principle unites.
- **Trained from the cradle**, morally and spiritually as well as intellectually. Democratic leaders arrive "unprepared", often rising suddenly from obscurity, which corrupts. He accepts Acton's "power corrupts" only "with many reservations".
- **International, not national.** Royal families intermarried, so wars between them were limited "cabinet wars". After Solferino, Francis Joseph said: "I have lost a battle and I pay with a province." The monarch "cannot logically be a nationalist or a racialist, since his ancestors are mainly aliens".
- **A ruler, not a leader.** He belongs to his country like its mountains and cathedrals, not to the nation as its "embodiment". The modern dictator, by contrast, is a man "of lowly origin" who embodies the masses.
- **More liberal by nature.** Monarchy is "rule from above", so it needs no "horizontal pressure" of conformity. "Even a Louis XIV would probably have been slain twenty-four hours after proclaiming prohibition."
- **Protector of minorities**, being "everybody's monarch", against majorities that sacrifice the unpopular (the Nisei in World War II).
- **Responsible to God.** "While God cannot be fooled, the masses can."
- **Thinks in generations**: he wants to hand his patrimony on unimpaired, and a dynasty can plan far ahead.
- **Patriarchal**, so it fits a Christian society of fathers: "The king was the father of his people, only because every father was king in his family" (Abel Bonnard).

*Mine*: some points are dated or weak. The claim that royal intermarriage bred better rulers rests on 1950s heredity talk. And his list of saintly monarchs proves less than he thinks.

## 5. Voices Against, From the Same Books

- **Aquinas himself** grants that republics can make citizens more devoted to the common good (Rome under the consuls), and that Israel's kings led it into idolatry.
- **The Russian Orthodox *Social Concept*** (III.7) reads 1 Sam 8 the other way round. Rule by the **judges** was the higher form: power worked "not through coercion, but authority", which needs very strong faith. Monarchy came when faith weakened, replacing "the King Invisible by the king visible": it is still God-given, but uses coercion more. The form of government depends on the society's spiritual state. Changing to "a more religiously rooted" form without a spiritual revival "will inevitably degenerate into falsehood and hypocrisy". The Church accepts the people's choice.
- **Maistre** too: every people has the government it deserves, and the real question is which people is best governed according to its own principles, not which form is best in the abstract.

## 6. Comparison

| | Aquinas | Dante | Maistre | Kuehnelt-Leddihn |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| main argument | unity → peace | the end of mankind → one world ruler | sovereignty is one; history shows monarchy is natural | a comparison of crutches |
| scale | city or province | the whole world | the nation | the nation |
| why government exists | natural, even without sin | to secure peace for the intellect's work | born with society, from God | the Fall |
| what restrains the king | choosing well, tempering his power | his lack of greed (nothing left to covet) | religion, customs, aristocracy, remonstrance | God, his oath, his heirs, Christian education |
| the enemy | tyranny | greed and division | rationalist constitution-making | egalitarian democracy and its dictators |

## 7. Notes

*Mine.*

- The **unity** arguments (Aquinas I.3, Dante I.x, Maistre II.1) really prove that there must be one **final** decision. They don't prove that it must be one **person**. Maistre admits as much: sovereignty is "one" in England too. The step to a single person needs the extra premises of nature (Aquinas) or history (Maistre).
- Dante's monarch is good because, having everything, he can covet nothing. That makes the argument depend on an idealised person, which is exactly Vernani's objection.
- The four agree that the king serves the common good (Aquinas's shepherd, Dante's "servant of all", Maistre's father, Kuehnelt-Leddihn's "first servant"). They differ on what keeps him there: Aquinas trusts institutions to temper him, Dante trusts his position, Maistre trusts custom and aristocracy, and Kuehnelt-Leddihn trusts religion and dynasty.
- Orthodox angle: compare Justinian's "**symphony**" of priesthood and kingdom (*Social Concept* III.4) with Dante's two guides. See [[Kingship of Christ]] for the biblical theme of God as Israel's true King.
