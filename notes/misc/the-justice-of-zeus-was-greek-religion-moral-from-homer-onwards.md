Were the Greek gods ever moral? Christian apologists thought not, and many Greeks agreed with them. Xenophanes complained that Homer and Hesiod gave the gods "stealing, adultery and mutual deception". Plato banned Homer from his city. In the twentieth century a powerful line of scholarship made the complaint historical. On this view religion and morality began apart. Homer's Zeus cares for his own honour, not for justice. The Greeks then moved slowly, and never completely, from a "shame-culture" to a "guilt-culture", until philosophy gave ethics the foundation the gods could not.

Hugh Lloyd-Jones gave the 1969 Sather Lectures at Berkeley against that story. They were published in 1971 as *The Justice of Zeus*. He argues that the justice of Zeus is already present in the *Iliad*, and that the moral and religious outlook of the Greeks stayed largely the same from Homer to the end of the fifth century. In his view what broke it was Plato, not the gods' moral weakness. Walter Burkert's *Greek Religion* (German 1977, English 1985), still the standard handbook, gives a more guarded answer. It agrees with Lloyd-Jones that the tie between gods and justice is very old. But it insists that Zeus is the *source* of justice without being *just*, and that a polytheism can never fully legitimate a moral order. This note reads Lloyd-Jones in full and gives Burkert's relevant chapters equal weight.

### 1. The Thesis Lloyd-Jones Attacks

The book opens with the scholars it means to answer (pp. 1–2):
- **E. R. Dodds** finds "no indication in the narrative of the Iliad that Zeus is concerned with justice as such."
- **Chantraine** allows that Zeus keeps a kind of order, but finds only one passage where he enforces a moral law.
- **A. W. H. Adkins** holds that "the gods as portrayed generally in the Homeric poems are far from just". Right triumphs in both epics, he says, but "it does not do so because it is right."

Behind Adkins lies a thesis about vocabulary. The Greek words of highest praise, *aretē* and *agathos*, first meant competitive excellence: valour, skill, success. The co-operative virtue of *dikaiosynē* never had the same prestige (p. 2). Behind Dodds lies the scheme of *The Greeks and the Irrational*: a passage "from 'shame-culture' to 'guilt-culture'" (p. 2). And behind both lies Bruno Snell's claim that Homer had no word for the psychic whole, and so no real concept of decision. If that were true, Lloyd-Jones notes, it "would make it impossible for the Iliad to be a poem dealing with great moral issues" (p. 9).

Lloyd-Jones's objection is first of all about method. "One of the most damaging sources of error about early Greek morality has been the assumption that in order to study the moral notions found in a work of art or in a society it is enough to list and analyse the words indicating moral concepts" (p. 2). The words must be supplemented "by a study of the actions performed in the book … and the attitudes shown towards them" (p. 3). The same objection returns in the *Conclusions*. There he accuses the lexical school of turning a method into "a mechanical system" that guarantees results "with a minimum of further intellectual exertion" (p. 157).

He is equally firm about the distance between Greek and biblical religion: "The Greek notion of the divine, it can never be said too often, differed utterly from the Jewish or Christian notion" (p. 3). Men are not Zeus's children. In the earliest account they were made from stones, and their patron is Prometheus. The gods treat them "as the nobles of an early stage of a rural society treat the peasants" (p. 4). They punish any slight to their honour (*timē*): Niobe's boast, Oeneus' forgotten sacrifice, Agamemnon's insult to Apollo's priest. "From a modern point of view, or indeed from that of Plato … such gods are monstrously unjust. But for Homer, and for later poets also, they are perfectly within their rights" (p. 4). So his claim is not that the Greek gods were moral in a Christian sense. It is that Zeus's justice was justice of a recognisable kind, and that it was there from the start.

### 2. Zeus in the *Iliad*: Justice as the King's Office

The argument for Zeus's justice rests on an analogy with kings (pp. 5–8):
- Zeus already holds the functions later tied to justice. He is **Horkios** (guardian of oaths), **Xeinios** (of strangers and the law of host and guest) and **Hikesios** (of suppliants). Dodds treats these as offences against Zeus's own *timē*, not concern "for justice as such" (p. 5).
- Homeric kings receive the *themistes*, the customs and principles of justice, from Zeus. They "protect the themistes that come from Zeus" (pp. 6–7). Since Zeus's attributes "were obviously based on theirs, it would be strange if he had not, as they had, the function of protecting justice" (p. 7).
- The justice involved is rough and reciprocal. Lloyd-Jones quotes a line from a lost epic that the emperor Claudius liked: "If he suffers as he did, straight justice will be done" (p. 7).

The one plain counter-example is the simile in *Iliad* 16. There Zeus sends a storm on men "who by violence pronounce in the market-place crooked judgments and drive out justice, having no care for the concern of the gods". It has usually been cut out as a Hesiodic interpolation. Lloyd-Jones refuses to cut it. He argues that the simile, and Phoenix's speech on the Prayers (*Litai*) in Book 9, "are in fact perfectly consistent with the theology of the Iliad as a whole" (p. 6).

The test case is the plot. Seen as Olympian politics, Troy falls because a powerful bloc of gods hates it. Seen from the human side, Menelaus is sure Zeus Xeinios will punish the abduction of his wife, and Agamemnon is sure Zeus Horkios will punish Pandarus' broken truce. Zeus's decision "is in accord with the basic principle of justice, that the aggressor must be punished. Is this accord merely a coincidence?" (p. 8).

The quarrel of Agamemnon and Achilles works the same way:
- Nestor's mediation in Book 1 aims at justice even without the abstract word: "he wishes to settle the dispute by persuading each participant to accord to the other his proper time" (p. 13).
- When Zeus grants Thetis' request he is "ensuring the triumph of Achilles in a dispute in which Achilles is undoubtedly in the right" (p. 13).
- When Achilles refuses Agamemnon's compensation in Book 9, the moral balance tips. Ajax's short speech, appealing to friendship (*philotēs*), shows that Achilles, "who until now has been in the right in his dispute with Agamemnon, has now put himself in the wrong" (p. 18).
- Against Adkins's claim that only military failure is at stake, Lloyd-Jones points to loyalty. Even in a heroic society "the so-called quieter virtues are essential, above all loyalty, without which not even a gang of thieves, let alone an army, can hope to operate successfully" (p. 15).
- The plan of Zeus then recoils on both men. Achilles' prayer is granted, and it costs him Patroclus. "Neither Agamemnon nor Achilles can complain that Zeus has been unjust; according to the terms of Zeus' justice, each has got what he deserved" (p. 21).
- Achilles finally dies "not for glory, but out of remorse for his responsibility for the death of Patroclus" (p. 22).

Lloyd-Jones's summary is careful about what kind of justice this is. Zeus's "concern to punish offenders against justice originates from the concern to punish offenders against his particular time that is felt by any god. He defends the established order (dike) by punishing mortals whose injustices disturb it, and at the same time by sternly repressing any attempt of men to rise above the humble place where they belong" (p. 27). Even the two jars of good and evil in *Iliad* 24 are "a mark not of his injustice but rather of his justice. What is just for mortals is not necessarily what mortals want" (p. 27). One problem stays open, and Lloyd-Jones names it himself. Zeus drives men to their crimes by sending *atē*, delusion, and then punishes them: "how can it be just that Zeus should punish them? Already in Homeric times that question caused much perplexity" (p. 27).

### 3. *Atē* and Double Motivation: Responsibility Without "Will"

Against Snell, Lloyd-Jones (following Albin Lesky) takes Odysseus' monologue in *Iliad* 11 as the pattern of a right decision. Odysseus weighs flight. Then he remembers what a good fighter must do, and stands his ground (p. 9). Snell complains that Homer lacks the concept of will, but that is "a concept of considerable sophistication" (p. 9), and its absence does not prevent Homer from describing a decision.

A wrong decision is the same process spoiled. When Agamemnon apologises in Book 19, he blames Zeus, his portion (*moira*) and the Erinys for the *atē* that took his wits. Achilles in turn cries "great are the atai which you send to men" (pp. 22–23). Lloyd-Jones reconstructs the mechanism. *Atē* sent by Zeus "takes away the phrenes of the person concerned; as a result his thymos is rendered uncontrollable … and the knowledge of how to make a right decision which he possesses is rendered ineffective" (p. 23). But neither hero denies responsibility. Agamemnon pays the compensation, and Ajax says both that the gods put the savage spirit in Achilles' breast and that Achilles put it there himself (p. 17).

From this follows the doctrine of **double motivation**: "the part played by the god can always be subtracted without making nonsense of the action" (p. 10). Aphrodite forcing Helen to Paris' bed in Book 3 is "a kind of allegorical picture" of what usually happens invisibly. "If the part of the gods in these disastrous decisions was removed, the working of human passions that determine them, eros, eris, cholos, would still be manifest" (p. 24).

Lloyd-Jones steers between two errors. One is the old classicism, which treated the gods as a mere *façon de parler*; that position, he says, was "not … as unintelligent as it has become the fashion to suppose" (p. 24). The other is the anthropological school, which takes Homer's world for primitive and strips his characters "of all power of decision and mental independence" (p. 10). His formula is: "Homer's gods are effective and his religion real, but … his human characters are free to decide and are responsible for their decisions" (p. 10).

### 4. Shame-Culture and Guilt-Culture

Lloyd-Jones accepts Dodds's anthropological terms but not his history. He takes the cleanest definition from J. K. Campbell, the ethnographer of the Sarakatsani shepherds. Shame "is concerned with a man's failure to approach some ideal pattern of conduct", while guilt refers to "the transgression of interdicted limits" (p. 25). Lloyd-Jones widens guilt to cover breaches of social as well as divine commandments. He then makes two points:
- **The labels are relative.** Modern Greece and Andalusia combine an honour-culture with Christianity. "I know no example of a culture of the one type which does not contain elements of the other also" (p. 25).
- **Every shame-culture has some guilt in it.** Any organised society must value loyalty, and "it may be hard to name an equivalent of 'duty' or 'loyalty' in Homeric Greek, but if the word is lacking the thing is not" (p. 26).

So he wishes Dodds had not called a chapter "From Shame-Culture to Guilt-Culture", "thereby obscuring the fact that in general Greek culture continued to be a shame-culture until well after the fifth century" (p. 26). The tragedy of the *Iliad* lies in "the tension between the demands of individual honour and those of loyalty to the group" (pp. 26–27), not in the absence of the second.

### 5. The *Odyssey*, Hesiod and the Lyric Poets: Continuity, Not Progress

The *Odyssey* opens with Zeus complaining that mortals blame the gods, when "through their wicked recklessness" they suffer beyond their fate. He cites Aegisthus, who was warned by Hermes (p. 28). Dodds called the speech "programmatic" (p. 29). In the *Odyssey* the gods put good ideas into men's minds but never evil ones, and fortune is sent according to desert. Strangers, suppliants and even beggars have gods and Erinyes of their own (pp. 29–30).

Lloyd-Jones accepts the difference but refuses to make it progress. The *Iliad* sees every situation from several sides: "not even Henry James is more sensible of the complexity of moral situations than the author of this poem". In the *Odyssey*, good and bad are divided "almost as clearly as in a Western film" (p. 31). "The difference in theology and morality between the two poems reflects a difference in style and purpose, and is of no value in fixing their temporal relation" (p. 31). And since the *Iliad* already holds men responsible for god-sent decisions, the Odyssean change is "of strictly limited significance" (p. 32).

**Hesiod.** Hesiod is likewise no inventor. A peasant cheated by bribed lords (*basileis*), he appeals to Dike, who sits beside Zeus and tells him of the wicked purposes of men. Zeus has given justice to men so they need not live like beasts (p. 35). But "the claim that Hesiod introduced the ideal of justice … must be rejected": he "could [not] have appealed for justice as he does unless her authority had already been recognized" (p. 35). Zeus's justice cuts two ways: "Zeus benefits men by forcing them to be just to one another, but at the same time his justice keeps them in their proper station" (p. 35). Lloyd-Jones adds that the Greeks did not rail against "whatever brute and blackguard made the world", in Housman's phrase. They "had not been told in childhood that they had immortal souls" and "would not have thought it reasonable to expect Zeus to have their interests at heart in preference to his own" (p. 33).

**The lyric poets.** Snell, Pfeiffer and Dodds found in the lyric age a new sense of helplessness (*amēchaniē*). Dodds called it "a different emotional reaction to the old belief" and a "feeling of divine hostility" (p. 36). Lloyd-Jones answers with two points of genre and many particular cases:
- Epic heroes talk with the gods directly, so divine action seems less mysterious than it does to an ordinary man.
- A short personal poem naturally voices sadness, "especially in a culture whose religion offers no easy consolation" (p. 37).

He then works through the poets:
- **Archilochus.** His "revolt" against the Homeric code dissolves on inspection. Throwing away his shield is not un-Homeric: the critics confuse Homer's code with the Spartan "with your shield or on it", and Odysseus would not have died for his shield either (p. 39). His endurance echoes Apollo's words in *Iliad* 24 (p. 38).
- **Alcaeus.** He holds an Iliadic view of god-sent delusion, which is "irritating to those who insist on linear development" (p. 42).
- **Solon.** His theology is the *Odyssey*'s. The guilty are always punished, in person or in their descendants, and Athens' danger comes from her citizens' greed (p. 44).
- **Theognis.** He sometimes reproaches Zeus for letting the wicked prosper and for punishing children for their fathers' sins (p. 46). But his couplet that all *aretē* lies in justice is not "amazing", as Adkins thought (p. 47).
- **Pindar.** His *dikē* is coloured by aristocratic politics, but "that does not mean that it is cynical, or that it lacks a moral element" (p. 50).

Delphi gives the archaic outlook in two sayings. "Know thyself" means "remember that you are a human being". "Nothing in excess" counsels the "safe thinking" (*sōphrosynē*) that protects from *hybris* (pp. 52–53). And "there is no reason why a belief in divine justice should not accompany a pessimistic view of human life … the combination of the two was in fact characteristic of the thinking of the archaic age" (p. 53).

### 6. Divine Envy and Pollution: Was the Archaic Age More Anxious?

Dodds's strongest evidence for an archaic change was the rise of two ideas. One was divine envy (*phthonos*), which Herodotus above all made "an oppressive menace". The other was the fear of pollution (*miasma*). "There is no trace in Homer," Dodds wrote, "of the belief that pollution was either infectious or hereditary" (pp. 53–54, 70).

**Divine envy.** Lloyd-Jones distinguishes two meanings of *phthonos* (p. 56):
- If it means "the unprovoked punishment of mortals by gods who are jealous of their wealth or happiness", it is not in the *Iliad*.
- If it means "the hostility of the gods to men who try to rise above the human sphere, it is."

Its root is "the ancient and undeniably primitive fear" behind the evil eye and touching wood. But in Pindar and Herodotus it "has already developed into a concept of a comparatively advanced theology" (p. 69). When Agamemnon walks on the purple, "the phthonos of the gods provoked by such an act is not unjust but just" (p. 69). Herodotus' rulers fall because wealth tempts them to pride, not because they are rich. Croesus loses his son for "having thought himself — not for having been — the most fortunate of men" (p. 63). The god "does not allow anyone but himself to think great thoughts" (p. 68). Herodotus' fateful decisions follow the *Iliad*'s pattern. Xerxes, like Croesus, deliberates carefully and is still sent a deceiving dream, like Agamemnon's in *Iliad* 2. "But neither in the Iliad nor in Herodotus do they destroy him without just cause" (p. 63).

**Pollution.** Lloyd-Jones rejects Dodds's psychological explanation. Dodds had suggested that loosening family ties set sons against fathers, and that men then projected onto Zeus "the jealous god … the awful judge" (p. 71). Lloyd-Jones's objection is historical: "how often cultural changes of such magnitude occur within so brief a space of time … Long before the bonds of family came to be relaxed, sons must have quarrelled with their fathers" (p. 71).

His alternative goes back to K. O. Müller in 1833: the epic poets chose not to show these beliefs (p. 72). He gives three pieces of evidence:
- The Apolline professions of seer, singer and healer were once one, as later in Epimenides and Empedocles, and implied ritual purification. Here he cites Burkert's own paper on the Greek *goētes* (shamans) (p. 72).
- The plague in *Iliad* 1 rests on the same belief as the plague in *Oedipus Tyrannus*: "an offence by one member of a community brings down a mysterious punishment upon all its members". "No word for 'pollution' occurs in the first book of the Iliad; the thing itself is there" (p. 74).
- Hector says Paris deserves stoning, a punishment by which the community works off a collective guilt. The Erinyes, in origin the ghosts or curses of the wronged, are already guardians of the whole order of things in Homer: they silence the horse Xanthus when he speaks (pp. 75–76).

The conclusion is deliberately deflationary. "The dark, the daemonic, the numinous side of religion is on any view surprisingly absent from the Homeric poems" (p. 76). That absence is a matter of poetic selection. If the late archaic age looks more haunted, "that is in the main due to accident. The beliefs and attitudes held in Greece in the epic and archaic ages are in the main very much the same", and belief in pollution in the fifth century "had become distinctly weaker" (p. 78). Dodds read the draft and replied generously: "I stressed the element of change in Greek beliefs … you stress the element of continuity; we are both of us right, though both of us at times exaggerate the partial truth we are stressing" (p. xi).

### 7. The Presocratics and Aeschylus: Dikē as the Order of the World

**The Presocratics.** Anaximander made the impersonal *apeiron* "the divine". Yet he described the world in the language of Dike: things "pay each other penalty and retribution for their injustice according to the assessment of time" (pp. 79–80). For Lloyd-Jones this extends the intricate chains of crime and punishment that Herodotus traces in human affairs "to the fates of all things within the universe" (p. 80). The doctors carry the idea furthest. The Hippocratic author of *On the Sacred Disease* denies that any disease is more divine than another, which affirms "that the same Dike governs the whole universe" (p. 80). The roots are Homeric: "Homer's is a unitary universe, governed by the same Dike under Zeus" (p. 81).

Xenophanes' god, who "governs all things by the thought of his mind", is foreshadowed by the *Iliad*'s Zeus, who controls events "without moving from his throne" (pp. 81–82). Heraclitus attacks blood-purification, the mysteries and images. But his "one wise" is "unwilling and … willing to be called by the name of Zeus". For him "to the gods everything is fair and just", and "human laws are nurtured by the divine law" (pp. 83–84).

**Aeschylus.** Lloyd-Jones denies that Aeschylus invented a new monotheism under philosophical influence (pp. 84–86):
- "Whoever Zeus is" is an age-old formula of prayer.
- The *Heliades* fragment ("Zeus is the aether, Zeus is the earth … Zeus is all things and whatever is higher than these") asserts Zeus's supremacy, not pantheism.
- His real sources are Homer, Hesiod and Solon.

The theology is consistent across the plays:
- Zeus gives men "a grace that comes by violence", the certainty that injustice will be punished in the offender or his descendants (p. 87).
- "Aeschylus strongly maintains that Zeus does not punish the innocent" (p. 87).
- When Agamemnon must sacrifice his daughter, "the sacrifice is none the less a dreadful crime. Why has Zeus allowed Agamemnon … to be faced with such a choice? Because Agamemnon is the son of the guilty Atreus" (p. 91).
- At the end of the *Eumenides* the Erinyes keep their office. Athena echoes their warning — "who among mortals that fears nothing is just?" — and what the Erinyes are in the universe, the Areopagus is in Athens (p. 93).
- "The cliché … that the Eumenides depicts the transition from the vendetta to the rule of law, is utterly misleading" (p. 94). "Aeschylus' politics are an extension of his theology" (p. 94).

**The Prometheus problem.** The hardest case is the *Prometheus Bound*, whose Zeus is "a savage and remorseless tyrant" (p. 95). Nineteenth-century scholars rescued it by giving Zeus a moral development across the lost plays. Lloyd-Jones calls this, "in its extreme form … a product of the modern liberal and progressive outlook" (pp. 95–96). Zeus does not develop; he negotiates. Prometheus trades the secret of Thetis for his release, and Chiron gives up his immortality (pp. 96–97).

Lloyd-Jones then offers what he calls "nothing but a speculation" (p. 102). Perhaps Zeus's side of the deal was to send Dike, "hitherto his own private property, to men", as in the myth Plato gives Protagoras. Perhaps the trilogy ended with the *Women of Aetna* in Sicily (pp. 98–102). Even then, Dike's gift is two-edged: "Dike may secure man against anarchy and war; she also holds him down in subjection to the immortal gods". "Whatever the generous sympathies of liberals like Shelley may lead them to imagine, Prometheus is only a minor figure in the Greek pantheon. Not Prometheus but Zeus is the ruler of the universe" (pp. 102–3).

### 8. Sophocles and Euripides: Are the Gods Just?

**Sophocles.** In a 1966 article Dodds dismissed three "heresies" about *Oedipus Tyrannus* and offered his own view. He held "that Sophocles did not believe, or did not always believe, that the gods are in any human sense 'just'" (p. 108). Rohde and Wilamowitz had said much the same (p. 108). Lloyd-Jones agrees with Dodds against the three heresies:
- the moralising reading that Oedipus deserves his fate;
- the reading of the play as a tragedy of fate, since "the philosophical notion of determinism did not exist before the Hellenistic age" (p. 106);
- the reading that the play means nothing at all.

But he finds Dodds's own view a paradox: "from the time of Hesiod to the collapse of paganism, it is generally true that anyone who believed in the existence of the gods believed that they were just and righteous. If the pious Sophocles rejected this belief, that is a fact so extraordinary that it needs detailed explanation" (p. 109).

His explanation is the **inherited curse**. Sophocles stopped writing connected trilogies, so the chain of crime and punishment drops out of view. But it is still there:
- in the *Electra* it goes back to Pelops' murder of Myrtilus (p. 112);
- in the *Antigone* the Chorus tell her "you are making atonement, I think, with an ordeal come to you by inheritance", and she accepts it (p. 115);
- in *Oedipus Tyrannus* it rests on Laius' rape of Chrysippus, Pelops' son, in breach of hospitality. That crime "would amount to neither more nor less than that of Paris" (p. 121).

This is why Oedipus calls himself "sprung from those who should not have begotten me" and "the child of parents unholy" (p. 122). Here Lloyd-Jones follows Perrotta (1935), whom he says no one had noticed (p. 123).

Lloyd-Jones does not make the play a morality tale. "His story is not primarily a story of guilt and punishment. It is told to illustrate the fragility of mankind" (p. 123). The Chorus after the discovery do not sing that the gods have punished a guilty family. They sing: "Ah, the generations of men, how I esteem you in your life equal to nothing" (p. 123).

Dodds's only other evidence is the end of the *Women of Trachis*, with Hyllus' "violent protest against divine injustice" (p. 127). Lloyd-Jones answers it with the audience's knowledge. Everyone knew that the pyre on Oeta made Heracles a god, so the poet lets the audience "see, for once, into the purposes of Zeus" (p. 128). His conclusion on Sophocles is that "the justice of the gods is not, in his view, easy for mortals to perceive". It is hard to see because of "the immense extent of time which may separate cause from punishment, and the complex interweaving within human history of different causal chains" (p. 128).

**Euripides.** Wilamowitz, and Gilbert Murray after him, made Euripides a "committed" poet of enlightenment. Reinhardt noticed that Wilamowitz's Phaedra looks like an Ibsen heroine, and Lloyd-Jones adds: "Murray's Shavian Euripides derives from the Ibsenian Euripides of Wilamowitz" (pp. 145–46). Lloyd-Jones's correction rests on one principle: "the Greek gods were never thought to intervene externally in moral action; they acted not from outside but from inside mortal minds" (p. 148). Several things follow:
- Medea and Phaedra decide wrongly through passion, as Achilles did. "Aphrodite has power only so far as she works through human passions; but does that make her power less real?" (pp. 149–50).
- When Helen blames Aphrodite and Hecuba blames Helen's lust, "by Homeric canons, both are right" (p. 150).
- In the *Medea* "justice, in the ancient sense, is done; as often, justice is terrible" (p. 152).
- The Greeks' vengeance on Troy goes too far and will be punished, as the epic tradition says.

The general verdict: "The justice of Zeus is from the beginning harsh; Dike means not simply justice, but the order of the universe; yet the justice of Zeus is at all times recognisable as a kind of justice" (p. 153).

### 9. Sophists, Thucydides and Plato's "Failure of Nerve"

The sixth chapter makes three moves.

**The sophists were not the atheists of legend.** Protagoras' agnosticism is "not a statement of unbelief but a confession of ignorance". In the myth Plato gives him, Zeus sends Aidos and Dike to men, and justice is a human norm that is "not … arbitrary" (pp. 130–31). Democritus insists that the just man feels shame (*aidōs*) before himself whether observed or not (p. 132). Plato never engaged this empirical theory of justice, which resembles modern empiricism (pp. 132, 135). In the Athens of the time, unbelief was socially frowned on but hardly persecuted. "It is when a religion is sure of itself that such amusement is permitted" (p. 133). Without a clergy or a dogma, "until the rise of Christianity, atheism in the ancient world was to present no social problem" (p. 134).

**Thucydides keeps the old pattern without the gods.** Melos is followed directly by the Sicilian expedition, and Diodotus' speech on hope and desire echoes Solon. Homer or Aeschylus "would have said that Zeus had sent Ate to take away its wits", and would have found the reason in the massacre of the Melians (p. 143). "This is not the tragedy of Pericles … but the tragedy of Athens", betrayed by *hybris* into injustice. "If he did reject them, it was the Greek gods, not any other gods whom he rejected. The world as he presents it … is the world of the traditional Greek religion" (p. 144).

**Plato is the real break.** Plato blamed the sophists for the crimes of Athens, but the decisions were made by conservative and pious assemblies (p. 135). Plato replaced the old religious sanction with a metaphysics of Forms, while clinging to punishment in the next world. His was a "metaphysical and theological dogmatism having more in common with the religions of the east" and with modern dogmatic systems. "The first important failure of nerve was that of Plato" (pp. 135–36). The phrase "failure of nerve" is J. B. Bury's.

Lloyd-Jones also asks whether Homeric ethics caused Athenian imperialism. He answers with an uncomfortable comparison: centuries of Christian moral teaching have not produced "notably higher" public or private morality than fifth-century Athens, and "I doubt if any serious student of history would reply with an unhesitating Yes" (p. 136).

**The conclusion.** Lloyd-Jones names three marks of early Greek religion (p. 160):
1. It is "neither monotheism nor, in the strict sense, polytheism, but something between the two."
2. It is "not anthropocentric; men are the creation only of a minor god."
3. Its gods "are not transcendent but immanent; they do not interfere from outside with the course of nature, but govern the inanimate world through natural processes and the animate through human passions."

From these he draws three consequences (pp. 160–62):
- Because Artemis and Aphrodite both claim honour, it is often hard to say whether a desire is wrong, "a proposition … to which dogmatic monotheism does not always lend a hearing."
- Because the gods "govern the universe not in men's interest but in their own", the problem of evil hardly arises.
- Because Dike is "the order of the universe", the Greek idea of a cosmos governed by law was "a prerequisite of rational speculation".

He ends with Eliot. "Human kind … cannot bear very much reality. The early Greeks were capable of their unique achievements largely because they could bear, as their religion shows, very much more reality than most human beings" (p. 164).

### 10. Burkert: Homer's Authority and a Zeus Above Justice

Burkert begins where Lloyd-Jones assumes:
- **No authority but poetry.** Greek religion had "no priestly caste with a fixed tradition, no Veda and no Pyramid texts; nor is there any authoritative revelation in the form of a sacred book" (p. 119). A polytheism made of cults, names, myths and images is "potentially chaotic", and "only an authority could create order amid such a confusion of traditions. The authority to whom the Greeks appealed was the poetry of Hesiod and, above all, of Homer" (p. 120).
- **The gods act in the mind.** The "double stage" of divine and human action is older than Homer, and so is the refinement "whereby divine intervention prefers to manifest itself in the psychic realm: a god sends or throws courage and despair, shrewdness and delusion into the person" (p. 122). This is Lloyd-Jones's double motivation, stated as a fact about the epic tradition.
- **The Iliad's gods are hard to take seriously.** Burkert stresses the poem's comedy more than Lloyd-Jones does: the inextinguishable laughter of the gods, the seduction of Zeus and the farcical battle of the gods. Because of these, "the Iliad has been described as the most irreligious of all poems" (p. 122). He does not choose between that verdict and its opposite. "The Greeks, at all events, had to come to terms with the gods as they were portrayed in this poem."
- **The Odyssey is different.** In it "the action which leads to what is good and just … becomes … part of a divine plan", and in place of the Iliad's ironic mirroring "a moralizing piety comes to the fore" (p. 122).
- **Prose brought theology.** The end of poetry's monopoly made the problem of *theologia* appear, and "that the conflict which arose in this way found no generally accepted solution was due precisely to the ineradicable force of Homer's influence" (p. 125).

Burkert's portrait of **Zeus** supplies the strongest qualification of Lloyd-Jones:
- **Power.** "Power is latent violence which must have been manifested at least in some mythical once-upon-a-time. Superiority is guaranteed only by defeated inferiors" (p. 128). Zeus's promiscuity "meets with no moralizing criticism" in the archaic world (p. 129).
- **Wisdom.** Yet "no one can compel Zeus or require an account from him, and yet his decisions are neither blind nor biased. That Zeus swallowed Metis signifies the union of power and wisdom" (p. 129).
- **Moira.** Fate is "not a person … but a fact", a portion. Zeus could overstep it but does not, "just as a good and wise ruler does not use his real power to encroach on the limits set by custom" (pp. 129–30).
- **Justice.** Then the decisive sentence: "Justice is of Zeus, Dios dika; but this is not to say that Zeus is just, dikaios: only someone who respects the ordinances in a dispute with an equal can be called just; Zeus stands above all disputes. He gives now good, now evil; often no one knows why; but the very fact that a planning father holds power in his hands makes justice among men possible" (p. 130).
- **Troy.** On Troy, Burkert agrees with Lloyd-Jones. "Athena and Hera hate Troy because Paris spurned them; Zeus resolved to destroy the city because Paris violated the laws of hospitality" (p. 130).

On **anthropomorphism**, Burkert marks the distance that Lloyd-Jones's "immanence" might hide (p. 188):
- "The gods exist, but they cannot be harnessed to the ends of man; they seem familiar in their humanity, they can even be laughed at, but still they remain distant."
- "The line which separates gods and men is death", and "in the last, decisive extremity the gods abandon man". Apollo leaves Hector; Artemis leaves the dying Hippolytus.
- The creation of man, the goal of creation in Genesis and in Babylon, is "almost completely suppressed in Greek mythology".
- "There is no devil in the ancient religions, but each god has his dark and dangerous side."

### 11. Burkert: Gods Between Amorality and Law, and the Oath

Burkert's section "Gods between Amorality and Law" (pp. 246–50) takes the question head-on. It opens with the Christian charge of immorality, one of the most successful arguments against the pagan gods. Its defenders could not deny the charge, and the Greeks themselves had made it for centuries, from Solon's "much the poets lie" and Xenophanes to Euripides: "If the gods do anything shameful, they are not gods" (p. 246). Yet "Greek religion was practised for 800 years after Xenophanes … Quite clearly that criticism had touched only the surface, not the roots" (p. 246).

Burkert grants the link between religion and morality. "The fear of god is the beginning of morality." Polyphemus has no care for the gods and is a man-eater, and Odysseus asks of every new shore whether its people are "wanton, wild and not just" or "hospitable and of a god-fearing mind" (p. 247). He rejects the easy developmental solution, in which the Olympians reflect a dark-age aristocracy that ethics outgrew: "Zeus, Apollo, and Athena are unquestionably more than members of a Junker aristocracy who tend to kick over the traces". Zeus has always embodied "a centre of meaning which is accepted without question". One may speak, he says, of "a quasi-amoral justice of Zeus — a justice which is not bound to established statutes, is neither predictable nor accountable, and yet is ultimately always in the right, even when it brings destruction" (pp. 247–48). That is close to Lloyd-Jones's position. But Burkert adds at once: "But this, too, is very far from disposing of the contradictions" (p. 248).

Three contradictions remain:

1. **Ritual contains its own negations.** Hermes steals, and there are festivals at which stealing is allowed. There is ritual obscenity alongside pious silence, and above all the killing at the heart of sacrifice. "With human sacrifice, religion and morality part company." More generally, "ethical postulates can never succeed in mastering the whole reality of nature and society; they achieve no more than partial clearings amid the impenetrable, chaotic mass; moreover, morality is always in danger of cutting off the roots of its own life" (p. 248).

2. **Polytheism divides the world into domains.** "Polytheism encounters fundamental difficulties in giving legitimation to a moral world order. Its multiplicity always implies opposition." Order is possible only as apportionment (*moira*). Each god "intervenes if, and only if, this domain is specifically violated. This is true at first even of Zeus." Asylum holds at the altar; "elsewhere one may murder" (p. 248). But Burkert sees the gain as well as the cost. Polytheism can "embrace a richly various reality without evading contradictions", and it leaves man "a sphere of freedom". So "law and ethics could develop among the Greeks as human wisdom, free and yet in harmony with the god … Greek gods do not give laws" (p. 248).

3. **The more justice is claimed, the more it fails in experience.** "The attempt to tie the gods to morality in a narrower sense is very old." It goes back to the *Iliad* 16 simile, Hesiod's thirty thousand watchers and the *Odyssey*, where "the theodicy motif is already sounded" (p. 249). Laertes cries: "Father Zeus, there are gods indeed on high Olympus if truly the suitors have paid the penalty." But "if in this way the gap between gods and morality seems to close, then the gulf between ideal and reality opens up all the more threateningly" (p. 249). Theognis' "Dear Zeus, I am surprised at you" follows: "Morality and piety seem to founder together" (p. 249). Practised religion makes it worse. In the *Iliad* sacrifice can win back the gods' favour, and Apolline purification amounts "to resocialization, but not atonement". The relation of cult and morality "is … one which no religion has managed to solve completely" (p. 250).

The **oath** is where Burkert sees religion, morality and society fused (pp. 250–54). "In the institution of the oath, religion, morality, and the very organization of society appear indissolubly linked together." He quotes Lycurgus: "What holds democracy together is the oath" (p. 250). In the *Iliad* 3 oath, Zeus, the sun, the rivers, earth and the powers below are called to witness, and the swearers pray that a perjurer's brains may "flow to the ground like this wine" (p. 251). "To the Greeks … it was indispensable that there be a divine person who watches over all this and who is able to intervene to punish." The ordinary man believed Zeus struck perjurers with his thunderbolt, "even if this is not confirmed by experience" (p. 252). On balance oaths worked, and religion was "the foundation of the civil organization of state, law, and commercial life. And yet the oath is not truly a moral force" (p. 254).

### 12. Burkert: Piety Without Creed, and Philosophy's Verdict

**Greek piety** was a matter of boundaries, not of feeling. "Hieros is as it were the shadow cast by divinity", and "it would also be totally impossible to call a god himself hieros" (p. 269). The word *hosios* presupposes "that the sacred does not constitute the entire world and does not lay infinite claims on men"; *hosion* and *dikaion* are "the duties towards gods and towards men, or the same duties in their divine and their civil aspect" (p. 270). Piety (*eusebeia*) is reverence measured by custom: "To change nothing of what our forefathers have left behind, this is eusebeia" (p. 273). It excludes intimacy and humility alike:
- Aristotle: "It would be absurd if someone were to say that he loves Zeus." To love mankind in general would be beneath Zeus's dignity, "left for Prometheus or Hermes, at best" (p. 274).
- "Greeks do not pray 'my god!', as Hittites or Hebrews do. The despairing question: 'My god, my god, why hast thou forsaken me,' is countered by the defiant assertion: 'Father Zeus, no god is more destructive than you.' It is left for men to endure as long as they are able" (p. 274).

Burkert warns against judging this by Christian standards. "A creed or confession of faith is as foreign to Greeks as the Spanish inquisition." It would be mistaken to call a piety "without faith, love, and hope" not genuine, since it rests "not … on the word but on ritual tradition" (p. 275). The revolution came with Plato: "faith supported by philosophy, love transcending the world, and hope for an afterlife … humility, service of the gods … assimilation to god." Before him, "religion was not seen in the symbols of the path and the gate, but in those of unchanging order" (p. 275).

**Philosophical religion** (ch. VII) is for Burkert "a strange battle". "The decisive turn seems to have been taken from the very beginning, but it remains without effect in practice" (p. 305).

The first philosophers transformed the gods without disowning them:
- Anaximander keeps the Homeric predicates of the gods, deathless and ageless, but gives them to a neuter, "the divine". His saying about penalty and retribution makes it possible to accept everything that happens, "including his own destruction", as lawfully ordained. That is "piety of a form which is not too far removed from the Homeric image of the gods, sovereign and aloof" (pp. 307–8).
- With Xenophanes, "for the first time, speaking about the divine is dominated by postulates of what is fitting", and his criticism "could not be outdone, and it was never refuted" (pp. 308–9).
- Heraclitus rebuilds the bridge to tradition: "Human laws are nourished by one, the divine" (p. 309).
- "At the start, the new approach was anything but impious." The Homeric gods were "representatives of reality, not pretexts for magical wish fulfilment" (p. 310).

The sophists then set *nomos* against *physis*, and "from enlightenment grows insecurity" (p. 312):
- Protagoras could not say whether the gods are (p. 313).
- Prodicus and Democritus explained belief in gods by usefulness, fear and order (p. 314).
- The *Sisyphus* fragment called the gods a clever lawgiver's invention (p. 315).

"The discovery of atheism can be seen to be one of the most important events in the history of religion" (p. 315). Lloyd-Jones plays down the same evidence.

Then a new synthesis follows:
- Laws "begotten in the heavenly aither", in the ode of *Oedipus Tyrannus*, try "to close the rift between physis and nomos" (p. 318).
- Diogenes and Xenophon's Socrates argue for providence (*pronoia*) from design, "an empirical argument … which was to prevail until the time of Darwin" (p. 319).
- Plato's *Laws* make religion "the very foundation of the state". Atheism becomes a capital crime watched by a Nocturnal Council, so that "heresy trials and inquisitions seem to be anticipated" (p. 333).
- Yet Plato leaves the old cults untouched. The result, for the next six hundred years, is that "the conflict between philosophical and traditional religion is denied" (p. 337).

### 13. Assessment

The two scholars agree more than their tones suggest:
- Both hold that Homeric gods act through human passions, so that divine and human motivation coexist and human responsibility remains.
- Both read the *Iliad* 16 simile and the *Odyssey*'s opening as genuine evidence that gods and justice were linked very early. Neither treats them as late moralising.
- Both locate Zeus's justice in the fall of Troy, as punishment of Paris' breach of hospitality.
- Both see Plato as the decisive revolution.

They divide over four questions:

| Question | Lloyd-Jones | Burkert |
|---|---|---|
| Is Zeus just? | Yes, in an archaic sense of *dikē*: reciprocal, harsh, often inscrutable, but "at all times recognisable as a kind of justice" (p. 153). | Justice is "of Zeus", but Zeus is not *dikaios*. He "stands above all disputes" and "gives now good, now evil" (p. 130). |
| Can polytheism found a moral order? | Yes. Its plurality is even a merit, since it admits that desires conflict (p. 160). | Only partly. It works by apportionment, each god guarding his own domain, so ethics develops as "human wisdom" (p. 248). |
| Did Greek religion change between Homer and the fifth century? | Little. Apparent change is mostly genre and accident (p. 78). | The practice of cult hardly changed. The gulf between ideal and reality, and the discovery of atheism, were real events (pp. 249, 315). |
| What is lost with Plato? | A religion that could "bear … very much more reality" (p. 164). | An unreflective piety of "unchanging order", replaced by faith, hope and assimilation to god (p. 275). |

**Strengths of Lloyd-Jones.**
- His method is right. You cannot read a culture's morality off its vocabulary, and his close readings of the embassy in *Iliad* 9, Nestor's speeches and Agamemnon's apology show that Homer's characters argue about right and wrong in terms an audience could follow.
- His critique of Dodds's Freudian account of the archaic age, and of the "vendetta to law" reading of the *Eumenides*, has largely held.
- His reading of *Oedipus Tyrannus* through the curse on Laius restores a background that a non-trilogic theatre would leave implicit, without turning the play into a sermon.

**Weaknesses of Lloyd-Jones.**
- He sometimes defends continuity by explaining away the evidence for change. Pollution is absent from Homer because the poets suppressed it. *Phthonos* in Herodotus is really justice. Prometheus' tyrannical Zeus will be corrected by a lost play. Each move is plausible alone, but together they make the thesis hard to falsify.
- His treatment of Plato is polemical rather than analytic. Calling Plato a "destroyer of the earlier culture" is not an argument against the Forms.
- Dodds's reply in the Preface is the fairest verdict: both men "exaggerate the partial truth" they stress (p. xi).

**Strengths of Burkert.**
- He supplies what Lloyd-Jones underplays: the comic and amoral side of the Olympians; ritual practice, which sits beside moral theory without fully submitting to it; and the structural limits of polytheism.
- His distinction between *Dios dika* and *dikaios* answers Lloyd-Jones's own unsolved problem from the end of chapter I. Zeus can send *atē* and still be the guarantor of justice, because he is the source of the order rather than a party bound by it.
- His remark that pressing the link between gods and morality widens "the gulf between ideal and reality" explains why the Greek complaint against the gods (Theognis, Euripides' Bellerophon, Thucydides' plague) grows *with* moralisation rather than against it.

**Weaknesses of Burkert.** His handbook format lets him state the contradictions without resolving them, and he is readier than Lloyd-Jones to let a fragment such as the *Sisyphus* speak for a period.

Taken together, they answer the title question with a qualified yes. From Homer onward the Greeks believed that Zeus punishes perjury, the abuse of strangers and injustice between men, and that the gods hate *hybris*. That belief is not a late moralising addition. But Zeus's justice is the justice of a king over the universe, not of a father to his children, and it is grounded in his power and *timē*. It never wholly mastered the amoral elements of myth and ritual, and so it left room both for Greek ethics as human wisdom and for the Greek complaint against the gods.

### Conclusion

*The Justice of Zeus* is a polemic for continuity. Against Dodds, Adkins and Snell it argues that Homer's Zeus already guards justice through kings, oaths and hospitality, and that Homer's heroes decide and are responsible despite the gods' share in their decisions. On this reading the archaic "age of anxiety", divine envy and pollution are mostly effects of genre, and Aeschylus, Sophocles, Herodotus, Euripides and even Thucydides share one harsh but intelligible theology of Zeus and Dike. Plato is the one who breaks with it. Burkert grants the antiquity of the link between gods and justice and the reality of double motivation. But his Zeus is the source of justice rather than just. His polytheism can apportion the world but not fully legitimate a moral order, and his gods stay amoral in myth and ritual even as they are moralised in poetry. Lloyd-Jones gives the stronger account of what the Greeks believed. Burkert gives the clearer account of why that belief never closed the gap between the justice of Zeus and the experience of men.
