The *Oresteia* (458 BC) is the only complete Greek tragic trilogy we have. It begins with a watchman on a palace roof praying to "the gods" for release from his toil. It ends with a torch-lit procession through Athens, led by goddesses. Between those points: a curse on a family, three killings, a trial, and the founding of the Athenian court of the Areopagus. The question is what kind of ending that is.

## 1. The Story

**Background: the house of Atreus**
- Atreus fed his brother Thyestes his own children at a banquet, in revenge for adultery with his wife.
- Thyestes' surviving son is Aegisthus.
- Atreus' sons are Agamemnon and Menelaus.

**Agamemnon**
- At Aulis the fleet sailing to Troy to avenge Helen is held back by **Artemis**, who is angered by an omen: two eagles (the two kings) tearing a pregnant hare.
- The seer Calchas says she wants "another sacrifice": Agamemnon's daughter **Iphigenia**. Agamemnon kills her.
- Ten years later Troy falls. Clytemnestra, his wife (now Aegisthus' lover), learns of it by a chain of beacons.
- She persuades Agamemnon to walk into the palace on purple tapestries, a hubris he knows is wrong. This is the **carpet scene**.
- She kills him in the bath, caught in a robe like a net, and with him the Trojan prophetess **Cassandra**, his captive. Cassandra has foreseen everything, but no one believes her.

**Libation Bearers** (*Choephoroi*)
- Agamemnon's son **Orestes**, ordered by Apollo, returns secretly.
- At his father's tomb he is recognized by his sister Electra. A huge ritual lament calls up the dead father's help.
- Clytemnestra has dreamed she gave birth to a snake that drew blood from her breast. Orestes: "I am the snake."
- He kills Aegisthus, then his mother. She bares her breast; he hesitates and asks Pylades, "What shall I do?" Pylades: obey Apollo.
- The **Furies** (Erinyes), the hounds of the mother's blood, appear to him alone and drive him out.

**Eumenides**
- At Delphi Apollo purifies Orestes and sends him to Athens.
- Athena sets up a court of Athenian citizens.
  - **Apollo's defence**: the mother is not the true parent, only "a host for the male seed". Proof: Athena herself, born from Zeus's head without a mother.
  - **The votes are equal.** Athena breaks the tie for Orestes: "no mother bore me... I am wholly of the father."
- The Furies threaten to poison Attica. Athena **persuades** them to accept a home under the Acropolis as honoured guests (*metics*), the "Kindly Ones" (*Eumenides*) or "Revered Ones" (*Semnai*).

## 2. The Key Formulas

- **"The doer suffers"** (*pathein ton erxanta*, *Cho.* 313). Every avenger becomes the next victim, "the hunter hunted, the sacrificer sacrificed" (Goldhill).
- **"Learning through suffering"** (*pathei mathos*, *Ag.* 177), from the "Zeus hymn": Zeus "lays it down as law that we must suffer, suffer into truth" (Fagles). And "from the gods grace is violent" (*charis biaios*).
- **The double bind** (*Ag.* 206–11). Agamemnon at Aulis: "A heavy fate not to obey, but heavy if I am to rend my child... What of this is without disaster?" Either choice is a crime.
- **"Ares will clash with Ares, Dikē with Dikē"** (*Cho.* 461).
- **Clytemnestra's** "the ancient bitter avenger" (*alastor*) "in the form of the dead man's wife" did it (*Ag.* 1497–1503). The chorus answers: "Who will bear witness that you are not responsible?"

## 3. The Standard Reading: From Vendetta to Law

Kitto and the long tradition he summarizes:
- In *Agamemnon*, *dikē* means "**requital**": wrongs must be paid in blood. Zeus's justice is still retaliation.
- In *Libation Bearers* this justice is shown to be **unworkable**: "If Dikē conflicts with Dikē... the universe is chaotic."
- Electra introduces a distinction that points ahead. She asks whether they are praying for "a **juror** or one who **brings retribution**" (*dikastēs* or *dikēphoros*, *Cho.* 120).
- In *Eumenides* the court replaces the feud. Wrath gives way to Reason, and "Zeus has moved forward from violence... to reason and mercy". Athena's persuasion turns the Furies from curse to blessing.
- So the trilogy is "a charter for the city": a myth of origin for the law-court and democracy, moving from the false light of beacons to the true light of the torches.
- **Fagles and Stanford** read it as an evolution of the gods too:
  - three generations of gods (Ouranos, Kronos, Zeus) parallel three generations of the house of Atreus;
  - "a Theogony that ends in harmony rather than suppression";
  - the court's verdict is "justifiable homicide, not innocence outright";
  - "the law is only medial; its contradictions call for the harmony... that is its goal".
- **The Marxist and feminist** version accepts the move to law but evaluates it differently. It is the rise of state power and the "myth of matriarchy overturned": female claims (blood, the mother, the Furies) defeated and housed under the city, as women were in Athens.

## 4. Goldhill: Three Problems with "Vendetta to Law"

1. **The trial doesn't resolve anything.** The human vote is **tied**. The Furies then threaten to destroy Athens. What ends the conflict is Athena's **persuasion**, not the legal process, and more than a third of the play comes after Orestes leaves.
2. **Every conflict is a clash of real obligations**, which the ending doesn't remove:
   - Agamemnon: the army versus the father;
   - Clytemnestra: the daughter versus the husband;
   - Orestes: restoring his house by destroying its most basic bond.
   - "The Oresteia shows the potential for violent conflict of interest within the obligations of social order."
3. **The word *dikē* won't stay still.** Its senses run from "right" and "proper" to "retribution" to "lawsuit" and "court".
   - Agamemnon's first words use it three times in three senses (*Ag.* 810–16), ending in a picture of the gods voting with urns.
   - "Dikē with Dikē" (*Cho.* 461) is followed at once by "O gods, be just in what you bring about." The appeal to justice comes in the same vocabulary as the clash.
   - **Every character claims *dikē* for their side.** The play shows evaluative language becoming a source of conflict, not just its solution.

**Gender** (Goldhill §7). Every conflict is male against female:
- Zeus against Artemis; Agamemnon against Clytemnestra; Orestes against his mother; Apollo against the Furies.
- The **female** side defends blood ties against the social order; the **male** side defends the social order against blood.
- Clytemnestra's "man-plotting heart" means both plotting *like* a man and *against* one. She transgresses by speech (persuasion, deceit) and by sex (adultery). Aegisthus is called "woman".
- The resolution needs **Athena**, a female who is "wholly of the father", a virgin warrior with no mother, who fits neither side. And she is uncomfortably like Clytemnestra: persuasive, male-minded, in command.

**The divine frame** (§12). The gods don't settle things from above:
- the chorus says Zeus is "all-responsible" (*panaitios*), then asks who will say Clytemnestra is "not responsible" (*anaitios*);
- the Furies call Apollo "all-responsible" for the matricide;
- the gods themselves disagree, and Apollo's "truth" doesn't win over the human jury.

"Aeschylus depicts the relations between men and gods as a shifting source of fear, doubt and loss as well as celebration, support and order."

**Homer** (§8): in the *Odyssey*, Orestes' story is told nine times as a **model** for Telemachus. Orestes kills **Aegisthus**, the usurper, and the matricide is barely mentioned. Aeschylus moves Clytemnestra to the centre and makes the matricide the crux.

## 5. Vellacott's Dissent: There Is No Reconciliation

Vellacott, the Penguin translator, says that twenty years after translating it he became "uneasy about the traditional acceptance of 'reconciliation' and the 'new concept of justice'". His "radically new interpretation":
- **Matricide was a crime of unique horror** to the audience, with "no cure", "no expiation", repeated in all three plays. The trilogy never answers it. Athena "evades" the difference between homicide and matricide.
- **Apollo lies.** He claims purification at Delphi, but Orestes later says his pollution was "rubbed off" by visiting "other houses". Apollo's threats to Orestes are false, and his biology is absurd. "If the sexual union of man and wife is greater than an oath, does not the bond of mother and son have an equal claim?"
- **Agamemnon** is no tragic hero caught in a double bind. He "would put his own glory first and the sanctity of kindred blood second". The eagles that tear the pregnant hare are his nature, and the carpet scene shows it.
- **Clytemnestra** is not a monster. "To call Clytemnestra abnormal invites... a hostility towards her which the poet gives no indication of sharing." She is the only one who calls the sacrifice of a child what it is.
- **Cassandra** shows what Apollo is: he destroyed her twice, by his gift and by bringing her to this house.
- **The Furies are "the sole voice in the trilogy that utters consistent and rational morality"**: they pursue only the guilty, and "the doer suffers".
- **The ending is political flattery.** Athena's speeches praised an ideal the audience "knew had faded", but they "did not want to relinquish the comfortable illusion". The first audience's applause "showed that the poet's supreme powers had failed to win... a sympathetic hearing" for his real point.
- **Euripides understood.** His *Electra*, *Iphigenia in Tauris* and *Orestes* are a "commentary on the *Oresteia*" written forty years into a war in which the Atreid revenges had become a symbol of Athens' own crimes. "That noble deed was not noble... 'Crime in a just cause' is an impious sophistry" (Euripides' *Orestes*).

## 6. Nikolopoulou: Justice as Cosmic Order, Not Just Procedure

*Hunting for Justice* (2025) reads the trilogy as a "cosmology of *dikē*", drawing on Burkert's *Homo Necans* (religion rooted in the hunt and sacrifice).
- ***Dikē* means first one's proper way, one's nature.** Its syntactic use is "as", "in the manner of": the watchman lies "**like a dog**" on the roof. Justice is each thing being what it is in the order of things (*ta pragmata*, "reality").
- **Agamemnon is Artemis's play.** She is the goddess of the hunt and of wild nature, protector of the young, and she punishes hunters' excess (Actaeon). The eagles' feast is a hunt gone wrong, and Agamemnon answers to *her* order, not just Zeus's politics.
- **Libation Bearers is the earth's play.** Electra prays to "Earth and victorious Justice" (*Gē kai Dikē*). The dead father under the ground, the tomb, the snake and Hermes "of the axis" linking above and below: justice has a **chthonic** root in the dead and in kinship.
- **Eumenides**: Athena's court doesn't *abolish* the old order. The Furies are given a home, not destroyed. "The gods of 'old' barbarism and the gods of 'new' rationality... have existed alongside each other from time immemorial."
- **There is always a remainder.** "There will always be a remainder of injustice: the 'last' victim may never be sufficiently vindicated, and the 'last' offender may be said to be simply released rather than 'forgiven'."
- Her polemic: modern readings that see the ending as patriarchal ideology assume that justice is only social power that better procedure can fix. The trilogy knows a justice rooted in **nature and necessity** that no court controls.
- Epilogue on *Oedipus*: the shepherd who lied to save a baby shows "the limits of human *dikē*".

## 7. Cassandra and the Chorus

- **Cassandra** (*Ag.* 1072–1330) is the trilogy's most haunting scene.
  - She smells blood in the house: "the house breathes blood dripping gore". The chorus: "This is the smell of the sacrifices at the hearth."
  - She sees the **band of Furies** drunk on human blood, singing in the house of "the first and originating outrage".
  - She throws down Apollo's insignia and goes in knowing she will die.
  - Fagles: her first true prophecy to be believed is about the future avenger, and through it "she herself becomes empowered".
- **The Argive elders** sing the great odes on Zeus and suffering. But Vellacott calls them "timid or well-meaning opportunism, a knowledge of moral principle without integrity to maintain or implement it". They say the right things and do nothing.

## 8. Imagery

**Sacrifice corrupted** (Goldhill §11):
- the eagles "sacrifice" the hare;
- Iphigenia is sacrificed like a goat;
- Agamemnon is killed like a victim, *teleios*, "perfect";
- Clytemnestra calls the murder a libation and rejoices in his spattered blood "as the sown field in the rain of Zeus".

Sacrifice, which should mark the proper order of gods, men and beasts, is used to kill kin.

**Net, robe and snake**:
- the robe that trapped Agamemnon;
- the snake of Clytemnestra's dream, which turns from mother to son;
- the Furies, snake-haired like Gorgons.

**Light**: the false beacon at the start and the torch procession at the end (Fagles: "the serpent and the eagle").

## 9. With Girard

The *Oresteia* fits Girard's account of how the **judicial system replaces sacrifice and revenge** (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 1).
- **Vendetta** is reciprocal violence without end. The house of Atreus is the "sacrificial crisis": doubles, each claiming *dikē*, "Ares against Ares".
- **The court** works because it has "a monopoly on the means of revenge", the last word. Its legitimacy depends on transcendence: Athena founds it, and the Furies must be given a place inside.
- And the trilogy shows its **sacrificial underside**:
  - the play opens with a human sacrifice (Iphigenia);
  - the Furies' violence isn't abolished but housed in the city as fear: Athena says fear must stay in her city;
  - harmony is bought by **expelling** something to the margins, the Furies as resident aliens.

## 10. Comparison of the Readings

| reading | what the ending is | key evidence | weak point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **evolutionary** (Kitto, Fagles) | justice grows from revenge into law; the gods grow too | Electra's "juror or avenger"; the Furies converted | the tied vote; Apollo's argument |
| **ideological** (Marxist, feminist) | the state and patriarchy legitimized | "I am wholly of the father"; Furies made subordinate | ignores the play's own doubts |
| **Goldhill** | a celebration with its questions still sounding | the ambiguity of *dikē*; persuasion, not trial, ends it | leaves us with no answer |
| **Vellacott** | a hollow "reconciliation" that excuses matricide | Apollo's lies; the Furies' consistency; Euripides | requires the poet to be ironic against his own ending |
| **Nikolopoulou** | justice rooted in cosmic order that the court can't abolish | Artemis, Earth, the Furies housed not destroyed | dense; polemical about modern readings |

## 11. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What the trilogy sees truly**
- **"The doer suffers"** and the endless return of violence: "they that take the sword shall perish with the sword" (Matt 26:52); "whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed" (Gen 9:6). Without something new, revenge never ends.
- **"Learning through suffering"** is near the biblical wisdom that "tribulation worketh patience" (Rom 5:3) and that Christ himself "learned... obedience by the things which he suffered" (Heb 5:8). But in Aeschylus it is *charis biaios*, violent grace from a distant Zeus.
- **The court as a gift of God, not an invention of man.** Athena founds the Areopagus; Scripture gives "judges and officers in all thy gates" (Deut 16:18) and "the powers that be are ordained of God... a revenger to execute wrath" (Rom 13:1–4). Public justice restrains private vengeance. Moses's cities of refuge (Num 35) do exactly what Athena's court does for Orestes: shelter the killer from the avenger of blood until trial.
- **The Areopagus** is where Paul preached to the Athenians about "the unknown god" who "will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained" (Acts 17:22–31). The court founded to end the Furies' vengeance hears of the Judge who is also the one who was judged.

**Where it falls short**
- **The tied vote** is honest. Human justice can't fully settle the guilt of a matricide that was commanded, nor the claims of a murdered mother. Something has to break the tie, and in Aeschylus it is a goddess's decision for "the father". In the Gospel, mercy doesn't mean the victim's blood is ignored. "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (Ps 85:10), at the Cross.
- **The Furies are housed, not healed.** They become honoured fear under the city. That is the limit of every civic religion: it can restrain vengeance but not take it away. "Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord" (Rom 12:19). The Christian can give up revenge only because it is handed to a God who is both just and merciful, and who bears the cost himself.
- **The house of Atreus and inherited guilt.** Cassandra's Furies feeding on "the first outrage" match the archaic idea Dodds describes. The Orthodox teaching of ancestral sin keeps the solidarity (one family, one nature wounded) without the personal inheritance of guilt (Ezek 18:20: "The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father"). See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]], [[Dionysus, Orpheus and the Mysteries: Dodds and the Irrational]].
- **Vellacott's point stands as a warning.** A city celebrating its own justice can paper over a terrible act. Compare [[Joseph de Maistre on Providence and Suffering]] on the executioner, and Girard on how order is bought.
