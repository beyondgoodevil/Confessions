> "From whence come wars and fightings among you? come they not hence, even of your lusts that war in your members?" (Jas 4:1)

Thucydides, an Athenian general exiled in 424 BC for failing to save Amphipolis, wrote the history of the war between Athens and Sparta (431–404) "as a possession for all time". He did not mean that he was a great writer. He meant that the same things will happen again "as long as the nature of mankind remains the same". He is the first author to treat **human nature** (*to anthrōpinon*, *hē anthrōpeia physis*) as the subject of history: not the gods' plans, but what fear, ambition, greed, hope and love do to men and cities under pressure. Hobbes translated him in 1629 and called him "the most politic historiographer that ever writ".

## 1. Method: Speeches, Deeds and "A Possession for All Time" (1.20–23)

- **Against easy belief.** "The way that most men deal with traditions… is to receive them all alike… without applying any critical test whatever." Even Athenians get their own tyrannicides wrong: Hipparchus was not the tyrant, Hippias was.
- **Two standards (1.22).**
  - For *speeches*, he made each speaker "say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions", keeping "as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said".
  - For *deeds*, he trusted nothing at first hand, "not even my own impressions". He tested each report "by the most severe and detailed tests possible", because eyewitnesses differ "from imperfect memory" or "undue partiality".
  - Price stresses what follows from this: the speeches show how people **thought and talked**, and that is itself evidence. How words are used is a symptom.
- **Use, not pleasure.** "The absence of romance in my history will, I fear, detract somewhat from its interest". But it is written for those "who desire an exact knowledge of the past as an aid to the understanding of the future, which in the course of human things must resemble if it does not reflect it". It was "not as an essay which is to win the applause of the moment, but as a possession for all time" (*ktēma es aiei*).
- **The truest cause (1.23.6).** Behind the stated grievances (Corcyra, Potidaea, Megara) lay "the real cause… formally most kept out of sight": "the growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Sparta, made war inevitable". Woodruff notes that the Spartan vote for war was officially about **Athenian injustice** (1.87), but Thucydides says it was really about **fear** (1.88).

## 2. The Athenian Thesis (1.75–77)

Before the war, Athenian envoys at Sparta give the clearest statement of what Orwin calls **"the Athenian thesis"**:

- The empire came to them honourably, by Sparta's default, and then "the nature of the case first compelled us… fear being our principal motive, though honor and interest afterwards came in".
- **The three compulsions.** "It was not a very remarkable action, or contrary to the common practice of mankind, if we did accept an empire that was offered to us, and refused to give it up under the pressure of three of the strongest motives, **fear, honor, and interest**. And it was not we who set the example, for it has always been the law that the weaker should be subject to the stronger."
- **Justice as a pretext.** Sparta "take[s] up the cry of justice" only now "calculations of interest" suggest it. Justice is "a consideration which no one ever yet brought forward to hinder his ambition when he had a chance of gaining anything by might".
- **And yet a claim to virtue.** "Praise is due to all who, if not so superior to human nature as to refuse dominion, yet respect justice more than their position compels them to do" (1.76.3). Athens judges her allies in impartial courts and is hated *more* for it: "men's indignation… is more excited by legal wrong than by violent wrong" (1.77.4).
- **Orwin's point.** The thesis contradicts itself from the start. If honour and profit are as *compulsory* as safety, nobody can be blamed for anything. But the envoys still want credit for a justice beyond what compulsion requires. "'Fiat justitia, where it costs little' cannot be the authentic voice of justice."
- **Woodruff's point.** The Athenians never say "might *makes* right", which is Thrasymachus' or Callicles' normative claim. They say might *trumps* right **as a matter of fact**. No Callicles appears anywhere in Thucydides. The debates are "not about what justice requires but about whether to pay attention to justice in a given situation".

## 3. The Funeral Oration and the Plague (2.35–54)

**Pericles' Funeral Oration** (winter 431/0) is the classic portrait of Athens:
- A constitution that is "a pattern to others". It is called a democracy because it favours the many. Advancement goes by merit, and "poverty [does not] bar the way".
- Freedom in private life together with obedience to the laws, "particularly such as regard the protection of the injured", including the **unwritten laws** "which… cannot be broken without acknowledged disgrace".
- "We cultivate refinement without extravagance and knowledge without effeminacy". Discussion is not "a stumblingblock in the way of action" but "an indispensable preliminary".
- "As a city we are **the school of Hellas**." Athens needs no Homer, for "we have forced every sea and land to be the highway of our daring… whether for evil or for good".
- Orwin: the oration presents the empire as **a free project, untouched by necessity**. It is "thoroughly godless" and "consistently abstracts from death and the body".

**Then, immediately, the plague** (2.47–54), which Thucydides caught and survived:
- Refugees crammed into "stifling cabins". "The bodies of dying men lay one upon another". The sacred places were "full of corpses". The burial laws collapsed.
- **Moral collapse (2.53).** "Men now did just what they pleased". Honour was abandoned because no one expected to live to reap it. "Present enjoyment… was both honorable and useful." "**Fear of gods or law of man there was none to restrain them**". As for the gods, "they judged it to be just the same whether they worshipped them or not, as they saw all alike perishing".
- Woodruff and Orwin agree that the placement is deliberate. "Pericles' glowing words might make us all 'lovers of Athens', but there is little to love when the plague strips away the veneer of decency."
- Orwin: plague and civil war are **opposite routes to the same dissolution**. Plague *depoliticises* (each man alone with his body). Stasis *over-politicises* (faction swallows everything). Both show that "Society proves to depend more fundamentally on our hopes and fears for our bodies" than on the noble self-transcendence Pericles praised.

**Pericles' last speech** drops the mask: "your empire is really like a tyranny; though it may have been thought unjust to seize, it is now unsafe to surrender" (2.63).

**Thucydides' verdict (2.65).** Pericles "led them instead of being led by them". "What was nominally a democracy was becoming in his hands government by the first citizen." His successors, "more on a level with one another, and each grasping at supremacy", handed policy to "the whims of the multitude". The result was Sicily, faction, and defeat by "their own intestine disorders".

## 4. The Mytilene Debate: Cleon against Diodotus (3.36–50)

Mytilene on Lesbos revolted in 428. In anger, the Athenian assembly voted to kill all its men and enslave the women and children. The next day they reconsidered.

**Cleon**, "the most violent man at Athens":
- "A democracy is incapable of empire". "Your empire is a despotism and your subjects disaffected conspirators".
- "Bad laws which are never changed are better for a city than good ones that have no authority".
- He mocks the assembly as "very slaves to the pleasure of the ear… more like the audience of a rhetorician than the council of a city".
- The "three failings most fatal to empire" are "pity, sentiment, and indulgence".
- Justice and expediency coincide: "if they were right in rebelling, you must be wrong in ruling". "Teach your other allies by a striking example that the penalty of rebellion is death."

**Diodotus**, otherwise unknown:
- "The two things most opposed to good counsel are haste and passion."
- "We are not in a court of justice, but in a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mytilenians useful to Athens."
- **On human nature (3.45).** Ever harsher penalties have never stopped crime. Poverty gives "the courage of necessity". Plenty gives "the ambition which belongs to insolence and pride". "Hope also and greed, the one leading and the other following… cause the widest ruin". "It is impossible to prevent… human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon, by force of law or by any other deterrent force whatsoever."
- So the death penalty for revolt only makes rebels fight to the end. Killing the common people, who surrendered the city, would drive the *dēmos* everywhere into the oligarchs' arms. "Put up with injustice voluntarily" rather than "put to death, however justly, those whom it is our interest to keep alive."

**The outcome.** Diodotus won "almost equal[ly]" on a show of hands. A second trireme rowed day and night, the crew eating barley-cakes "kneaded with oil and wine as they rowed", and arrived just as the commander Paches was reading the first decree. Still, "rather more than a thousand" ringleaders were executed on Cleon's motion.

**The paradox Woodruff and Orwin both notice.** Cleon argues for slaughter in the name of *justice*. Diodotus argues for mercy while explicitly *setting justice aside*. "Even if they were guilty, we ought to seem not to notice it." For Orwin, Diodotus is the wisest speaker in the book. He announces "a more terrible truth than that human beings are evil; namely, that they are not". They are carried away by hope, eros and fortune, and "tout comprendre est tout pardonner".

**Plataea (3.52–68), the Spartan counterpart.** The Spartan judges asked the surrendered Plataeans one question, whether they had done Sparta "any service in the war", and killed every man who said no. The Thebans had argued: "if justice requires that they suffer… their suffering ought to be a source of delight." Spartan "justice" is Spartan interest dressed up.

## 5. Corcyra: When Words Changed Their Meanings (3.81–83)

The democrats of Corcyra, protected by an Athenian fleet, massacred their opponents for seven days. "Sons were killed by their fathers, and suppliants dragged from the altar or slain upon it". Some were "walled up in the temple of Dionysus". Thucydides then gives his longest comment in his own voice:

- **War is a violent teacher.** "In peace and prosperity states and individuals have better sentiments… but war takes away the easy supply of daily wants and so proves **a rough master** that brings most men's characters to a level with their fortunes." These things "have occurred and always will occur as long as the nature of mankind remains the same".
- **The transvaluation of words.** "Words had to change their ordinary meaning". Reckless audacity was called loyal courage. Prudent hesitation was "specious cowardice", moderation "a cloak for unmanliness", and the ability to see all sides "incapacity to act on any". "The advocate of extreme measures was always trustworthy; his opponent a man to be suspected."
- **Kinship, law and the gods fail in turn.** "Blood became a weaker tie than party". Faction members trusted each other not through "any religious sanction" but through "complicity in crime". Oaths held only "so long as no other weapon was at hand". "Revenge also was held of more account than self-preservation." "Men are readier to call rogues clever than simpletons honest."
- **The cause.** "The cause of all these evils was the lust for power arising from **greed and ambition**" (*pleonexia* and *philotimia*). The slogans ("political equality of The People", "a moderate aristocracy") were fair names for private prizes. "Thus religion was in honor with neither party". The moderates "perished between the two".
- **The end of simplicity.** "The ancient simplicity into which honor so largely entered was laughed down and disappeared". Hornblower calls this "a clear authorial rejection of the 'relativistic' teaching of certain of the sophists". The blunter wits won, because they struck first.
- **3.84** (human nature "always rebelling against the law and now its master") is rejected by most editors as a later interpolation. It is in the Landmark text but should not be leaned on.

**The readings**
- **Orwin.** Thucydides anticipates Aristotle's ethics of the mean. Stasis makes "the excess of a disposition" the standard and despises the mean as deficient, so "the extreme continuously feeds on itself". His deepest lesson is negative: "the chasms that yawn beneath us in politics are deeper than the peaks that beckon us are high". Hence his quiet sympathy for **Sparta**, whose regime aimed less at the best than at avoiding the worst, and was not convulsed by stasis as Athens was. Hobbes learned his "war of all against all" here. But where Hobbes debunks, Thucydides mourns, because plague and stasis destroy "the better citizens most".
- **Orwin against the linguistic fashion.** The breakdown is not "internal to the realm of conventions or 'culture'". It is "the power of *nature* in human life, opposing and overwhelming that of convention": the frailty of the body and the ambition and vindictiveness of the soul.
- **Price.** The Corcyra passage is a **clinical model** of a disease, with symptoms, aetiology and course, applied to the whole *History*. Stasis differs from ordinary war (*polemos*). It is fought to annihilation. Neutrality and moderation become impossible. Treaties are "temporary devices". Revenge breeds counter-revenge. Price's thesis is that Thucydides presents the whole Peloponnesian War as **a stasis of Hellas**, a civil war of one people rather than a war between states. Plato later draws the same line (*Republic* 470b): Greek against Greek is stasis, not war.

## 6. The Melian Dialogue (5.84–116)

In 416, in the middle of a nominal peace, Athens came to neutral Melos, a Spartan colony, with 38 ships and about 3,000 hoplites. The Melian magistrates heard the envoys in private, "before… the few".

- **The ground rules.** No "specious pretenses" about Persia or wrongs suffered, "since you know as well as we do that right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while **the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must**".
- **Melos: justice is in your interest too.** Don't destroy "our common protection… the privilege of being allowed in danger to invoke what is fair and right", for "your fall would be a signal for the heaviest vengeance".
- **Neutrality refused.** "Your hostility cannot so much hurt us as your friendship will be an argument to our subjects of our weakness". Islanders above all must not "thwart… the masters of the sea".
- **On hope.** Hope is "danger's comforter". Do not, like "the vulgar", "turn to the invisible, to prophecies and oracles… that delude men with hopes to their destruction".
- **On the gods.** "Of the gods we believe, and of men we know, that by a necessary law of their nature **they rule wherever they can**. And it is not as if we were the first to make this law… we found it existing before us, and shall leave it to exist forever after us". The Spartans "consider what is agreeable honorable, and what is expedient just".
- **On disgrace.** Do not be "enslaved by the phrase" of disgrace and so "fall willfully into hopeless disaster".
- **The answer.** "We will not in a moment deprive of freedom a city that has been inhabited these seven hundred years". **The end (5.116):** after "some treachery" inside, the Athenians "put to death all the grown men… and sold the women and children for slaves", and settled 500 colonists.

**The readings**
- **Woodruff.** The Athenians are not wrong that *justice* in the narrow sense, which settles disputes between equals, has little purchase here. But they are wrong to discard **reverence** (*to hosion*, *eusebeia*), "the virtue that is supposed to prevent the strong from abusing the weak", the virtue that protects suppliants. Thucydides probably planned an "Athenian dialogue" for 404, when Athens herself lay at Sparta's mercy. The Melian oligarchs are also "hypocrites". They kept the envoys from the people, who in the end let the Athenians in, and they put their own power above their people's safety.
- **Price.** The dialogue is "the greatest example of the breakdown of communication in all the *History*". Stitch together only the Athenian lines and you get a coherent speech in which the Melian replies "would be hardly felt". It is a philosophical form that achieves no philosophy. The two sides argue from "irreconcilable and incompatible world views". The theory of the strong ruling is not Thucydides' doctrine but reveals "how their particular circumstances influence the way they see the world".
- **Orwin.** Even here the Athenians cannot let go of nobility. They boast of the "daring" that is a condition of justice, while preaching pure prudence to the Melians. "In context this claim seems wistful and even slightly ludicrous… In the light of the sequel, it is shocking."
- **The placement.** Book 6 opens straight after, with the Sicilian expedition. Scione and Torone were treated just as harshly, but only Melos gets a debate.

## 7. Sicily: Eros and Ruin (Books 6–7)

- **6.24.** Nicias tried to deter the assembly by demanding an enormous force. It backfired: **eros for the expedition fell upon all alike**. The old hoped for conquest, the young for sights abroad, the many for pay and new dominions. Orwin links this to Diodotus: "the city as plaything of eros" is incompatible with Pericles' "city as object of eros".
- **Mycalessus (7.29).** Thracian mercenaries hired by Athens and sent home in the middle of the war took a small Boeotian town and killed "children and women, and even beasts of burden". They attacked "a boys' school, the largest that there was in the place… and massacred them all". It was "unsurpassed in magnitude, and unapproached by any in suddenness and in horror".
- **Nicias' last speech (7.77).** "My life has been one of much devotion toward the gods, and of much justice and without offense toward men". "If any of the gods was offended at our expedition, we have already been amply punished… we have become fitter objects for their pity than their jealousy."
- **His death (7.86).** He was butchered against Gylippus' will, a man "who, of all the Hellenes in my time, **least deserved such a fate**, seeing that the whole course of his life had been regulated with strict attention to virtue". About 7,000 prisoners lay in the quarries for months on half a pint of water a day.
- **7.87.** "Everything was destroyed, and **few out of many returned home**." It was the greatest Greek reverse in history.
- **Orwin on piety.** The pious (the Melians, Nicias, the Spartans) expect the gods to protect the just. Thucydides shows no such providence. Yet the Athenians lost Sicily partly *because* of piety. Alcibiades was recalled over the mutilation of the Herms, leaving command to the most pious and least suited man. Orwin quotes Strauss: "Not indeed the gods, but the human concern with the gods without which there cannot be a free city, took terrible revenge on the Athenians." Thucydides' theology, says Strauss, is "in the mean" between Nicias and the Athenian envoys at Melos.

## 8. Thucydides in Byzantium (Kennedy and Kaldellis)

- The text survives **only** because Byzantium copied it. No translation existed before the late 14th century. A 9th-century epigram, often added to the manuscripts, warns: "if you are completely ignorant of the Muses, throw away what you do not understand".
- The Byzantines read him above all as a **model of hard Attic prose and rhetoric**, after Demosthenes in the curriculum. The 6th-century *Life* by Markellinos defends his obscurity as a mystery for initiates. They mostly did *not* care about the Peloponnesian War, since they were Christian *Romans*, not Greeks.
- **Imitators.**
  - Prokopios modelled his account of the Justinianic plague on Thucydides. In the *Wars* he borrowed the Corinthians' description of the Athenians to "praise" Justinian, quietly dropping the word "innovators": a hidden door to his *Secret History*.
  - Psellos explained a phrase in Trinitarian theology by citing Thucydidean grammar.
  - In the 14th century, Nikephoros Gregoras rewrote the Plataeans' speech as a warning against civil war while the Turks crossed into Europe.
  - After 1453, Kritoboulos wrote a "Thucydidean" history of Mehmet II. Its closing plague narrative subtly buries the sultan's new city, just as Thucydides' plague followed Pericles' oration. "Expectation of providence completely disappeared."

## 10. Assessment

*Mine.*

**A true diagnosis of fallen man**
- Thucydides' "human nature" is very close to what Scripture calls the heart after the Fall: "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Gen 8:21, which Orwin himself cites). His causes of Corcyra, **pleonexia** and **philotimia**, are exactly the Fathers' **covetousness** and **vainglory**, two of Evagrius' eight thoughts. His analysis of the plague (the body's terror producing indulgence) is the patristic logic of how *fear of death* enslaves man to sin: "through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" (Heb 2:15). See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]] and [[Death and Corruptibility as Privations of Life and the Ability to Sin]].
- **Corcyra** is Isaiah's woe: "Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light" (Isa 5:20). The corruption of language is the passions' first victory. The ascetic tradition makes the same point when it says the demons work through *logismoi* that rename the sin before it is committed. See [[How a Thought Becomes a Sin: The Stages of Temptation]].
- **Diodotus' "terrible truth"** is remarkably Orthodox. Men are not evil by essence: evil is not a nature, and the Orthodox teach ancestral sin, not total depravity. But they are swept away by hope, desire and the will to more. What Orthodoxy adds is the cure. Thucydides can offer only foresight and sober institutions. The Church offers *metanoia*, which reorders the very passions he shows to be ungovernable by law. See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]] and [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]].

**Against the "law" of the strong**
- The Athenian envoys claim the strong rule always, gods and men alike. Christ names the same law and abolishes it among his own: "The kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them… But ye shall not be so: but he that is greatest among you, let him be as the younger" (Luke 22:25–26). The Theotokos sings its reversal: "He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree" (Luke 1:52).
- Woodruff's point about **reverence** is the biblical one. God is the defender of those with no defender. "Remove not the old landmark; and enter not into the fields of the fatherless: for their redeemer is mighty; he shall plead their cause" (Prov 23:10–11). The Melians had no justice of equals to appeal to. That is exactly why the fear of God mattered, and its absence at Melos foreshadows Athens' own fall.

**On hope and providence**
- The Athenians mock the Melians' hope in "the invisible". Christian hope is indeed in the invisible ("hope that is seen is not hope", Rom 8:24). But it does not promise what the Melians hoped for, rescue in this life. The martyrs are Melians who were *not* saved from the sword and are nonetheless vindicated. Thucydides is right that pious hope for worldly reward is often self-deceit. He cannot see the hope that does not depend on outcomes.
- **Nicias**, the just man who "least deserved such a fate", is the problem of Job and Ecclesiastes: "the race is not to the swift… but time and chance happeneth to them all" (Eccl 9:11). Thucydides leaves the problem open. Scripture answers it only at the Cross. See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].

**What Thucydides teaches the Christian statesman**
- **Restraint in judgement.** Diodotus' plea for the innocent of Mytilene and against haste and anger is a better political ethic than most Christian rhetoric. It is the ascetic counsel Papanikolaou urges in [[Orthodox Political Theology: Symphonia, Autocracy or Democracy?]].
- **Sobriety about regimes.** His preference for avoiding the abyss over reaching for the heights matches Orthodoxy's suspicion of utopias and its view of the state as a restraint on sin.
- **The limit.** But a purely human prudence cannot keep a city from Corcyra for long. The bond that is stronger than faction is the one Thucydides watched dissolve: oaths sworn before God, kept by men who fear Him. "Except the Lord keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain" (Ps 127:1).
