> "Have I been understood?—Dionysus versus the Crucified.—" (the last line of *Ecce Homo*)

N. = Nietzsche. Girard's essay is short (about twenty pages) but it is one of the most interesting things written on N. and religion, because Girard takes N.'s anti-Christianity more seriously than N.'s admirers do.

## 1. Why the Formula Gets Ignored

- After the war, people argued about whether N. was responsible for Nazi antisemitism. Nobody bothered defending his **anti-Christianity**: most intellectuals agreed with it, so it needed no apology.
- The fashionable French Nietzsches (genealogist, "free play", counter-culture) all ignore what obsessed his last lucid years.
- **Girard blames Heidegger** for emptying the formula:
  - religion and "thought" are incompatible for Heidegger, so N.'s religious side has to be non-thought;
  - he reads "Dionysus versus the Crucified" as just a rival monotheism, the same empty power struggle with the sign reversed;
  - he presents himself as indifferent to religion, and that indifference became a model.
- Girard's view: the closer N. gets to the end, the more the notebooks are full of the Christian theme, "a volcano pouring... murky lava with... the sparkle of a jewel". Genius and madness can't be neatly separated there.

## 2. What Nietzsche Saw That the Positivists Didn't

- The anthropologists of 1850–1914 (and Freud in *Totem and Taboo*) noticed that religions everywhere centre on a **collective murder** of a god or victim, often by a crowd. Dionysus torn apart by the Titans, Osiris, and so on.
- The positivist conclusion: the Passion is one more example, so **all religions are basically alike**. That is still the popular cliché.
- **N. refused that conclusion** because "facts" mean nothing until interpreted. He shared the comparative insight and drew the *opposite* conclusion: the Christian difference is real.
- *Will to Power* 1052, "The two types: Dionysus and the Crucified":
  - The difference is "not a difference in regard to their martyrdom—it is a difference in the meaning of it."
  - For Dionysus, life "creates torment, destruction, the will to annihilation", and being is holy enough to justify it. "Dionysus cut to pieces is a promise of life."
  - For the Crucified, suffering, "the 'Crucified as the innocent one'", counts as "an objection to this life, as a formula for its condemnation".
- **Girard's key observation**: in the Titan episode Dionysus is the victim. But in almost all other Dionysus stories he is the **instigator of the lynching**: someone is torn apart (*sparagmos*) by a frenzied crowd (Pentheus in the *Bacchae*). The god can be victim or victimizer. Myth is indifferent to the victim's innocence.
- Jesus can never become the instigator of a "holy lynching". When a crowd gathers to stone the woman taken in adultery, he disperses it: "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her" (John 8:7).
- (The Orphic reading of Dionysus's death as a symbol of human guilt is late, probably under biblical influence, and isn't N.'s Dionysus.)

## 3. Two Types of Religion

- **Pagan**: says yes to the violence that founds and renews the community, "beyond good and evil". It accepts the worst along with the best. N. never made Dionysus idyllic; he always kept the violence in, even if only in stock phrases.
- **Biblical**: takes the **victim's side**. It reveals that the victim is innocent and the crowd guilty.
- Girard's twist: the usual complaint is that Christianity *encourages* suffering. N. complains that it **rejects** suffering, i.e. rejects the sacrificial violence on which all societies "worth their salt" were built.
- What N. saw: the Passion is the *same* collective murder as all the others, and that is exactly why it condemns them all. It reveals the "absurdity" (N.'s word) of every Dionysian mob. So it isn't anti-Jewish; it is **anti-pagan**. And since all human culture rests on that violence, the Gospel finds the whole human race guilty. To N., that is "slander" of life.
- **Cain vs Romulus.** Same data: a brother kills a brother and a city is founded. Rome glorifies it. Genesis calls it a crime. N.'s project is to **rehabilitate Cain**.
- From the Gospel's side, Cain, Romulus and Dionysus all get one name: not a rival god but Satan, "a murderer from the beginning" (John 8:44). *Satan* means the accuser, and the *Paraclete* means the advocate for the defence, who makes martyrs witnesses to the truth.
- The Gospels link Jesus to all earlier victims: "from the blood of righteous Abel unto the blood of Zacharias" (Matt 23:35). The Passion repeats the founding murder, but this time in full light, which spells the end of "the prince of this world".
- **What Girard values in N.**: he refused the "bland eclecticism" that dissolves sharp differences. He's an antidote both to making mythology into a Bible (the Jungians) and to dissolving the Bible into mythology (more or less everyone else).

## 4. Where Nietzsche Goes Wrong: *Ressentiment*

- N.'s **value judgement** is untenable, and pious attempts to excuse him are misguided. Siding with the persecutors against the Bible's defence of victims led him, in words, to more and more inhuman positions.
- **Ressentiment is interiorized, weakened vengeance.** N. sees it as Christianity's **father**. Girard says it's Christianity's **child**:
  - the Gospels target vengeance in all forms;
  - they have managed to wound it, not kill it;
  - *ressentiment* is how the spirit of vengeance survives the Gospel and turns it to its own use.
- So N. was fighting a relatively **minor** evil. Living in a quiet, post-Christian century, he could afford to find *ressentiment* worse than real vengeance, and he called on Dionysus to bring real vengeance back as a cure.
- **"Sincere prayers are never in vain."** Real vengeance has returned as nuclear weapons, making the planet a "global primitive village" under threat of unlimited blood-feud. N. resembles the fairy-tale character who makes the wrong wish. (Our missiles have mythological names, Poseidon, Pluto, Ariadne.)
- **"Versus" became "and".** In his final collapse N. signed his letters "Dionysus" and "The Crucified". For Girard, the difference N. had fought for collapsed back into the undifferentiation it came from. He thinks the strain of glorifying the violent sacred is what broke him. The forces were almost evenly balanced: "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (Heb 10:31).
- N.'s strange achievement: he "put his finger on that 'sword'" Christ brings ("I came not to send peace, but a sword", Matt 10:34), the force that destroys the old sacred by revealing its violence. Christian thinkers, Girard says, never dared to go that far.

## 5. The Madman: Murder, Not Death (*Gay Science* 125)

- Everyone quotes this as *the* "God is dead" text, but its novelty is that it replaces natural death with **murder**: "We have killed him—you and I. All of us are his murderers... What was holiest and most powerful... has bled to death under our knives. Who will wipe this blood off us?... What festivals of atonement, what sacred games shall we have to invent?"
- The usual "death of God" (Jean-Paul, Hugo, everyone since) is a peaceful death of old age as mankind grows up. N. rejects exactly that.
- "How can you kill a God who doesn't exist?" is the naïve reader's question, and the best one. In Girard's theory gods begin to exist *by being murdered*: the victim becomes sacred after the killing. The new festivals and games will be rituals that re-enact the murder. **The death of God is also his birth.**
- **Three levels** in the text:
  1. modern unbelief as a collective murder;
  2. the founding murders behind all pagan gods;
  3. hardest of all, the Passion, which can't be the death of the Christian God (if murder makes gods, this one reveals the mechanism) but could be the death of all the others.
- **Heidegger's reading** ("Nietzsche's Word: 'God Is Dead'") turns it into "the end of the supra-sensible in the Platonic sense". The murder disappears, buried under "philosophical pedantry". Heidegger's hoped-for "new god" is 19th-century historicism in new words.
- The text is enshrined while *Totem and Taboo*, which says the same thing about a collective murder at the origin of religion, is excommunicated. Both reactions avoid the same subject.
- The madman: "This deed is still more distant from them than the most distant stars—and yet they have done it themselves."

## 6. Reginster's Reading for Comparison

- Reginster takes 1052 at face value: the antithesis is about **the meaning of suffering**. The tragic man affirms even the harshest suffering; the Christian makes it an argument against life. This fits Reginster's account of N.'s revaluation of suffering. See [[Nietzsche on Nihilism and Its Overcoming]] sec. 8.
- Reginster also notes how the *Genealogy* refines the noble/slave split: the priests are a noble subgroup competing with the warriors for power. Unable to win, their *ressentiment* turns creative.
- The difference from Girard: for Reginster, "suffering" means the resistance any striving meets. For Girard, it means **victims**: the people a society sacrifices. Read 1052 Girard's way and "affirming suffering" means affirming the crowd.

## 7. Girard and Maistre

These read the same facts (sacrifice is universal) in three ways.

| | universal sacrifice is... | the Cross is... | the crowd's violence is... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Maistre** | a true principle ("the innocent can pay for the guilty"), corrupted into human sacrifice | the fulfilment of the sacrificial system; "heaven... could only be appeased by blood" | part of a world-altar; "war is divine in itself" |
| **Nietzsche** | the healthy religion of life | an indictment of life | to be affirmed |
| **Girard** | the trace of a founding murder, hidden by myth | the revelation that unmasks the mechanism | Satan's work, exposed |

- **Maistre and N. are oddly close.** Both see blood as the hidden law of the world. Maistre's "the entire earth, perpetually steeped in blood, is nothing but an immense altar" could almost be N.'s Dionysus. Maistre baptizes the sacrificial order; N. defends it against Christianity.
- Girard says the Gospel does something different: it doesn't continue the sacrificial order but brings it to an end by showing what it is.
- See [[Joseph de Maistre on Providence and Suffering]] sec. 8 and 10.

## 8. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's convincing**
- N. and Girard together are a strong argument against "all religions are the same". The Gospels tell the lynching from the victim's side, and that is unusual.
- It fits Scripture well:
  - Caiaphas states the scapegoat logic exactly: "it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not" (John 11:50).
  - Paul on the powers: "having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it" (Col 2:15); "had they known it, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (1 Cor 2:8).
  - That fits the patristic theme of the devil deceived by the Cross (Gregory of Nyssa's fish-hook).
- It explains why Christians, at their best, side with victims: why a post-Christian world, even an anti-Christian one, judges everything by concern for victims.
- The point about *ressentiment* is good. It is a Christian vice, a corruption of a Christian insight, not its source.

**Where to be careful**
- **Sacrifice.** Early Girard (*Things Hidden*, 1978) wanted to remove sacrificial language from the Cross altogether and criticised Hebrews; he later softened this. The Orthodox position keeps both:
  - the Cross exposes and ends the old sacrifices;
  - Christ is truly a sacrifice, but a **self-offering**, not a victim seized by the crowd: "For thou art he that offereth and is offered" (the priest's prayer at the Cherubic Hymn, Liturgy of St John Chrysostom).
  - The difference from Maistre: no wrathful Father appeased by blood. See [[Recapitulation Doctrine vs PSA]], [[Why Jesus' Death and Resurrection Had to Be So Violent]].
- **The Cross is more than revelation.** Exposing the mechanism isn't the whole of salvation. Death itself has to be destroyed, and that takes the Resurrection: "by death He trampled down death". Girard's account on its own risks making Christ a great teacher of anthropology.
- **Mimetic theory as total explanation.** Girard's founding-murder thesis is a bold hypothesis about all of culture, and it hasn't won over anthropologists. The reading of N. doesn't depend on the whole theory, though: 1052 really does say "not a difference in regard to their martyrdom".
