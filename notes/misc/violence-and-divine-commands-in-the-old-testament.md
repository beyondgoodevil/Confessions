When skeptics want to show that the Bible approves of cruelty, they reach for the same few passages: the command to wipe out the Canaanites, the Flood, the death of Egypt's firstborn, the psalm that blesses whoever smashes Babylon's babies against the rock, and the stories of Jephthah's daughter and the Levite's concubine in Judges. Christian apologists usually answer that these verses are being "taken out of context." This note tests that claim against the commentaries. For each passage it states the objection as strongly as a skeptic would, then sets out what the commentators actually say, including where they concede the point. The commentators are mostly evangelical (Wright, Alexander, Hamilton, Steinmann, Kidner, Wilcock, Guthrie), with a mainline Lutheran (Fretheim), a critical scholar (Hendel), a symbolic reader (Pageau) and the Church Fathers. The question of what these texts say about God's own character, his "regret," his hardening of Pharaoh, and his creating "evil," is taken up in [[God's Character in the Hard Texts: Evil, Hardening, Inherited Guilt, Divine Regret, Isaac and Job]].

### 1. The Conquest of Canaan: "You Must Destroy Them Totally"

**The objection.** Deuteronomy tells Israel to "destroy them totally" and "show them no mercy" (7:2), and in the cities of Canaan to "not leave alive anything that breathes" (20:16). This looks like divinely commanded genocide, and it is hard to square with a God who is love.

Christopher Wright does not soften the problem. Deuteronomy 7 is built in concentric rings, and its "outer skin" is the command to exterminate the Canaanites. Yet at its centre (vv. 7–10) is God's love, so that "Divine love and grace stand at the core of a chapter commanding total destruction!" (on 7:1–26). He also blocks the easy way out of treating these commands as a primitive layer of the text. They rest on the same theological basis, Israel's holiness (Exod. 19:4–6), as the food laws and the neighbour-love of Leviticus 19. They are "too interwoven with precisely these fundamental threads of Israel's self-identity and theological affirmation to be dismissed as merely peripheral lapses into the barbaric" (on 7:6).

His answer has five strands.

**The word.** The verb *heḥerîm* had a technical sense. Wright thinks the common explanation, "devoting" things or people to God, "is probably not the best." He prefers "an absolute and irrevocable renouncing of things or persons, a refusal to take any gain or profit from them," so that persons "could be renounced without necessarily being destroyed." Otherwise the ban on treaties and intermarriage in the very next verse would be pointless, "since everyone should have been exterminated" (on 7:1–2). He adds that the wars explicitly fought as *ḥērem* "were in fact few" (additional note on 7:2).

**The rhetoric.** The language of total destruction is the stereotyped "idiom and vocabulary" of conquest. Joshua 10–11 describes the destruction of all that breathed, yet later in the same book we "discover that various lands were not conquered and certain groups of Canaanites were definitely still breathing, even if somewhat heavily from being put to forced labor" (additional note on 7:22, citing Josh. 13:13; 15:63; Judg. 1).

**Judgment, not favouritism.** Wright admits that it "does not ease the moral issue" to say that fewer people died than the texts envisage, since the texts still present total destruction as God's command. What does "transform the ethical landscape" is the declared wickedness of the Canaanites (on 9:4–6). Genesis 15:16 had already said that "the iniquity of the Amorites" was not yet full in Abraham's day. Moses forbids Israel to infer its own righteousness from its victory: "The wickedness of the Canaanites did not prove the righteousness of Israel." And God later did to Israel "exactly what he had done to the Canaanites for the same catalogue of wickedness." So the conquest "was not an arbitrary act of inter-ethnic violence, nor was it an act of divine favoritism toward Israel" (on 9:4–6).

**Restraint in the law of war.** Deuteronomy 20 is "preaching, not military briefing." For cities outside Canaan it requires an offer of peace first, limits killing to male combatants, protects women taken captive (21:10–14) and forbids cutting down fruit trees (20:19–20). It even sends home the newly married, the man with a new house and the fearful, which makes the chapter "actually antimilitaristic" (on 20:5–9). Wright sums up:

> Without a Geneva Convention, Deuteronomy advocates humane exemptions from combat; requires prior negotiation; prefers nonviolence; limits the treatment of subject populations; allows for execution of male combatants only; demands humane and dignified treatment of female captives; and insists on ecological restraint. (on 20:19–20)

**The New Testament.** Christ abolishes the distinction between Jew and Gentile on which Deuteronomy 7:6 rests. So "the idea of the people of God engaging in warfare against other allegedly 'pagan' nations is no longer an option." Wright stresses that this is "a theological argument based on the transformation of the nature of Israel in Jesus Christ, not merely on presumed higher Christian ethical standards or sentiments" (on 7:6).

T. Desmond Alexander gives three reasons for the policy: punishment of the nations' wickedness (Deut. 9:4–6), protection of Israel from idolatry (20:18), and a distinction "between dispossessing the nations and annihilating them" (p. 303). Following Weinfeld, he notes that earlier texts speak of "dispossessing" or "expelling" the Canaanites. The *ḥērem* is first applied to a nation when the Canaanite king of Arad attacks Israel (Num. 21:1–3), then to Sihon and Og, who also attacked first. A survey of Joshua "suggests that ḥērem is applied only to those who are actively hostile toward the Israelites," while Rahab and the Gibeonites are spared and the Israelite Achan is destroyed. Since Israel later suffered the same fate at the hands of Assyria and Babylon, "At no stage can God be accused of adopting double standards" (p. 304).

The two scholars differ on the word itself. Alexander takes *ḥērem* to mean "devoted thing(s)" and says that where it is applied "this involves the death of everyone, men, women, and children" (pp. 303–4). Wright doubts the "devoting" sense and prefers "renouncing." Both, however, narrow the scope of total destruction, one by restricting it to the actively hostile, the other by treating much of the language as rhetoric.

### 2. The Flood

**The objection.** God drowns the whole human race except one family, along with nearly every animal. What had the infants and the animals done? And why would an all-knowing God make a creation he then has to destroy?

Victor Hamilton points to the feature that sets the biblical Flood apart from its Mesopotamian parallels: "right at the beginning there is a clear-cut moral motivation behind sending the Flood" (on 6:5). The *Gilgamesh Epic* gives no real reason, and in it the god Ea later rebukes Enlil: "How could you, unreasoning, bring on the deluge?" In the *Atrahasis Epic* the gods send the flood because the noise of humanity keeps Enlil awake. Hamilton concludes that "in a system of thought where the gods are not necessarily morally superior to human beings," there is no record "of the fact that man is to be drowned because he is a rebel and a sinner." Genesis, by contrast, grounds the Flood in human evil that is total, "every scheme in man's imagination was nothing but evil," and chronic, "not just a spasmodic lapse."

Ronald Hendel calls Genesis 6:5 "a dark vision of human nature," and notes that even after the Flood "this harsh realism remains unchanged," since God accepts that "the designs of the human heart are evil from their youth" (8:21) (p. 285). The Flood is not presented as a cure for human evil.

Two further points bear on the objection.

**Delay before judgment.** Andrew Steinmann reads the "120 years" of 6:3 not as a new human lifespan, which the rest of Genesis does not bear out, but as "a grace period before God would pour out his wrath on humankind." God "postpones his judgment to allow for repentance," as he later does with Nineveh (on 6:3). Ephrem the Syrian had read the passage the same way: "in the years given to them for repentance they had increased their sins" (*Commentary on Genesis* 6.6.1, in Louth, p. 127).

**The animals.** The Fathers raised this question themselves. Augustine says the announcement that the animals will die is meant "to declare the magnitude of the coming disaster. There is no question here of punishing with death irrational animals as though they were guilty of sin" (*City of God* 15.25, in Louth, p. 128). Ambrose asks outright, "What transgression could the irrational creatures have ever committed?" His answer is that, having been made for humanity's sake, they shared humanity's end (*On Noah* 4.10, ibid.). Hamilton is blunter: the animals "contributed to the depravity in the world, or else they are innocent victims," since "a deluge, would of necessity kill all forms of life" (on 6:7).

Matthieu Pageau puts the hardest form of the question plainly: "Why did God create the world if only to destroy it?" Flooding "expresses contradiction on the part of God, especially when God is understood as omniscient," and blaming human free will "provides a way around the contradiction locally" but not "on the cosmic level" (p. 142). His own answer is symbolic. Scripture repeatedly treats a reversal, a "double negative," as "an ironic cause of renewal and resurrection" (p. 142), and the Flood is the cosmic instance of that pattern.

### 3. The Death of Egypt's Firstborn

**The objection.** God kills every firstborn child in Egypt, from Pharaoh's heir to the son of the prisoner in the dungeon (Exod. 12:29), for the stubbornness of one king.

Terence Fretheim is the commentator who feels this most sharply. "As hard as it is to say, the victims were primarily children: both boys and girls—whoever happened to be the firstborn in the family. It helps but little to say that there was no suffering." Whatever is said about judgment, "no reader can rejoice at the deaths of children. Their lives were snuffed out because of what adults had done." And he does not shift the blame: "The text does not back off from identifying the subject of this judgment" (on 12:29–32).

His interpretation has four points.

**Measure for measure.** Exodus 4:22–23 had already framed the plague: Israel is God's firstborn son, and Pharaoh will lose his if he will not let Israel go. Pharaoh had ordered every Hebrew boy drowned (1:16, 22), "a genocidal measure that would in time have killed off the people of Israel." So "the firstborn of Egypt suffer a fate comparable—it is not genocidal—to what Pharaoh had planned and begun to carry out."

**The means.** The text uses words for disease: *negaʿ* and *negep* ("plague"), *deber* ("pestilence") and the *mašḥît*, "the destroyer" (12:23). "It is best to think of a pestilence epidemic that kills quickly." The "all" should not be pressed literally; "its possible historical basis is that no household remained untouched."

**Creation gone wrong.** As with the other plagues, the emphasis on "all" portrays "an aspect of creation gone berserk. The moral order has 'boomeranged' in such a way that the order of nature (which includes epidemics) has become something it ought not be."

**A public claim.** Firstborn children were understood to belong to the gods. The plague is "a public statement as to God's claim over Egypt, God's authority over the Egyptian people rather than Pharaoh's" (12:12).

Fretheim even suggests a modern analogy, Pharaoh and Hitler, and American bombs that killed German children in their sleep, while admitting that "it seems almost blasphemous that God is the one who 'drops the bomb.'" Why Pharaoh's resistance made the plague inevitable, and what it means that God "hardened" his heart, is discussed in the companion note.

### 4. Psalm 137: "Happy Shall He Be Who Dashes Your Little Ones"

**The objection.** "Happy shall he be, that taketh and dasheth thy little ones against the stones" (137:9). The Bible calls a man blessed for killing babies. Skeptics cite this as the plainest proof that Scripture endorses atrocity.

The commentators agree on the starting facts. The speaker is not God but an exile. Derek Kidner writes that the psalm "needs no title to announce that its provenance was the Babylonian exile. Every line of it is alive with pain, whose intensity grows with each strophe to the appalling climax." He adds that dashing children to pieces "was a common enough sequel to a heathen victory," and that Babylon "had been in no mood for restraint at the fall of Jerusalem." Verse 8 ("who repays you as you have served us") tells the reader that verse 9 mirrors what Babylon had already done (Kidner, *Psalms 73–150*, on Ps. 137).

Beyond that the commentators take three lines.

**A plea in court.** Kidner and Michael Wilcock both stress that "Remember … against" (v. 7) is courtroom language. The psalmist is pleading before God the Judge and invoking the *lex talionis*, "an eye for an eye," which "applied to legal but not personal decisions" (Kidner). Three of the psalm's key verbs echo God's own sentence on Babylon in Jeremiah 51, so "The psalm is therefore a response to Scripture as well as to events" (Kidner). Wilcock adds that "savage though the psalmist's words may sound, he does leave the issue to the Judge," which is exactly Paul's instruction: "'It is mine to avenge; I will repay,' says the Lord" (Rom. 12:19).

**A scream that reveals.** In the introduction to his first volume, Kidner argues that "invective has its own rhetoric, in which horror may be piled on horror more to express the speaker's sense of outrage than to spell out the penalties he literally intends." These outbursts "have the shocking immediacy of a scream, to startle us into feeling something of the desperation which produced them" (*Psalms 1–72*, Introduction). But he will not let Christians pray them as they stand:

> To the question, Can a Christian use these cries for vengeance as his own? the short answer must surely be No; no more than he should echo the curses of Jeremiah or the protests of Job. (*Psalms 1–72*, Introduction)

Between the psalmists' day and ours, he writes, "stands the cross."

**The little ones as sins.** The Fathers read "the little ones" as sins in their infancy. Origen: "the little ones of Babylon (which signifies confusion) are those troublesome sinful thoughts that arise in the soul," and whoever dashes them "against the firm and solid strength of reason and truth" is "truly blessed" (*Against Celsus* 7.22, in Wesselschmidt, on 137:8–9). For Ambrose the rock is Christ, against whom "all corrupt and filthy thoughts" are broken (*Concerning Repentance* 2.11.106, ibid.). Jerome: "Blessed is the man who puts the knife instantly to sinful passion and smashes it against a rock! Now the Rock is Christ" (*Grace for Grace*, p. 577). Wilcock ends his chapter with C. S. Lewis's modern version of the same reading: the "infantile beginnings of small indulgences, small resentments" that must be killed while they are small.

Wilcock adds a historical detail that supports reading beyond the literal. The psalm quotes exiles in Babylon but was written after they had come home, and so after Babylon had already fallen to the Persians, who, "far from devastating the city," made it a regional centre. He asks whether the psalmist is "consciously looking beyond the Babylon of his day to some fearsome ongoing spiritual reality," the Babylon of Revelation 18. He is also honest about the text: "we have to face the fact that the psalmist did write the objectionable closing verses of 137, that the compilers of the book saw no reason to edit them out, and that generations of Hebrew worshippers were prepared to sing them" (on Ps. 137).

### 5. Jephthah's Daughter

**The objection.** Jephthah, on whom "the Spirit of the LORD came" (Judg. 11:29), vows to sacrifice whoever comes out of his house to meet him, and it is his only daughter. God does not intervene, and Hebrews 11:32 later lists Jephthah among the heroes of faith.

Michael Wilcock refuses every escape route (pp. 116–20).

- **Did he really mean a sacrifice?** "Yes." It would be "comforting to think" that the vow was commuted to lifelong virginity, but "he 'did … according to his vow' (11:39), and the vow was to offer a burnt offering" (p. 116).
- **Did he really mean the Lord?** "Yes." The Lord's presence is recognized "more than a dozen times in this chapter" (p. 117).
- **Was he a man of his primitive time?** No. "Earlier ages had already recoiled from it," as the laws against child sacrifice show (Lev. 18:21; Deut. 12:31; 18:10). Quoting Davis: "He is no early blob on some scale of moral evolution" (p. 118).

His judgment on the vow is that "the vow itself is appalling enough, and no-one expostulates. This is simply not acceptable" (p. 117). His resolution is a distinction. Jephthah heard one word of God, that a man must keep his promises, and failed to hear another, that human life is sacred, which should have overridden any foolish vow. Jesus made the same point when he told the Pharisees that a vow could not cancel a man's duty to his parents (Mark 7:9–13). So:

> What he did (the sacrifice of his daughter) is a thing all Scripture condemns; why he did it (in order to keep his word) is a thing all Scripture commends. (p. 120)

Wilcock also draws a warning about God's servants: "how the Lord uses his servants in public is not necessarily related to what he knows them to be in private" (p. 119). George Guthrie's comment on Hebrews 11:32 takes the same line: Jephthah led Israel to victory "in spite of his foolish, horrific vow."

### 6. The Levite's Concubine

**The objection.** In Judges 19 a Levite throws his concubine out to a mob, who rape her all night until she dies; he then cuts her body into twelve pieces and sends them round the tribes. The Bible tells the story without a word of comment.

Wilcock shows that the comment is in the frame. The book's last chapters are bracketed by the refrain "In those days there was no king in Israel; every man did what was right in his own eyes" (21:25), and their subject is "a morality adrift from its moorings" (p. 166). The story deliberately echoes Sodom (Gen. 19). What shocks the narrator is that the gang rape "was apparently expected, as part of the regular night-life of the town. And not among Jebusites and Canaanites, but among the people of God!" (p. 169). The Levite is no hero. His account to the assembled tribes is "heavily edited," so that "No-one would suspect from it that any folly or callousness of his own might have contributed to the death of the girl," and "the wronged Levite is steadily forfeiting our sympathy" (p. 170). The civil war that follows, the near-destruction of the tribe of Benjamin, is presented as a disproportionate response whipped up by "a corpse, a knife, and an unerring instinct for what the public relishes" (p. 170).

Judges is the clearest case of a book that records in order to condemn. To read its silences as approval confuses what the Bible reports with what it endorses.

### 8. Assessment

On these passages the better commentators do not claim that the skeptic has simply misread the text. Wright, Fretheim, Wilcock and Kidner all admit the force of the problem in plain words. Where they do find misreading, it is of three kinds.

The first is **genre**. Psalm 137 is a lament addressed to God as judge, not a command; Judges 19 is a narrative whose frame passes judgment on everything in it. Reading either as an instruction is a category mistake, and here the "out of context" reply is fully justified.

The second is **rhetoric**. The conquest texts use a stereotyped "all that breathes" language that the same books then qualify. A strictly literal reading of Deuteronomy 7 overreads it, as Wright and Alexander show from Joshua and Judges themselves.

The third is **context**. The Flood is grounded in human evil where its Near Eastern parallels give none; the death of the firstborn mirrors Pharaoh's own decree; Deuteronomy 20 is more restrained than the warfare of Israel's neighbours.

What the commentators do not do is make the hard core disappear. Even on Wright's and Alexander's readings, *ḥērem* involved the killing of non-combatants in some cases, and their main justifications, God's judgment on wickedness and the protection of Israel's monotheism, are theological premises a skeptic will not grant. Alexander's "no double standards" shows that God was consistent, not that the punishment was just. The strongest Christian reply is therefore not "you misunderstood the verse" but Wright's argument that these commands belonged to a particular moment in the history of a national people of God, a moment the New Testament explicitly ends. The weakest is the patristic allegory of Psalm 137, which is spiritually fruitful but does not answer what the original singer meant. Kidner and Wilcock are right to hold the literal and the spiritual readings together rather than let the second hide the first.

Fretheim's chapter on the firstborn is the most honest piece in the set. He keeps the theological claim, that God judged Egypt, without pretending that the deaths of children can be made comfortable. That combination, conviction without evasion, is probably the most a believing commentator can offer on these texts, and it is more persuasive than any attempt to explain them away.

### Conclusion

The passages skeptics cite as proof that the Bible endorses atrocity fall into two groups. Psalm 137 and the stories of Judges are misread when taken as approval: the first is the cry of a victim handed to God as judge, the second a deliberate portrait of a society that had lost its way. The conquest, the Flood and the death of the firstborn are not misread in the same way. They really do present God as commanding or carrying out the deaths of many people, and the commentators answer with context, with the moral framing that sets them apart from their Near Eastern parallels, and with the claim that they belonged to a stage of history that Christ has closed. Whether that answer satisfies depends on whether one already grants that God has the right to judge nations. On that point the commentaries can clarify the question, but they cannot settle it.
