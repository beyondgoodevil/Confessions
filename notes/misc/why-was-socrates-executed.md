In 399 BC an Athenian jury of 501 citizens convicted **Socrates** of impiety and of corrupting the young, and he drank hemlock a month later, once the sacred ship had returned from Delos. The question is **why**. Athens had no creed to break, Socrates was neither rich nor a politician, and the city that killed him was the most open in Greece. The answers point in several directions at once: an old **comic caricature**, the trauma of the **Thirty Tyrants**, a city shaken by **war and plague**, and Socrates' own **defence**, which, as Plato wrote it, seems to have been built to provoke rather than to persuade.

### 1. The Charge and the Procedure

Finley gives the indictment as the clerk of the court read it out:

> "Socrates is guilty of not believing in the gods in which the polis believes and of introducing other, new divinities. He is also guilty of corrupting the young. The penalty proposed is death."

- **The procedure.** The case was a *graphē asebeias*, a public prosecution for impiety, brought by **Meletus** and backed by the politicians **Anytus** and **Lycon**. After a preliminary hearing before the king archon it went to a popular court. The trial took one day. Prosecution and defence spoke for set times, and the jurors voted without discussion. Finley gives the vote as **281 to 220**.
- **Choosing the penalty.** Impiety was an *agōn timētos*: the law fixed no penalty, so after conviction each side proposed one and the jury chose between them. Meletus asked for death; Socrates' counter-proposal is discussed in section 5.
- **"Not by a lynch mob."** Ober stresses that "the entire procedure was legal and orderly; Socrates was condemned by the ordinary processes of the Athenian state, not by a lynch mob." What was unusual was the defendant: public prosecutions were "used primarily by and against elite Athenians", and Socrates was neither wealthy nor politically prominent.
- **The paradox.** Finley puts the puzzle sharply. Greek religion was a matter of **ritual, not doctrine**, so sacrilege normally meant acts: robbing a temple, profaning a rite, revealing the Mysteries. "Where there is no orthodoxy there can be no heresy." Yet Socrates was condemned for "not believing", although "all sources agree that his piety was in fact unimpeachable."
- **The "new divinities".** In the *Euthyphro*, set at the court on the day Socrates learned of the charge, he reports that Meletus calls him "a maker of gods" and accuses him of creating "new gods while not believing in the old gods". Euthyphro at once guesses the reason: "This is because you say that the divine sign keeps coming to you" (3b). Socrates' private *daimonion*, the inner voice that warned him off actions, looked to outsiders like a private religion.

### 2. The Old Accusers: Aristophanes' *Clouds*

Socrates opened his defence by saying the real danger was not Meletus but an older slander that had "got hold of most of you from childhood":

> "that there is a man called Socrates, a wise man, a student of all things in the sky and below the earth, who makes the worse argument the stronger. Those who spread that rumor, gentlemen, are my dangerous accusers, for their hearers believe that those who study these things do not even believe in the gods." (*Apology* 18b–c)

He could name only one of them, "a writer of comedies", and pointed to "the comedy of Aristophanes, a Socrates swinging about there, saying he was walking on air" (19c).

- **The play.** Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BC, later revised) shows Socrates running a fee-charging school, the **Thinkery**, which teaches how "to win at speaking, whether right or wrong". He hangs in a basket ("I levitate and contemplate the sun", 225) and tells the old farmer Strepsiades, "What, Zeus! Don't be so absurd. Zeus doesn't exist" (367): the Clouds make the rain and a "celestial whirl" has replaced Zeus. At the end Strepsiades burns the school down, crying "Pursue them, hit them, pelt them, for many reasons, / But most of all because they wronged the gods!" (1508–9).
- **Whose ideas these were.** MacDowell shows that the Thinkery's science is real but borrowed. The meteorology comes from **Anaxagoras**, the theory that thought needs dry air from **Diogenes of Apollonia**, the "worse argument" from **Protagoras**, and the appeal to nature against law from **Antiphon**. Aristophanes, following Dover's explanation, needed one recognisable face for "the intellectuals". Most of them were foreigners, while Socrates was a lifelong Athenian with a comic face, always talking in public, so "Aristophanes therefore chose Socrates... and simply assigned to that character all the intellectual theories and activities which he wished to ridicule."
- **Not wholly false.** MacDowell modifies Dover's case. In the *Phaedo* (96a–c) Socrates admits he studied natural science when young. He did gather young men around him, and his method, refuting what people believed true, looked from outside exactly like what Strepsiades wanted to buy. "The Platonic Socrates refutes statements which are apparently true, whereas the Aristophanic Socrates refutes statements which are actually true." His verdict: the portrait is "a comic reflection of the man in a distorting mirror, not a picture of a quite different man."
- **Earlier victims.** Socrates' indictment came at the end of a run of cases. MacDowell and Finley both cite a decree moved by the diviner **Diopeithes** in the 430s (reported by Plutarch, *Pericles* 32) against those who did not recognise the gods or taught about "the things up above". It was aimed at Anaxagoras, Pericles' friend, who fled to Lampsacus. Protagoras too is said to have been condemned and his books burned. Both authors note the evidence is late and doubtful. Finley's judgment is that, "details apart, it is clear that Athens went through a bad patch of thirty or forty years' duration, when a number of intellectuals... were persecuted for their beliefs", and that this was "a purely Athenian phenomenon".
- **Socrates and Anaxagoras.** At his trial Meletus said Socrates called the sun a stone. Socrates answered, "do you think you are prosecuting Anaxagoras?", whose books "the young men... can buy from time to time for a drachma, at most" (26d–e). Finley notes that Plato "took pains" to separate the two men, although astronomy is not in the indictment.

### 3. The Shadow of the Thirty

The trial came four years after the bloodiest episode in Athenian history. In 404 the oligarchy of the **Thirty** killed about 1,500 citizens, according to the sources Krentz collects, before the democrats of Phyle and Piraeus restored the democracy in 403.

- **Socrates' friends.** Two of the Thirty were Plato's kinsmen: **Critias**, their leader, and **Charmides**. Finley adds that Charmides belonged to the Socratic circle and "Xenophon reports in detail how Socrates urged him to take up a political career." Krentz describes Critias as a pupil of Socrates, "an amateur among philosophers, and a philosopher among amateurs." Critias was a notorious admirer of Sparta, and in his satyr play *Sisyphus* a character argues that men invented the gods to control lawlessness. "Socrates was later charged with teaching Critias disrespect for Athenian laws and government." Krentz adds that this became a major charge in the pamphlet *Accusation of Socrates* by Polycrates (c. 393/2), which Xenophon's *Memorabilia* answers. Finley: "Many Athenians drew the conclusion that Socrates' teaching fostered this bloodiest chapter in the history of the Athenian democracy."
- **Leon of Salamis.** Socrates' own evidence points the other way. The Thirty ordered him and four others to arrest Leon of Salamis for execution; "the other four went to Salamis and brought in Leon, but I went home" (*Apology* 32c–d). Krentz identifies Leon as a former general who had helped the Samian democrats against oligarchs in 411. Plato's *Seventh Letter* makes the same point: the Thirty tried to make "an older friend of mine whom I should not hesitate to call the justest man of that time" a party to an illegal killing, "but he refused, risking the utmost danger" (324e–325a).
- **Krentz's cooler view.** Krentz accepts that Socrates fell out with Critias and records Xenophon's claim that the Thirty passed a law against teaching "the art of words" aimed at him. He cautions that "this might be invented apology". He also asks what the Leon story actually shows: "he did not try to prevent others from arresting Leon, nor did he endeavor to warn Leon himself. He went home." Even so, "Socrates must have been unusual in opposing the government even this much"; most Athenians outside the privileged 3,000 "acquiesced for the most part, or ran away."
- **Anytus.** The leading prosecutor was no fanatic. Krentz places him among the moderates, with Archinus and Phormisius, who wanted the "ancestral constitution", refused to join the Thirty and then "participated in the resistance against the oligarchy". The man who brought Socrates to court had fought for the restored democracy.
- **The amnesty.** The settlement of 403 included an amnesty: apart from the Thirty and a few officials, no one was to be prosecuted for what had been done under the oligarchy. Krentz shows that litigants found ways around it, for example by charging a killer with entering holy places rather than with the killing itself. Socrates' indictment names no political act. It is a modern inference, not the claim of any source used here, that an impiety charge let the Thirty's associations be put before a jury without breaking the amnesty. What the sources do show is that, four years on, the political memory was still raw.

### 4. Finley: War, Plague and the New Learning

Finley rejects the purely political explanation. If Socrates died for Critias, why were Anaxagoras (a friend of Pericles) and Protagoras (who "appears to have had democratic leanings") prosecuted? And why was Plato left alone, "who taught and wrote freely for forty years or more, with a passionate dislike for the whole Athenian way of life that was no secret to anyone"? "The simple political explanation is inconsistent with the rest of Athenian behaviour in this area. The ill was more complex."

- **A frightened city.** The war had "destroyed many lives and disrupted the lives of everyone else for years on end". The plagues of 430–426 killed perhaps a third of the population, and the doctors "had a rational explanation but no cure", so people turned to soothsayers and magicians. Athens had been defeated, stripped of its empire and "ground down by the Thirty Tyrants and a Spartan garrison."
- **The new sages.** In the old days a sage like Solon "expressed hopes and ideals widely shared in the community". Anaxagoras, the sophists and Socrates seemed instead to be "trying to tear down the accepted beliefs and values". Their pupils were "rich young men, members of the dining-clubs" that turned into "conspiratorial centres for oligarchic revolution". So "to many it looked as if philosophy, impiety and oligarchy were in close concert."
- **No witch-hunt.** Yet "the ingredients were therefore present for a wild popular panic, but... none really developed". There were outbursts against individuals, like the mutilation of the herms in 415, and private grudges: "Xenophon said, perhaps correctly, that Socrates was brought to trial for no better reason than the desire of Anytus... to avenge a personal insult." In the *Meno* (89e–95a) Plato shows Anytus angrily defending the city's great men against Socrates and warning him.
- **Only in Athens.** The persecution could not have happened in Sparta, which was closed to philosophers altogether. "Only Athens provided the necessary combination of conditions: popular sovereignty, a large and active group of vigorous original thinkers and the unique experiences brought about by the war."
- **The cock for Asclepius.** Finley adds a telling detail. The healing cult of **Asclepius** came to Athens because of the plague, and Sophocles housed the god's sacred snake. Socrates' last words were "Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius; make this offering to him and do not forget" (*Phaedo* 118a). The philosopher condemned for impiety died honouring the city's newest god.

### 5. Ober: A Defence That Turned the Rules Inside Out

Ober reads Plato's *Apology* as an expert performance that keeps raising the expectations of an Athenian jury and then overturning them. "Socrates' speech is a rhetorical masterpiece." But by its end he has shown "that his own political convictions are drastically at odds with popular ideology."

1. **The opening gambit.** Socrates begins with the standard plea of the quiet citizen unused to courts: "This is my first appearance in a lawcourt, at the age of seventy" (17d). Jurors "must have settled comfortably into their seats".
2. **Hoi polloi turned around.** He calls the jurors as character witnesses, a normal move, but then asks them to learn that "the other things said about me by the majority are of the same kind. Not one of them is true" (19d). Ober's point is that instead of saying public opinion clears him, Socrates tells the jurors that **public opinion is false**.
3. **The horse-trainer argument.** Under cross-examination Meletus says that "all the Athenians" improve the young and only Socrates corrupts them. Socrates answers that with horses, one man or very few improve them and "the majority, if they have horses and use them, corrupt them" (25b). Most jurors, Ober observes, would have agreed with Meletus. The argument "yoked" his opponent to the people.
4. **"Not Meletus or Anytus".** Socrates accepts that the general opinion of the citizens, not the prosecution, will condemn him: "This will be my undoing, if I am undone, not Meletus or Anytus but the slanders and envy of many people. This has destroyed many other good men and will, I think, continue to do so" (28a–b).
5. **The god over the city.** Even if acquitted on condition that he stop philosophising, "I will obey the god rather than you" (29d). His benefaction to the city is not money spent on public services, the usual claim, but his reading of the Delphic oracle as a command. Ober notes that oracles were "notoriously obscure", and that Socrates must interrupt himself to beg the jury not to shout him down (21a).
6. **The gadfly.** "I was attached to this city by the god... as upon a great and noble horse which was somewhat sluggish because of its size and needed to be stirred up by a kind of gadfly" (30e). For Ober this repeats the horse analogy: the people are a lazy beast; "popular ideology is no better than a state of sleep."
7. **Why no politics.** His divine sign kept him out of the Assembly: "no man will survive who genuinely opposes you or any other crowd... A man who really fights for justice must lead a private, not a public, life if he is to survive for even a short time" (31e–32a). His proofs are the trial of the **Arginusae generals**, when as a member of the presiding committee he alone opposed the illegal collective vote while "your shouts were egging them on", and Leon of Salamis. Xenophon confirms the first: when the Assembly bullied the presiding committee, they gave way, "all of them except one—Socrates son of Sophroniskos, who said he would do nothing except in accordance with the law" (*Hellenika* 1.7.15). Ober's comment is that setting the two episodes side by side **implied a moral equivalence between the democratic Assembly and the Thirty**.
8. **No weeping children.** He refuses the usual family tableau. It would be shameful for one "generally believed... in certain respects... superior to the majority of men" (34e–35a), and asking for pity would invite the jurors to break their oath. Democratic dignity, Ober says, was a collective good protected by the people's courts. Socratic dignity was "the self-willed determination of the one good man".

The verdict was close: "a switch of only thirty votes would have acquitted me" (36a). Ober thinks that "if Socrates had employed a more traditional rhetorical strategy... he would have been acquitted," and that he could have avoided death with a reasonable counter-penalty. Instead he proposed free meals in the Prytaneum (next section), then a fine of one mina, raised to thirty minas guaranteed by "Plato here... and Crito and Critobulus and Apollodorus" (38b). Ober points out that at least one of these wealthy guarantors was a relative of members of the Thirty. His summing-up: "Perhaps you think that I was convicted for lack of such words as might have convinced you... I was convicted because I lacked not words but boldness and shamelessness" (38d), and "I would much rather die after this kind of defense than live after making the other kind" (38e). For Ober the speech was "his last, best sting". The closing prophecy, that younger and harsher critics would follow (39c–d), was a summons to his followers.

### 6. The Olympic Victor's Free Meals

Socrates' counter-proposal is the most famous moment of the trial:

> "What is suitable for a poor benefactor who needs leisure to exhort you? Nothing is more suitable, gentlemen, than for such a man to be fed in the Prytaneum—much more suitable for him than for any one of you who has won a victory at Olympia with a pair or a team of horses. The Olympian victor makes you think yourself happy; I make you be happy. Besides, he does not need food, but I do." (36d–e)

- **The real honour.** Miller shows what Socrates was alluding to. A fifth-century Athenian decree (*IG* I³ 131) gave a returning victor at any of the four crown games "a free meal at state expense in the prytaneion... each day for the rest of his life", an extension of the victory banquet held in the Prytaneion at Olympia. Ober adds, citing Schmitt Pantel, that the honour was rare in the democracy and had "archaic, aristocratic, and religious associations".
- **Miller's reading.** Socrates names only winners of the **chariot races**, the sport of the rich, and says nothing of the running, wrestling and boxing events, whose victors also ate in the Prytaneion. "It seems to me that Sokrates, the poor democrat, is disassociating himself from the wealthy equestrian competitors and, by his silence, even identifying himself with the athlete in the gymnikos agon." Miller argues that the athletic contests, decided by objective results and run naked, were a school of equality: "All three—jury, athlete, and Sokrates—are democrats."
- **Ober's reading.** Ober hears the same words as "this arrogant refusal to treat the will of the court seriously". After it the fine "was unlikely to persuade many on the jury." The two readings need not exclude each other. Socrates may have been claiming a poor man's place among the city's benefactors while knowing that the claim itself would sound like contempt.

### 7. The *Crito*: Why He Did Not Escape

Friends could have bribed his way out of prison; Crito had it arranged and had friends ready in Thessaly. Socrates refused.

- **The principle.** "One should never do wrong in return, nor do any man harm, no matter what he may have done to you." He admits "only a few people hold this view" (49c–d). Ober, following Vlastos, notes how new this was. The common Greek ethic was to help friends and harm enemies.
- **The Laws speak.** Socrates imagines the Laws of Athens asking whether a city can survive "if the verdicts of its courts have no force but are nullified and set at naught by private individuals" (50b). They bore, nurtured and educated him. He is their "offspring and servant". His country "is to be honored more than your mother, your father, and all your ancestors", and he must "either persuade it or obey its orders" (51a–b). He agreed to this "not only in words but by your deeds" by staying seventy years. He never left the city "even to see a festival", yet he was "always saying" that Sparta and Crete were well governed (52b–e).
- **Escape as self-refutation.** If he fled, he would "strengthen the conviction of the jury that they passed the right sentence on you, for anyone who destroys the laws could easily be thought to corrupt the young" (53b–c). He ends: the words sound in him "as the Corybants seem to hear the music of their flutes", and he can hear nothing else (54d).
- **Ober's critique.** Ober finds the Laws' speech much weaker than it looks.
  - Socrates says a citizen must ignore the opinion of the many and obey the laws. But Athens' laws had just been **recodified by the democracy itself**. After 403, on the motion of Teisamenos, boards of lawgivers chosen by the Assembly revised them, and the Assembly ratified them, with a yearly vote on further revision. "The Laws of Athens that spoke to Socrates had very recently been inscribed on the orders of the Assembly... How, then, could Socrates possibly build a code of ethical behavior... on the basis of a fundamental distinction between the opinion of the many and the law?"
  - The Laws argue like the crowd. They appeal to shame, mockery, the peasant disguise and children raised as foreigners, exactly the considerations Socrates had just told Crito belong to "the majority of men" (48c).
  - Their claim that he never cared to learn other cities' laws but praised Sparta and Crete is the kind of careless slander the *Apology* attacked.
  - The Corybant image suggests Socrates is "to some degree in thrall to a rhetoric based ultimately on popular opinion". For Ober the *Apology* and *Crito* together lay down an "ethics of social criticism": criticise the city, never harm it, accept its procedures. But they leave the deeper questions open.

### 8. Plato's Answer: Philosophy Withdraws

The trial shaped what Plato did next.

- **The *Seventh Letter*.** Plato (or an early Academic writing in his name) says he meant to enter public life as soon as he came of age. The Thirty disgusted him. The restored democracy impressed him: "in general those who returned from exile acted with great restraint." Then "certain powerful persons brought into court this same friend Socrates, preferring against him a most shameless accusation, and one which he, of all men, least deserved" (325b–c). The longer he looked, "the more I realized how difficult it is to manage a city's affairs rightly." Ober reads the letter as an apology for Plato's absence from politics. It "discredits democracy's moral victory in 403 by equating the mistreatment of Socrates by democrats with the various crimes of Athenian oligarchs." For Ober, the "monstrous *ergon*" of the execution stands "between the might-have-been Plato (the philosophical politician) and the real Plato (the political philosopher)".
- **The *Gorgias*.** Here Socrates foresees his own trial and calls himself one of the few Athenians, perhaps the only one, to practise the true art of politics. Because he will not flatter, his position in court will be that of a doctor prosecuted by a pastry cook before a jury of children (521e–522a). Ober notes that this contradicts the *Apology*: here Socrates expects to have nothing he can say. Plato has concluded that Socratic speech cannot heal a democratic audience. The dialogue points onward to the *Republic*, where the critic moves out of "the polis-as-it-is" into a city founded in speech.
- **The Academy.** Plato did not follow Socrates into the agora. He "withdrew to the Academy" and talked seriously with a few chosen students, "most of them noncitizens", and "never had trouble with Athenian law." Finley adds that the hostile mood vanished almost at once. "Not later than 385" Plato had founded the Academy, and Athens became "the main Greek centre for philosophy" for nine centuries.
- **Finley's irony.** "Athens paid a savage penalty: the greatest Greek democracy won its greatest fame because it executed Socrates and nourished Plato, the most powerful and most radical anti-democratic moralist the world has ever known."

### 9. Assessment

- **Several causes, not one.** None of the explanations is enough alone.
  - *The religious explanation.* The charge was real but strange in a religion without doctrine. It drew its force from a generation of suspicion against "atheist" science, much of it built from Aristophanes' composite caricature.
  - *The political explanation.* Critias and Charmides were real, Anytus was a hero of the resistance, and the amnesty kept politics officially out of court. But Finley's objection stands: Athens prosecuted democratic intellectuals too, and left Plato alone for forty years.
  - *The social explanation.* Finley's frightened city explains why the climate turned. It does not explain why this man, at this moment, was convicted by only some sixty votes.
  - *The forensic explanation.* Ober's reading supplies what the others lack. By Plato's account the margin was small, and Socrates chose a defence that could only alienate the waverers.
- **Plato's Socrates, not a transcript.** All of this depends on Plato's *Apology*, written by a partisan who was present (34a, 38b) and who later turned the trial into the founding injustice of political philosophy. Ober is careful to say he is reading "Plato's initial take" on the problem. Xenophon wrote his own *Apology* and *Memorabilia*, and some scholars Ober cites (M. H. Hansen) prefer his more conventional Socrates as closer to the original. The indictment and the vote are the nearest thing we have to neutral facts. A reading that makes the speech a deliberate "last sting" is a reading of a literary work.
- **Democrat or critic?** Miller's "poor democrat" and Ober's "critic of popular rule" are both defensible from the same speech. Socrates served at Potidaea, Amphipolis and Delium "like anyone else" (28e), sat on the Council and talked with everyone, "citizen or stranger", rich and poor (23b, 33b). He also told the people that the many corrupt what the few improve. The tension is real, and it is why Popper could make Socrates a friend of the open society and Plato its enemy, while Strauss read both as wary critics who knew what democracy could do to philosophers.
- **Krentz's corrective.** Krentz's remark that Socrates "went home" is a useful check on hagiography. Refusing to do evil is not the same as resisting it. But Socrates' own principle, stated in the *Apology* and again in the *Crito*, was precisely to suffer injustice rather than commit it, not to fight for justice in public. On those terms his conduct under the Thirty was consistent.
- **Was the city wrong?** Ober's verdict on the law is the important one. The procedure was legal and the jurors were ordinary citizens. Socrates himself reminds them that the collective trial of the Arginusae generals, which he alone had opposed, was "illegal, as you all recognized later" (32b). The trial shows the weakness Plato saw in rule by the many: a court with no fixed definition of impiety could convict a man for his reputation. It also shows the democracy's strength. The city that killed Socrates made room within a decade for the Academy, whose founder spent his life arguing that it should not exist in its present form.

### Conclusion

Socrates was executed because several things converged in one courtroom. There was a long-standing image of him as a godless sophist, fixed by Aristophanes. There was the memory of Critias and the Thirty, whom many Athenians blamed on his teaching. There was a city battered by war, plague and civil strife and suspicious of the new learning. And there was a defence that, by Plato's account, refused every convention that might have saved him: it treated popular opinion as error, set Arginusae beside Leon of Salamis, and asked for an Olympic victor's meals as his penalty. The *Crito* then made his death a matter of principle, though Ober shows that the Laws' argument rests on more popular opinion than Socrates admits. Plato drew the lesson that philosophy could not heal the city as it was. The execution became the founding scandal of political philosophy, which, in Finley's words, made Athens famous for killing one philosopher and nourishing another.
