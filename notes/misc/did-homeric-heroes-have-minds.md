Achilles, insulted by Agamemnon before the army, reaches for his sword. In Lattimore's translation:

> within his shaggy breast the heart was divided two ways, pondering
> whether to draw from beside his thigh the sharp sword, driving
> away all those who stood between and kill the son of Atreus,
> or else to check the spleen within and keep down his anger.
> (*Iliad* 1.188–92)

Then Athena comes down, unseen by anyone else, takes him by the hair and tells him to stop. Is this a man making a decision, or a man having a decision made for him? The question carries a larger one. If Homer's characters have no word for "mind", speak to their own *thumos* as to another person, and attribute their worst actions to gods, did they think of themselves as unified agents responsible for what they do?

In the twentieth century the question was usually framed around two books. Bruno Snell's *The Discovery of the Mind* (1946) argued that Homer had no concept of a unified body or soul, only organs and impulses, and that true decision began with Aeschylus. E. R. Dodds's *The Greeks and the Irrational* (1951) called the gods' interference in human minds "psychic intervention" and explained it by the Homeric world being a "shame culture" that projected unacceptable impulses outside the self. This note does not cover what happens to the soul after death, which is the subject of [[Homer on Life and Death]]. It sets out five later answers to Snell and Dodds and one older dissent about a single speech.

### 1. Snell, Dodds and Fränkel: The Thesis

Russo and Simon summarise the inherited position fairly (pp. 42–44). Snell made two claims. If Homer has no word for something, he has no concept of it; there is no word for the living body, so no concept of the body. And Homeric vocabulary is concrete: there are no abstract words like "soul" or "mind", only *thumos*, *phrenes*, *kradiē*, *noos*, and these name both an organ and its activity. There is not even a general verb "to see", only words for peering, staring and glancing. Dodds added that mental states are typically represented as coming from outside, and that Homeric men converse with their *thumos* "almost as man to man". Hermann Fränkel described the Homeric self as "not encapsulated, but an open force field", whose parts can each stand for the whole. Kurt von Fritz showed that *noos* means the organ of planning, the capacity to plan and the plan itself, without distinction.

Gaskin states the strongest form of the claim. Homeric agents "do not make choices in clear self-awareness of what they are doing; choices are made for them rather than by them". Snell applied to them Descartes's description of an animal, *agitur, non agit*: it is acted upon, it does not act. Christian Voigt, Snell's follower, said they "do not really act… they react" (Gaskin, p. 147).

### 2. Russo and Simon: A Mind Made for Performance

Joseph Russo and Bennett Simon, a classicist and a psychiatrist, largely accept this description but change the explanation. They begin from two observations: Homer shows mental life as "common and publicly observable", not private, and he renders inner processes as "personified interchanges" — between a hero and a god, a hero and a horse, or a hero and his own *thumos* (p. 45). Athena's arrival in Book 1 does not merely start Achilles' thinking, as Dodds put it; "the whole of Achilles' state of mind is presented" through the exchange (p. 47). When Odysseus, alone in the sea off Scheria, "addressed his dauntless heart", the soliloquy is announced as a dialogue: "in Homer *no one* really carries on mental activity in isolation" (p. 47).

Their explanation is the oral tradition. A poet who composes in traditional formulas before an absorbed audience will favour "the communal, the private view to the public view of things", and the bard himself believes that his song comes from the Muse or from his teachers, not from himself (pp. 50–52). They set out the parallel in a table: the poem is made in an interchange among poet, audience and song; mental life is an interchange between persons. The poet "receives" his song from outside; mental activity is "initiated from outside the person". There is no distinction between the gift of song and the song; there is none between mental organ, activity and product (p. 53). The Homeric model of mind was "highly adaptive" for a society of constant close contact and continuity between generations (pp. 55–56). They qualify Dodds on one point: projection is not demonstrably more common in shame cultures, and since "virtually every kind of mental activity", however trivial, can be ascribed to an outside source, the device cannot be only a defence mechanism (p. 56). Drawing on Havelock, they suggest that Plato's quarrel with the poets was a quarrel with this communal model of mind, which philosophy had to replace with "more private, analytic" attitudes (p. 51).

They are careful about the limits. They do not claim that Homer had "no notion that mental activity is private"; only that the private is not what interests the poet (p. 48). And they treat the difference from Plato as "a difference in emphasis, not an absolute difference": like Freud's Rome, older ways of speaking survive beside newer ones, as when we still say someone "has guts" (p. 44).

### 3. Lesky: Two Faces of One Coin

Albin Lesky's 1961 essay begins where Snell and Dodds did, with the gods who fill warriors with *menos* or take away their wits, but then collects the passages on the other side. Odysseus, cut off in *Iliad* 11, deliberates and decides alone. Menelaus in Book 17 deliberates in the same situation using the same formulas and reaches the opposite decision, so the warrior code did not dictate the outcome (p. 172). At the decisive moment of the poem, the Embassy, Achilles answers Ajax:

> All that you have said seems spoken after my own mind.
> Yet still the heart in me swells up in anger, when I remember
> the disgrace that he wrought upon me before the Argives
> (9.645–47)

"What is played out before our eyes has only one stage, the soul of Achilles, who has knowledge and yet can do no otherwise than as his rage compels him. Not a word of the gods' being involved!" (p. 173). Hector's speech to his *thumos* before the duel is likewise "a true soliloquy", and that he then runs "shows only that the heroic world of Homer is populated by genuine human beings. That is why it is immortal" (pp. 173–74). Even the proem of the *Iliad*, read backwards, ends not in Zeus' plan but in Agamemnon's unforced decision to insult Chryses (pp. 174–75).

Lesky's main contribution is the cases in which human and divine causes cannot be separated. When Patroclus disobeys Achilles and charges the walls, the poet first calls him a fool, then says "Zeus' mind is always stronger than men's"; "Patroclus' infatuation is the will of Zeus" (pp. 176–77). Phoenix begs Achilles not to have such thoughts "and may no daimon turn you that way" (9.600–01). Lesky notes that Lattimore softens this into "let not the spirit within you turn you that way", which "is of course un-Homeric", since the *daimon* acts from outside (p. 178). Diomedes says that Achilles will fight again "whenever the time comes / that the heart in his body urges him to, and the god drives him" (9.702–03). "A human being's internal stimuli and divine intervention merge in the same action, which is motivated in both spheres" (p. 179). He compares German *mit Gott*. Phemius' plea in the *Odyssey* is the clearest case: "I taught myself though a God sowed in my spirit / every kind of song" (22.347–48, McCrorie). "What Phemius feels welling up from the depth of his being as he sings is at the same time the gift of the god" (p. 185).

Divine involvement never removes responsibility. Lesky shows that one and the same act can be described from either side, "the same coin with first this face shown and then that" (p. 195). Priam tells Helen, "I am not blaming you: to me the gods are blameworthy" (3.164), while she calls herself a bitch and wishes she had died (3.172–80, 6.344–58). Agamemnon in Book 9, in deep depression, admits, "I was mad, I myself will not deny it", acting "in the persuasion of my heart's evil" (9.116, 119). In Book 19, his dignity restored, he says: "I am not responsible / but Zeus is, and Destiny, and Erinys the mist-walking" (19.86–87). Yet in both speeches he pays compensation. Which face he shows "depends… on his situation at the time" (pp. 196–97). Pandarus breaks the truce because Athena persuades him; Troy pays for the perjury anyway. As in the *Odyssey*'s line about Epikaste, who "did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind", "the deed counts as such". Lesky sees the line running from here to Oedipus and to tragic guilt, which is "by no means necessarily subjective guilt" (pp. 198–200).

He rejects Dodds's "over-determination" and Kullmann's "pre-established harmony". He traces the Homeric idea to a common experience: everyone has felt that some act he must answer for did not entirely belong to him, "that 'something came over him'" (p. 200). He notes that the *Odyssey* tends to separate the spheres more ("either he had an idea, or a god bid him") and has gods give good ideas where Iliadic gods take wits away, but warns against reading this as rapid moral evolution (pp. 171–72, 189–92).

### 4. Gaskin: Real Decisions, Even Weakness of Will

Richard Gaskin's 1990 essay, revised for the Oxford Readings, is the most direct philosophical rebuttal. He identifies three precise forms of Snell's claim and rejects each.

**No word for the self.** "The concept of self is just the concept of whatever is referred to using" a personal pronoun or proper name, which Homer has (p. 149). When Odysseus in *Iliad* 11 "spoke to his own great-hearted spirit" and then asks, "Yet still, why does the heart within me debate on these things?" (11.403–07), the switch of speaker is not confusion: "What we have is simply a dialogue of the self with itself" (p. 150). The "lexical method" — no word, no concept — is false. Dodds himself described Patroclus' death as "overdetermined" without Homer having a word for it, and a modern speaker can call someone "switched off" without implying no one was inattentive before electricity (pp. 151–52). Gaskin uses Frege's distinction between referent and mode of presentation: *thumos* and "self" can refer to the same thing under different modes. "Homer's world contains selves" (p. 153).

**Decisions made by gods or forces.** Gods advise but do not compel: Athena tells Achilles she has come "to stay your anger—but will you obey me?" (1.207), and Aegisthus does disobey Hermes (*Odyssey* 1.37–43). More fundamentally, divine prompting does not remove responsibility; it is "consciously matched to an antecedently constituted human motivational array", and so subserves "not subvert[s] human autonomy" (pp. 155–56). As for the claim that choosing by norms is not real choosing: "action which is chosen for reasons is the only genuine sort of action we know", and an agent who acts on reasons he approves "thereby makes them his own" (pp. 157–58). Menelaus' deliberation in Book 17 shows sophisticated reasoning: he weighs the norm that only cowards retreat against the norm that only fools fight a god, and retreats without disgrace (pp. 159–60). When Thetis warns that his death will follow Hector's, Achilles answers, "I must die soon, then" (18.98, Lattimore; Greek *autika tethnaiēn*, "may I die at once"). That is a decision, whatever word for deciding is lacking (p. 161).

**No akrasia.** Snell argued that only an agent who can act against his own best judgement and know it is fully self-aware. Gaskin finds Homeric akrasia in three places. Helen first refuses Aphrodite, then follows her; first tells Paris to go back and fight, then tells him to stay, "a double instance of akrasia" (pp. 163–65). Achilles tells Ajax he is right but anger prevents him, fitting three of Aristotle's four marks of anger-akrasia (pp. 165–66). Hector, who knows he must face Achilles, wanders into a fantasy of negotiation that has no apodosis, then runs: "He dies as a man before his body dies" (pp. 166–67).

What, then, distinguishes Homer from tragedy? Not the self, Gaskin argues, but the world. Aeschylus' Agamemnon, Pelasgus and Orestes face dilemmas with grave moral claims on both sides; Euripides' Agamemnon in *Iphigenia at Aulis* faces one with no reason to prefer either, and becomes something like an existentialist agent. Homer's heroes "live in a world which repeatedly and generously serves them up preponderating reasons for action. So far from enslaving or doing away with the agent, such a world — this is the paradox of freedom — can alone set him free" (p. 169).

Douglas Cairns's introduction to the volume adds two refinements. Arbogast Schmitt argued that Lesky still concedes too much to Snell: divine power and human susceptibility are always separable, and the gods choose agents suited to them, like the "foolish" Pandarus (pp. 15–16). Cairns warns against both Lesky and Schmitt treating every passage as evidence of a psychological theory. Priam's "I am not blaming you" is a kind man's rhetoric to Helen, not heterodox theology; ascriptions of responsibility "are at the mercy of the speaker's intention in context" (pp. 16–17).

### 5. Page: Phoenix's Prayers and a Different Morality

Denys Page, in an appendix to *History and the Homeric Iliad*, took the opposite view from Lesky and Gaskin, and used it as evidence of multiple authorship. In the Embassy Phoenix tells an allegory:

> There are also the spirits of Prayer, the daughters of great Zeus, and they are lame
> of their feet, and wrinkled, and cast their eyes sidelong, who toil on their way
> left far behind by the spirit of Ruin
> (9.502–05)

If a man refuses the Prayers, they ask Zeus that "Ruin may overtake this man, that he be hurt, and punished" (9.512). Page argued that the words "when any man does wrong and transgresses" (9.501) and the idea of injury as punishment are "entirely foreign to the rest of the *Iliad*" (p. 301). Elsewhere, folly comes from the gods and "nobody ever supposed that Agamemnon or Achilles was to blame for what the gods have done to them"; Ajax himself says the gods put the implacable spirit in Achilles (9.636–37). He quoted Snell: "Homeric man does not possess the concept of will, … and therefore cannot possess the concept of free-will." Phoenix alone speaks "the familiar language of the new philosophy of Crime and Punishment". This poet "has at least partly turned Epic into Tragedy; he has inserted a layer of guilt-culture into the fabric of shame-culture" (pp. 302–03).

Lesky's and Gaskin's evidence tells against this. Agamemnon admits his own madness in Book 9 and pays; Phoenix says Achilles' anger had been blameless until now (9.523); Ajax says both that Achilles "has made savage the proud-hearted spirit" and that "the gods put in your breast a spirit not to be placated" (9.628–29, 636–37). Phoenix's speech states openly what the poem assumes elsewhere: refusing a fair settlement is a choice with consequences. Page's dichotomy depends on the Snell-Dodds framework that the others reject.

### 6. Mueller: Heroism as Knowledge Won Through Delusion

Martin Mueller's essay moves from psychology to the shape of the *Iliad*. Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus (12.322–28) shows that "heroism is thus knowledge issuing in action", and "heroic knowledge is primarily a knowledge of death" (p. 106). But Sarpedon's knowledge is a set of maxims that protect him; what happens to him confirms what he always knew (p. 108). Hector and Achilles reach a deeper knowledge only because they first lose it. "The knowledge of death comes to them as they return from a state of delusion: they acquire a knowledge that is born from deception" (p. 109).

Hector begins with Sarpedon's clarity: he tells Andromache that "there will come a day when sacred Ilion shall perish" (6.447–48). Outside the walls he becomes progressively deluded, boasting of immortality (8.538–41) and rejecting Polydamas (18.285–309). Athena's disguise as Deiphobus is "the last flickering of the self-delusion to which Hector has always been prone", not an arbitrary trick (p. 109). When he turns for a second spear and finds no one, "as a great hero he is granted the ultimate privilege of seeing things as they are" (p. 114). Mueller contrasts Pandarus, whom Athena also deceives: his delusion is "the vulgar error" of thinking he can break an oath, and he never learns he was deceived; Hector's error "is not of a moral but of an existential kind and concerns the limits of human power" (p. 116). Achilles' delusion shows in "the rhetoric of the unreal": the gifts "as many as the sand or the dust is", the promise to sail home tomorrow, the prayer that he and Patroclus alone might take Troy — each contradicted by events (pp. 116–18). The immediacy with which Hector's recognition becomes action is "one more demonstration of how deeply the Socratic paradox, the identity of knowing and doing the right thing, is rooted in the Greek mind" (p. 115). Apollo, the god who brings death in the poem, is already "the god of 'Know Thyself'" (p. 123).

Mueller's reading assumes what Gaskin argues for: heroes who can be deceived, see through the deception and act on what they then know.

### 7. Pucci: The Heart and the Belly

Pietro Pucci adds a contrast between the two poems. The *Iliad* compares Sarpedon to a hungry lion whom "his bold heart [*thumos agēnōr*] commands" to attack a fenced fold (12.299–300). The *Odyssey* uses the same simile for the naked Odysseus approaching Nausicaa, but "his belly [*gastēr*] commands him" (6.133). The heroic heart is replaced by "a bodily need, a subjective and ephemeral concern" (pp. 157–59). When Odysseus the beggar says that "the reckless belly" drives him to fight Irus, Telemachus replies as if quoting him, "if your spirit and your bold heart compel you" (*Odyssey* 18.53–62): the son translates the father's beggar language back into heroic language (pp. 161–64).

*Gastēr* in the sense of hunger appears only once in the *Iliad*, and it is Odysseus who uses it. In Book 19 Achilles refuses to let the army eat before avenging Patroclus; Odysseus insists that "there is no way the Achaians can mourn a dead man by denying / the belly" and that the living must remember food (19.225–31). Pucci reads this as the *Iliad* setting "the man of *gastēr* and the man of *thumos*" against each other, Achilles' ascetic mourning that mimes death against Odysseus' return to life (pp. 165–69). In the *Odyssey*, Odysseus tells the Phaeacians:

> Nothing is more hateful and doglike than hunger:
> it tells then forces a man to remember its own needs,
> however worn the man is, the longing he suffers,
> the way my heart still mourns.
> (*Odyssey* 7.216–19, McCrorie)

The belly here is "a lower *thumos*, a vital principle that forces upon men its irresistible needs" (p. 174). Pucci distinguishes "two poetics": hearts that remember griefs and drive heroes to a glorious death, and bellies that forget griefs and drive endless adventure (p. 175). When Odysseus calls the belly "a curse that hands out plenty of evil to mankind" (17.286–87), the phrasing echoes the opening of the *Iliad*, where Achilles' wrath is the accursed thing that "caused countless griefs". The *Odyssey* names the belly where the *Iliad* named the wrath (pp. 175–76).

Pucci's point concerns psychology only indirectly, but it complicates the picture of a single "Homeric model of mind". The two poems use the same vocabulary of inner organs to describe opposed ideals of what moves a man.

### 9. Assessment

The strongest arguments now favour the view that Homer's characters are unified, responsible agents who deliberate, decide, and sometimes act against their own judgement. Gaskin's case against the lexical method is decisive: a language without the word "self" can still present selves, and Homer's narrative could not work otherwise. Odysseus' and Menelaus' deliberations in the same situation with opposite outcomes, Achilles' reply to Ajax, Helen's resistance and surrender, and Hector's flight are as clear as any later cases.

The gods do not cancel this. Lesky's material shows that divine and human causation are not competitors in Homer. A god can put an impulse in a man's heart that is also his own, as with Phemius' self-taught song or Diomedes' "the heart urges him and the god drives him", and the man still answers for the act. Agamemnon blames Zeus, Destiny and the Erinys, yet everyone, including Agamemnon, expects him to pay, and he does. Page's idea that only Phoenix holds men responsible does not survive this evidence. Cairns's point is a necessary qualification: when a character blames a god, the poem is often showing tact, self-pity or self-justification, not a doctrine.

What remains of Snell's and Dodds's observations? Russo and Simon identify something real: Homer prefers to show the inner life outwardly, as speech to one's heart, a visit from a god, or a simile, rather than through private introspection, and the oral tradition plausibly explains this. The *Odyssey* separates human and divine motives more often than the *Iliad*, as both Lesky and Fränkel noticed. Gaskin locates the larger difference from tragedy correctly: it lies in the moral world, not in the self. Homeric heroes rarely face choices in which every option is wrong. Mueller and Pucci show that the poems explore states of mind through plot and imagery — Hector's deceptions, Achilles' unreal promises, the shift from heart to belly — more richly than a theory based on vocabulary can capture.

The answer to the question in the title is yes. The interesting differences lie in how the mind is depicted and what moral world it inhabits, not in whether it exists.

### Conclusion

Snell argued that Homer's heroes lacked a unified self and real choice, and Dodds that the gods' interventions were projections of a shame culture. The later readers surveyed here have largely reversed this. Russo and Simon explain the outward, dialogic depiction of mind by the conditions of oral performance. Lesky shows that divine and human causation are fused without removing human responsibility. Gaskin argues that Homeric heroes reason from norms, make genuine decisions and fall into akrasia. Mueller reads the *Iliad* as a story of heroes who learn the truth about death through their own delusions. Pucci shows the *Odyssey* replacing the heroic heart with the belly as the force behind human action. Page's attempt to isolate Phoenix's moral language as foreign to the poem is the weakest of these positions. Homer's characters think and choose; the poet shows their thinking as speech, divine visits and images rather than as private reflection.
