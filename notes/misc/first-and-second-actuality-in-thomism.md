The distinction between first and second actuality in Thomism is as follows:

- First Actuality refers to a being's *basic capacity or disposition*—its *form* or *potency made actual*. For example, a living human being is in *first actuality* by simply *having* the power to think or move.
    - A substance's **form** is first actuality: it makes the thing exist as the kind it is (Gorman).
- Second Actuality is the *exercise* of that capacity—when the human being actually *thinks* or *moves*.
    - A substance's **operations** are second actuality: the thing doing what its form is for (Gorman).
    - "**Agere sequitur esse**": action follows being. What a thing does flows from what it is.

Application to God in Thomism:

Thomism holds that God has no potency, only pure act (*actus purus*). Therefore, in God, there is no real distinction between first and second actuality—He is *always fully actual*, *always acting*, and *always being* His own essence and operation. God's intellect and will are not passive powers awaiting activation but are eternally in act.

Thus, unlike creatures who move from first to second actuality (e.g., from ability to action), God is always identical with His being, knowing, and willing—*He is His act of being itself* (*ipsum esse subsistens*).

- Creatures always have some unrealised second actuality; that's part of being finite. God has none: in Him being and acting are one (Gorman).
- Aristotle's own nearest thing: the "active" or productive intellect of *De Anima* III.5 is "separate and unaffected and unmixed, **being in its essence actuality**", and "it is not the case that sometimes it reasons and sometimes it does not". Whether this is the human intellect or the divine one has been argued over since antiquity; Bermúdez lays out both readings. In *Metaphysics* Λ the unmoved mover's only activity is thinking, and it thinks itself: "its thinking is a thinking of thinking" (1074b34).

## Three Levels: First Potentiality, First Actuality, Second Actuality

Aristotle distinguishes **three** levels (*De Anima* II.1 and II.5), with the example of knowledge:

| Level | Example | |
|---|---|---|
| **first potentiality** | a child who **can** learn grammar | can acquire the capacity |
| **first actuality** = second potentiality | someone who **knows** grammar but is asleep, or not using it | has the capacity, actualised as a disposition (*hexis*) |
| **second actuality** | someone **actually reading** or speaking grammatically now | exercises it |

- Aristotle's own wording (II.1): actuality "is spoken of in two ways, first as **knowledge** is and second as **contemplating** is". Bermúdez notes he uses a noun (*epistêmē*) for the first and a verb (*theôrein*) for the second: a **state** versus an **activity**.
- The terms shift between chapters, which is confusing. Bermúdez lines them up:

| *De Anima* II.1 | *De Anima* II.5 | example |
|---|---|---|
| first potentiality | potentiality | able to acquire knowledge |
| second potentiality | first actuality | has knowledge but isn't using it |
| actuality | second actuality | actually using it |

## The Soul as First Actuality

**The soul** is defined with this: "the **first actuality** of a natural body having life potentially" (*De Anima* II.1, 412a27). The soul is like knowledge possessed, not like knowledge used: a sleeping person still has a soul.

- Aristotle gives the definition in three formulations (Bermúdez's "official account"): the soul is (1) "substance as form of a natural body which has life in potentiality" (412a19–21); (2) "the first actuality of a natural body which has life in potentiality" (412a27–28); (3) "the first actuality of an **organic** natural body" (412b5–6). They're one account, not three. "First actuality" picks out the strand of "form" meant here.
- *Organikon* is a false friend: translated "organic" (Shields), "instrumental" (Reeve), "organized" (Ross), "furnished with organs" (Hicks).
- Choosing *first* actuality sets the agenda for the rest of the book. Books II–III go through the soul's **capacities** (nutrition, perception, imagination, intellect) and how each is **exercised**.
- So "it is unnecessary to inquire whether the soul and the body are one", any more than the wax and its shape (412b6). That doesn't mean they're identical; matter and form are different. The question just doesn't arise for form and its matter.
- Bermúdez compares the bare definition to defining "squaring" as "making a square equal to a given figure": true but empty until you know how it's done. To understand the soul you need the capacities and how they work.

Example: if the eye were an animal, **sight** would be its soul. The eye's first actuality is the power of sight; its second actuality is seeing.

## Two Kinds of "Change" (De Anima II.5)

- Going from first potentiality to first actuality (**learning**) is a real change, an alteration.
- Going from first to second actuality (**using knowledge you have**) is "not an alteration, or a different kind of alteration": it's progress **into itself**, the thing becoming more fully what it is.
    - Bermúdez: learning is a change from a state to its **contrary** (ignorance to knowledge), "a kind of destruction by a contrary" (417b2–3), though an odd one, since each state can only be defined by the other. Aristotle hedges: either the learner "should not be said to be affected, or there are two types of alteration", one toward privation and one "towards positive states and a thing's nature" (417b12–16).
    - Using knowledge is not a change to a contrary at all, because exercising it **doesn't remove** the capacity. Possessing it is the precondition of using it.
    - **Perception** has no first-potentiality stage: animals don't learn to be perceivers; they're born able. So perceiving is always the move from first to second actuality, not a qualitative change. That's the framework for the rest of the book.
    - A capacity is exercised when **enabling conditions** (being awake, light to see by) and **triggering conditions** (the opponent's move for a wrestler, the object for perception) are in place.
    - Not everything that looks like an exercise is one: the monkey at the typewriter isn't exercising a capacity to write plays. In ethics, the foolhardy only mimic courage (*Nicomachean Ethics* III). Habituation is how a capacity becomes "second nature".
- This is why Aristotle can say the highest activities (contemplation) are *energeia*, complete at every moment, not *kinēsis*, which aims at an end outside itself. (Seeing: at any moment you **have seen**. Building: you haven't built until the end.) See [[Aristotle East and West]].

## The Orthodox Angle

*Mine.*

- This is where Orthodox and Thomist part ways. Thomism: in God first and second actuality are **identical** (He is His own act). Palamas: God's **essence** and His **energies** (activities) are really distinct, without composition. The energies are like "second actuality" that doesn't add anything to God and doesn't make Him change, but isn't simply His essence either.
- Gregory of Nyssa's *dunamis* language fits here. See [[Differences Between Gregory’s Dunamis and Thomistic Pure Act]], [[God's Uncreated Energies]].
