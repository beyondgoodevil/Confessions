title: Reformer That Believed That the Father Damned the Son
tags: [theology, reformation, salvation]
summary: Five Reformers whose penal substitution teaching implies Christ bore the Father's wrath.
---

1. **Martin Luther**  
   Luther, especially in his commentary on Galatians, speaks of Christ becoming sin for us (based on 2 Corinthians 5:21) and bearing the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13). While he does not use the exact phrase "the Father damned the Son," his view that Christ bore the full weight of God's wrath on behalf of humanity leans toward this interpretation.
2. **John Calvin**  
   Calvin, in his *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, articulates the penal substitution theory. He teaches that Christ bore the "weight of God's vengeance" against sin. Though Calvin doesn't explicitly say the Father "damned" the Son, he describes Christ as experiencing the fullness of divine wrath, which some interpret as implying damnation in the sense of separation from God's favor.
3. **Ulrich Zwingli**  
   Zwingli supported penal substitution in his understanding of the atonement. He emphasized that Christ took on the punishment that was due to sinners. Like Luther and Calvin, Zwingli never used the term "damned," but his theology implies that Christ endured the wrath of God for humanity's sins.
4. **Theodore Beza**  
   Beza, a close follower of Calvin, further developed Calvin's penal substitutionary theory. He spoke of Christ bearing the divine punishment for sin, emphasizing the legal aspect of Christ's substitutionary role, though without explicitly saying the Father "damned" the Son.
5. **Heinrich Bullinger**  
   Bullinger, Zwingli's successor, also endorsed penal substitution. He viewed Christ as the one who endured God's punishment on behalf of humanity, though, like the others, he does not explicitly state that the Father "damned" the Son.
