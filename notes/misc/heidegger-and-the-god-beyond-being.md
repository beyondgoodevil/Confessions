Martin Heidegger accused Western philosophy of **forgetting Being** and of turning God into the **highest being**, a supreme object among other objects. He called this **"ontotheology"**. Orthodox thinkers have argued that this charge does not touch the Greek Fathers, because they never treated God as a being among beings at all, but as **beyond being**. Here's why:

### 1. Heidegger's Question

- In *Being and Time* (1927), Heidegger asks what it means **to be**. He distinguishes **Being** (the fact and manner of being) from **beings** (the things that are). He calls this the "**ontological difference**".
- He claims that from Plato onwards, philosophy has studied beings and asked which being is highest, while forgetting to ask about Being itself.

### 2. The Charge of Ontotheology

- In *Identity and Difference* (1957), Heidegger argues that metaphysics turns God into the **first cause** and **highest being**, the *causa sui* that explains everything else.
- His famous objection: "Man can neither pray nor sacrifice to this god. Before the *causa sui*, man can neither fall to his knees in awe nor can he play music and dance before this god."
- **Significance:** this is a criticism of the "God of the philosophers", a God who exists to complete an explanation rather than a God who is worshipped.

### 3. The Greek Fathers: God Beyond Being

1. **Plato's Precedent**: Plato already said that the Good is "**beyond being**" (*epekeina tēs ousias*, *Republic* 509b). The source of all being is not one more being (compare [[The Good in Plato]]).
2. **Dionysius the Areopagite**: God is **hyperousios**, "beyond being". In the *Mystical Theology*, God is beyond every affirmation and every negation, and is known in **unknowing**.
3. **St. Gregory Palamas**: God's **essence** is utterly beyond being and knowledge, while He makes Himself truly known and shared in His **uncreated energies** (see [[Essence-Energies Distinction]]).
4. **Apophatic Theology**: the way of negation keeps theology from treating God as an object. Every name for God is true but falls short.

- **Significance:** for the Fathers, God is neither a being alongside others nor Being in general. He is the **personal** source of all being, known not by explanation but by communion.

### 4. "I AM" as Personal

"And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.'" (Exodus 3:14)

- In the Septuagint this reads *ho ōn*, "**the One who is**". These letters are written in the halo of Christ on Orthodox icons.
- **Significance:** the God beyond being reveals Himself not as an abstract principle but as **someone**, a Person who speaks and is worshipped. This is the opposite of the god of ontotheology, before whom no one dances.

### 5. Modern Responses

1. **Christos Yannaras**: in *On the Absence and Unknowability of God: Heidegger and the Areopagite*, the Orthodox philosopher argues that Heidegger's critique hits Western scholastic theology, but that Dionysian apophaticism had already gone beyond it.
2. **Jean-Luc Marion**: the Catholic phenomenologist, in *God Without Being* (1982), accepts Heidegger's critique and argues that God should be thought first as **love** (gift), not as being.
3. **The Thomist Reply**: Thomists answer that Aquinas was never ontotheological either, because for him God is not a being but **Being itself subsisting** (*ipsum esse subsistens*). The question is whether that formula escapes the charge (see [[Thomism EXTENDED]] and [[The Neo-Platonic Proof]]).

### 6. Why It Matters

- **Apologetics**: arguments for God, such as [[The Unmoved Mover]], can make God sound like the last link in a chain of causes. Heidegger's critique is a warning to keep in mind that the conclusion of such an argument is not yet the living God.
- **Prayer**: the God of the Fathers is the God who is prayed to. Theology done in prayer, rather than as theory, is the Orthodox answer to ontotheology.

### Conclusion

Heidegger charged Western metaphysics with forgetting Being and reducing God to the highest being, a God to whom no one can pray. The Greek Fathers, following a line that runs from Plato's "Good beyond being" through Dionysius to Palamas, never made that move: for them God is beyond being, known apophatically, and yet revealed personally as "the One who is". Orthodox thinkers such as Yannaras have argued that the patristic tradition therefore already answers the problem Heidegger raised.
