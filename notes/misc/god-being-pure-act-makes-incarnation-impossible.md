## 1. Pure Act and Divine Immutability

- *Pure act* in classical Aristotelian terms implies a state of absolute perfection, unchanging, and utterly detached from any potentiality. If applied to God without nuance or relational context, it would suggest that God is so utterly "static" that He cannot engage dynamically with His creation.
- In the Incarnation, God the Son takes on human nature and enters into time, space, and history. This act of self-emptying (*kenosis*, Philippians 2:7) involves a relational dynamic: God interacts with His creation in a deeply personal way. If God were understood as pure act in a rigid, Aristotelian sense, it could imply that this relational engagement or entry into time would involve change in God, which classical theology rejects. Hence, holding to a concept of God as pure act without relational distinctions risks undermining the Incarnation's relational nature.

## 2. Potential Denial of Distinctions in God's Activities

- In Christian theology, God's activities are distinguished: His eternal being (*essence*) is unchanging, while His energies (manifestations of His will, such as creation, providence, and the Incarnation) interact with the created world. If God were conceived as pure act without such distinctions, it could lead to confusion about how God interacts with creation without undergoing change.
- The Incarnation is one such divine act where God interacts with creation in a profound way. Without the distinction between essence and energies, one might mistakenly think that the divine essence itself underwent change in the Incarnation, which theologically would compromise the unchangeable nature of God.

## 3. Undercutting God's Freedom

- If God were pure act in the sense that He can only ever do one thing—namely, be fully actualized—it could imply that His actions (like the Incarnation) were not freely chosen but instead necessary expressions of His being. This would undermine the doctrine of divine freedom, which teaches that God freely willed to become incarnate out of love for humanity. The Incarnation would then appear to be an inevitable outcome of God's "pure actuality" rather than a free and loving act.

## 4. Challenges to Divine Participation

- The Incarnation enables humans to participate in the divine nature (2 Peter 1:4). For participation to occur, there must be a distinction between God's uncreated nature and the created nature of humanity. If God were understood as pure act in a way that blurs this distinction, it would undermine the theology of *theosis* (deification) by making the relationship between God and humanity impossible to bridge. The uncreated and created would be collapsed into one undifferentiated being, which contradicts the Christian understanding of the Incarnation as the means of uniting humanity with God while maintaining their distinct natures.

## 5. Christ’s Humanity in the Incarnation

- The Incarnation teaches that Christ possesses both a fully divine and a fully human nature. If God were pure act in the Aristotelian sense, it might suggest that the divine nature of Christ would overwhelm or render irrelevant His human nature, since pure act would not seem capable of coexisting with the potentiality inherent in human nature. This would undermine the doctrine of Christ’s full humanity, which is essential for His role as the mediator between God and man.

## Conclusion

A rigid understanding of God as pure act (without the essential relational distinctions between God and creatures) risks making God so static and detached that the Incarnation becomes incomprehensible. It would imply that God cannot interact freely or relationally with creation, nor take on human nature without undergoing change, both of which are core to Christian theology. Therefore, God’s status as pure act must be understood in a nuanced way: God is fully actual and unchanging in His essence, but through His energies, He freely and lovingly interacts with His creation, making the Incarnation not only possible but a profound act of divine condescension and love.
