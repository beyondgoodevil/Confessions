## 1. Absence of a Fixed Canon

- **No Masoretic Text (MT):** The **Masoretic Text**, the basis for the modern Hebrew Bible, was not finalized until the 6th-10th centuries AD.
- **Septuagint (LXX):** The Greek translation of Hebrew scriptures (3rd–1st century BC) was widely used, particularly in Hellenistic Jewish communities. It included additional books (Deuterocanonicals) not found in the Hebrew Bible (e.g., Tobit, Judith, Maccabees).

## 2. Jewish Sects and Variations

- **Pharisees:** Emphasized oral traditions alongside the written Torah. Predecessors to Rabbinic Judaism.
- **Sadducees:** Conservative group focusing on the written Torah, rejecting the oral law.
- **Essenes:** Ascetic group with unique texts (e.g., Dead Sea Scrolls), not part of the mainstream Jewish canon.
- **Zealots:** Political movement against Roman occupation, focusing on freedom rather than religious texts.

## 3. Diverse Textual Traditions

- **Septuagint's Prevalence:** For many Jews, the **Septuagint** was the primary scripture, especially in the **Hellenistic Diaspora**.
- **Different Versions:** There were regional variations of the **Septuagint** (e.g., **Alexandrian** and **Palestinian** versions), leading to textual differences.

## 4. The Temple and Revolts

- **Destruction of the Second Temple (70 AD):** Led to a loss of central religious authority and a fragmentation of Jewish practice.
- **Jewish Revolts:** These revolts (e.g., Bar Kokhba, 132–136 AD) deepened the division within Jewish communities and further splintered Jewish religious traditions.

## 5. Emergence of Rabbinic Judaism

- After the Temple's destruction, **Rabbinic Judaism** began to take shape, based on the **oral Torah** (eventually compiled into the **Mishnah** and **Talmud**). However, it was not yet universally accepted, and regional differences persisted.

## 6. Christianity's Role

- **Early Christians** used the **Septuagint** for their scriptures, which included books not recognized in the later **Jewish canon**.
- **Christianity's Separation:** Christianity developed its own **New Testament** canon, distinct from Jewish traditions, reinforcing the divergence between Judaism and early Christianity.

## 7. Canonical Diversity in Early Judaism

- **Regional Differences:** Jewish communities, such as **Alexandria** and **Palestine**, used different sets of scriptures, leading to a **diverse canon**.
    - **Alexandrians** embraced a broader set of texts (including deuterocanonicals).
    - **Palestinian Jews** adhered to a more restricted canon.

## Conclusion

The first two centuries AD were marked by **religious diversity within Judaism**. The **Septuagint** was central for many, and there was no unified **Jewish canon**. Different sects and regions used various texts, and the process of canonization did not solidify until later centuries. Christianity's adoption of the Septuagint further emphasized the divisions between the emerging **Rabbinic Judaism** and **early Christian communities**.
