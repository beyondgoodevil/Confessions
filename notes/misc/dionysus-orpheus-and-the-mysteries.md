Nietzsche made Dionysus the god of frenzy and Apollo the god of clear form and measure (see [[Apollo and Dionysus: The Birth of Tragedy]]). The classical scholarship on Greek religion both confirms and complicates that picture. The key book is Dodds, *The Greeks and the Irrational* (1951), written explicitly against the "rationalist" picture of the Greeks.

## 1. Dodds's Starting Point

- **Homer** (ch. 1): Agamemnon says it wasn't him who took Briseis, it was *atē*, a god-sent blindness. Homeric man has no unified "soul". Sudden impulses, courage, bad ideas are "**psychic intervention**", put in by gods.
- **Shame to guilt** (ch. 2): Homer's is a **shame-culture**: what matters is public honour. By the Archaic Age it becomes a **guilt-culture**:
  - fear of divine envy (*phthonos*) at too much success, hubris as "the primal evil";
  - **pollution** (*miasma*) that spreads;
  - **inherited guilt**: the sins of the fathers fall on the children.
- Zeus becomes the god of justice, and with that comes anxiety: "There is no word for 'god-fearing' in the Iliad". Love of God is "missing from the older Greek vocabulary".
- "To explain origins is not to explain away values."

## 2. Plato's Four Divine Madnesses (Dodds ch. 3)

"Our greatest blessings come to us by way of madness... provided the madness is given us by divine gift" (Socrates in the *Phaedrus*).

| madness | patron | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| **prophetic** | Apollo | the Pythia, the Sibyl, Dodona: a god speaking through a medium |
| **telestic** (ritual) | Dionysus | collective, healing ecstasy through wine and dance |
| **poetic** | the Muses | the poet given what he could not know |
| **erotic** | Aphrodite and Eros | love as the soul's wings regrowing |

**Against Nietzsche and Rohde.** Rohde, following "his friend Nietzsche", held that Apollo's religion was "hostile to anything in the nature of ecstasy", and that prophetic madness came in with Dionysus. Dodds: wrong.
- Plato carefully distinguishes the **Apolline**, which is **mediumship**, aimed at knowledge, the rare gift of individuals, and the **Dionysiac**, which is **collective**, for its own sake or for healing, highly infectious.
- Their methods differ completely. Wine and dance have "no part whatever" in Apolline ecstasy.
- Ecstatic prophecy is old in Asia and attached to Apollo's oracles there (Claros, Branchidae, Patara). Prophecy and madness are probably linked in the Indo-European root (*mantis*, *mainomai*).

**The Pythia**
- The god "entered into her and used her vocal organs as if they were his own", hence the first-person oracles.
- **No vapours.** That theory is a Hellenistic invention; the French excavations found no chasm and no gas. Chewing laurel does nothing; one professor tried it "in the interests of science". The trance was self-induced, like a modern medium's.
- Plutarch reports a session that went wrong: the Pythia, unwilling, spoke in a hoarse voice "filled with a dumb and evil spirit", rushed screaming at the door and died a few days later.
- Why did the Greeks trust Delphi even after it backed Persia? "Because they could not do without believing in it." In a guilt-culture without "a Bible or a Church", Apollo was the reassuring voice that "knew the count of the sand grains".

**Dionysus as cure**
- Early Dionysiac ritual was **cathartic**. It gave an outlet to "infectious irrational impulses" that otherwise broke out as dancing mania.
- "Apollo promised security... Dionysus offered freedom: 'Forget the difference, and you will find the identity.'"
- He is ***Lusios***, "the Liberator", the god who lets you "**stop being yourself**" for a while. He is **democratic**, open even to slaves, while Apollo "moved only in the best society".
- His appeal grew as the individual emerged from the old family solidarity and "found the unfamiliar burden of individual responsibility hard to bear".
- The **Corybantes** used the same technique (Phrygian flute and drum) as therapy for anxiety. The patient's response to a particular god's tune was the diagnosis. Socrates may have taken part; Plato and Aristotle thought it worked.

## 3. Maenadism (Dodds appendix I)

- Were the maenads of the *Bacchae* real or a poet's fantasy? Real.
  - Diodorus: in many Greek cities, women's companies gathered every second year, girls carrying the thyrsus.
  - Plutarch tells of maenads stranded on Parnassus in a snowstorm who had to be rescued.
- Parallels with ecstatic religion elsewhere:
  - flute and drum, the "orgiastic instruments par excellence" (also used for Cybele and Rhea);
  - **the head thrown back** ("flinging his long hair to the sky"), which is recorded in medieval dancing mania and in modern ecstatics;
  - **snake-handling**: Dodds compares the Appalachian sects who take up serpents (Mark 16:18).
- ***Sparagmos* and *omophagia***, tearing animals apart and eating them raw, are in the *Bacchae* "with a gusto which the modern reader has difficulty in sharing". Done first to the Theban cattle and then to Pentheus.
- **Girard's reading** (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 5): the bacchanal is a staged **sacrificial crisis**. Differences dissolve (men and women, men and beasts) until the mob converges on one victim. Dionysus is the god who organizes the lynching "from afar". Euripides comes close to revealing the "naked violence". See [[Girard's Scapegoat Mechanism: Sacrifice, Myth and the Gospels]].

## 4. The Greek Shamans (Dodds ch. 5)

**What was new.** Not belief in survival (the Neolithic dead were fed), nor rewards and punishments (Eleusis promised favoured treatment already in the 7th century). What was new was **an occult self of divine origin, at odds with the body**. Earlier the *psyche* was simply the living self, "perfectly at home" in the body. That opposition "introduced into European culture... the interpretation we call puritanical".

**Where it came from.** Dodds follows Meuli: contact with **Scythian and Thracian shamanism** once the Black Sea was opened (7th century). A shaman goes through a retreat of solitude and fasting, then can leave his body in trance. His soul travels to the spirit world, and he becomes healer, seer and poet.

**The Greek series:**
- **Abaris** rides an arrow from the far North, needs no food, banishes plagues, serves the "Hyperborean Apollo".
- **Aristeas** leaves his body as a bird, is seen in two places at once, reappears years later.
- **Hermotimus** wanders in spirit while his body lies at home; his enemies burn the empty body.
- **Epimenides** sleeps 57 years in the cave of Zeus, fasts, purifies Athens, claims past lives. He is the Cretan quoted in Titus 1:12.
- **Pythagoras** remembers former lives, founds a religious order whose rule is shaped by lives to come, is identified with the Hyperborean Apollo.
- **Empedocles**: "I go about among you an immortal god, no longer a mortal". He claims to stay winds and raise the dead, and denounces marriage and meat. Dodds: "the last belated example" of the type, magician, naturalist, poet, healer at once, not yet split into separate functions.

**Orpheus himself** is probably "a mythical shaman or prototype of shamans": Thracian, a musician who charms animals, descends to the underworld to recover a soul, and lives on as a singing, oracular head.

**How puritanism came about:**
- Trance and dreams sharpen the soul/body split; the shaman's retreat becomes *askesis*.
- **Reincarnation** solved the archaic problem of divine justice. Once the individual is responsible only for his own acts, inherited guilt looks unjust. Punishment after death doesn't explain the suffering of the innocent. Rebirth does: no soul is innocent, all are paying for past lives.
- So the body becomes **prison** or **tomb** (*sōma sēma*). "Pleasure is in all circumstances bad; for we came here to be punished" (old Pythagorean catechism).
- **Purity rather than justice** becomes the way to salvation. Vegetarianism goes back less to transmigration than to "the ancient horror of spilt blood" ("shed no blood", the rule of Orpheus).
- "Puritanism not only originated in Greece, but was carried by a Greek mind to its extreme theoretical limit."

**Plato** (ch. 7) takes over the occult self as "daemon" (*Timaeus*). Late in life he wants the many kept healthy by a diet of edifying myths, "incantations".

## 5. Eliade's Correction

Eliade (*Shamanism* ch. 11) accepts Dodds's general direction but draws a line:
- **Bacchic ecstasy is not shamanic.** "The Dionysiac mystical current appears to have an entirely different structure." Possession and collective frenzy are not the shaman's lone flight.
- **The Greek figures that look shamanic are all Apolline**, and come "from the North, from the land of the Hyperboreans, from Apollo's country of origin": Abaris, Aristeas, Hermotimus, Epimenides. Delphi itself, though, is "nothing shamanic".
- **Orpheus**: the descent to bring back Eurydice, the healing, the music and animals, the oracular head (like the skulls of Yukagir shamans) all fit. But "nothing" in **Orphism** as a religion is shamanic, except perhaps the gold leaves that tell the dead the road to take, a sort of "book of the dead".
- **Empedocles**: Kahn's objection. He never leaves his body, rides an arrow or appears in two places.

So, against Nietzsche, the "irrational" belonged to **Apollo** as much as to Dionysus, but in a different form: individual ecstasy and flight on one side, collective possession on the other.

## 6. Orpheus and Orphism

**Guthrie's Orpheus** (1935, 2nd ed. 1952):
- Orphism was a **way of life**, not just a cult. "There are many who join in a Dionysiac orgy, but few who carry out all that an Orphic thought necessary for the attainment of union with the god."
- Three things: Orpheus was:
  - the revealer of mysteries (*teletai*);
  - the singer of the gods (*theologos*);
  - the preacher of a way of life (purity, no meat, white clothes, no burial in wool).
- **The gold leaves** (Thurii, Petelia): the soul tells Persephone "I too claim to be of your blessed race", "I have passed out of the sorrowful wheel", and is told "Happy and blessed one, thou shalt be god instead of mortal!"
- **The dark side**: Plato's *Republic* 364 describes travelling Orpheus-initiators selling purifications "according to [whose] recipes they make their sacrifices", which free the living and the dead from punishment. "Orphism at its worst."

**The sceptical turn** (Linforth, 1941):
- There was no Orphic **church** or **sect** in the Classical Age, only a "hubbub of books" (Plato) attributed to Orpheus.
- Dodds: "I have lost a great deal of knowledge". The old edifice is "a house of dreams", "the unconscious projection... of certain unsatisfied religious longings characteristic of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries".
- What remains certain, from some of these poems:
  - the body is the soul's prison;
  - vegetarianism;
  - sin's consequences can be ritually washed away;
  - probably transmigration.
- **Orphic and Pythagorean** can't be cleanly separated. Ion of Chios thought Pythagoras wrote under Orpheus's name.

**Meisner on the theogonies:**
- **Bricolage**: Orphic poems recombine traditional Hesiodic material with Near Eastern motifs that "stand out undigested", such as a cosmic egg and winged, many-headed gods.
- **The Derveni papyrus** (4th century BC), found half-burnt in a Macedonian tomb, is our oldest Orphic text. A commentator allegorizes an Orphic poem in which Zeus **swallows** the phallus of Ouranos (or Protogonos) and re-creates the world from inside himself.
- **The Rhapsodies**: Chronos produces the cosmic egg, out of which springs **Phanes** ("he who appears"), the first-born, double-sexed and winged. Zeus swallows Phanes and so contains all things: "Zeus the head, Zeus the middle, from Zeus are all things made".
- **The Neoplatonists** (Proclus, Damascius) read the Rhapsodies as a map of their metaphysics, and Christian apologists (Athenagoras, Clement) read them literally to mock pagan gods. There are three "Orphisms": the ritual specialists, the compilers of poems, and the late interpreters. See [[Neoplatonism: Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus]].

## 7. Dionysus and the Titans: Original Sin?

**The myth.** Zeus's son Dionysus (Zagreus) is to rule. The Titans, faces whitened with gypsum, distract the infant with toys (a mirror, knucklebones, a top, apples), tear him apart, boil, roast and eat him. Zeus blasts them with lightning, and from their soot comes the human race: Titanic by nature, but with a portion of Dionysus in it. Athena saves the heart, and Dionysus is reborn.

**The interpretation** that we have a Titanic, wicked nature and a divine, Dionysiac spark, and must purify the one to free the other, is found clearly only in **Olympiodorus** (6th century AD), a Neoplatonist.

**The debate:**
- **Wilamowitz and Linforth**: the anthropology is late, "an audacious conjecture" of Olympiodorus.
- **Edmonds**: the idea of an "Orphic original sin" is "a modern fabrication", read back through Christianity.
- **Dodds** cautiously defends an early date. The myth rests on archaic sparagmos ritual and the archaic belief in inherited guilt. Pindar (in Plato's *Meno*) speaks of souls paying "the penalty of an ancient grief".
- **Bernabé and Herrero**: the anthropology goes back to the Classical period, and what's inherited is "ancestral fault" in the traditional Greek sense, not a "proto-Christian" doctrine.
- **Meisner**: reads the dismemberment simply as an episode in the succession myth, Dionysus as the last king after Zeus.

**Why it matters.** The 19th-century picture of Orphism as a "Greek Christianity" (a suffering, dying and rising god; original sin; salvation by initiation) mostly came from reading late Neoplatonic sources early, which is just what Dodds warned about. Girard's remark that the Orphic guilt reading "must have occurred under the influence of the Bible" points the same way. See [[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]].

## 8. Eleusis

- The **Homeric Hymn to Demeter**: Hades carries off Persephone. Demeter in grief withholds the harvest and nurses a prince at Eleusis, trying to make him immortal by fire. Zeus compromises: Persephone spends part of the year below.
- The rites:
  - **Lesser Mysteries** at Agra in spring, a preparation;
  - **Greater Mysteries** in autumn: procession from Athens, fasting, drinking the *kykeon* (barley and mint), a night in the Telesterion, and a final "seeing" (*epopteia*).
- **Secrecy** held remarkably well. Pausanias: "whoever has seen an initiation at Eleusis, or read the writings called Orphic, knows what I mean", and says no more.
- **The promise** was a better lot after death, already in the Hymn: "Happy is he among men upon earth who has seen these mysteries".
- **Not doctrine.** Aristotle: initiates don't *learn* something but *undergo* something.
- **A cautionary source.** Wright's 1919 book is written for **Freemasons**. It opens by claiming there is "scarcely a single ceremony" in Masonry without its counterpart in the ancient mysteries, and that some Church Fathers boasted of being initiated. Its introduction adds that some say "the Mysteries died at last, only to live again in the ritual of the Church". This is the 19th-century esoteric theory of a continuous secret tradition. See [[Alchemy: Spiritual Art or Early Chemistry?]] for the same pattern with alchemy.

## 9. The Return of the Irrational (Dodds ch. 8, appendix II)

- In the Hellenistic and Roman period, rationalism lost its nerve. Astrology, magic, theurgy and mystery cults spread. Dodds's phrase is "**the fear of freedom**": a society that opens up every choice to the individual finds the freedom unbearable and flees back to fixed authority.
- **Theurgy**: the *Chaldean Oracles* ("Julian the Theurgist", 2nd century AD) and Iamblichus. Magic for raising the soul:
  - "sympathetic" stones and herbs placed inside statues to animate them;
  - mediums "young and rather simple";
  - apparitions of light.
- Plotinus never practised it. Proclus did.

## 10. Comparison: Three Kinds of Greek Ecstasy

| | **Apolline** | **Dionysiac** | **"shamanic"/Orphic** |
|---|---|---|---|
| who | chosen individuals (the Pythia) | everyone, women especially, slaves too | individual holy men and their followers |
| how | trance, possession, the god speaks in the first person | wine, dance, flute and drum, collective frenzy | fasting, retreat, purity, the soul leaving the body |
| aim | knowledge (oracle) | release, "stop being yourself", healing | release from the cycle of rebirth, becoming a god |
| view of the body | indifferent | celebrated, then torn | a prison or tomb |
| what it gave later thought | the idea of inspiration | catharsis, tragedy | soul/body dualism, asceticism, "puritanism" |

## 11. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's useful**
- Dodds is a corrective on two fronts:
  - against Enlightenment Greeks who were all reason;
  - against Nietzsche's tidy Apollo (reason) versus Dionysus (frenzy). Ecstasy was Apolline too.
- The **shame/guilt** shift and the **need for assurance** explain why oracles, purifications and mysteries thrived. These are real human needs that the Gospel speaks to: forgiveness that doesn't depend on rituals bought from travelling priests; "the hairs of your head are all numbered" (Matt 10:30), which Dodds himself sets next to Apollo's sand grains.
- The **sceptical turn** about Orphism is a lesson in method. Don't build a "pagan Christianity" out of late sources. The same is true of claims that the Eucharist or baptism were copied from the mysteries.

**Where the Fathers differ**
- **Body as prison** is exactly what Christianity rejects. The soul doesn't pre-exist or transmigrate, and the body isn't a tomb but will rise. Nyssa's *On the Soul and the Resurrection* and the condemnation of Origenist pre-existence (553) both stand against the Orphic-Pythagorean-Platonic line. See [[On the Soul and the Resurrection]], [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]].
- So Christian **asceticism** isn't Greek "puritanism". It isn't purifying a divine spark from a guilty body, but healing the whole person, body included, which is meant for transfiguration. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]], [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- **Ancestral fault vs original guilt.** The Orthodox teaching of **ancestral sin** (we inherit mortality and corruption, not Adam's personal guilt) is closer to Greek "ancestral fault" than to the Augustinian picture often read into the Titan myth. But in Christianity the remedy is a person, the New Adam, not a ritual purification. See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]].
- **Mystery and sacrament.** Christians used the word *mysterion* and the language of illumination and initiation, as the Fathers' mystagogical catecheses show. But the content is a historical event (Christ's death and resurrection), offered publicly to all, not secret for a few. The Orthodox approach to the Eucharist is "with fear of God and faith and love", not with a technique for inducing trance. See [[The Eucharist in Type and Fulfilment]].
- **Possession.** The Fathers never questioned that the Pythia was possessed, as Dodds notes; they disagreed about by whom. Compare the slave girl with "a spirit of divination" (literally a "python spirit") whom Paul delivers at Philippi (Acts 16:16–18).
