Book I of the *Republic* is a small Socratic dialogue that opens the most famous book in philosophy. Socrates asks what **justice** (*dikaiosunē*) is, and three people answer. **Cephalus**, a rich old man, thinks it is telling the truth and paying your debts. His son **Polemarchus** thinks it is helping friends and harming enemies. The sophist **Thrasymachus** says it is "the **advantage of the stronger**", a racket by which rulers exploit the ruled. Socrates refutes all three, but at the end admits he still does not know what justice is. The rest of the *Republic* starts from that failure. This note follows Book I in Bloom's translation, with the readings of Julia Annas, Roslyn Weiss and Devin Stauffer.

### 1. Going Down to the Piraeus

The first line is "I went down to the Piraeus yesterday with Glaucon, son of Ariston, to pray to the goddess" (327a). The goddess is the Thracian Bendis, whose festival was being held "for the first time" (327a, 354a). Socrates admires both the Athenian and the Thracian processions and starts for home.

Polemarchus' slave catches his cloak, and Polemarchus arrives with a crowd that includes Glaucon's brother **Adeimantus**:

> "Well, then," he said, "either prove stronger than these men or stay here." "Isn't there still one other possibility . . .," I said, "our persuading you that you must let us go?" "Could you really persuade," he said, "if we don't listen?" (327c)

This joking exchange sets up the whole book: force against persuasion, and the question of whether argument can work on people who will not listen. The bait is a torch race on horseback and an all-night festival with "many of the young men" (328a). They go to the house of **Cephalus**. There they find his other sons Lysias and Euthydemus, and also Thrasymachus of Chalcedon and Cleitophon (328b).

Weiss reports Corinne Sze's suggestion that the scene acts out the allegory of the Cave from Book VII. Socrates goes down from the city into the port, as the freed prisoner must go back down. Cephalus, who can no longer travel up to Athens, plays the chained prisoner (Weiss, ch. 1). Weiss herself is not fully persuaded.

### 2. Cephalus: Old Age, Wealth and Fear

Cephalus is very old. He has just made a sacrifice and wears a garland. He tells Socrates to visit more often, because "as the other pleasures, those connected with the body, wither away in me, the desires and pleasures that have to do with speeches grow the more" (328d). Asked what old age is like, he quotes **Sophocles**, who was asked whether he could still make love to a woman:

> "Silence, man," he said. "Most joyfully did I escape it, as though I had run away from a sort of frenzied and savage master." (329c)

Old age brings "great peace and freedom", Cephalus says. What makes it hard or easy is "not old age, Socrates, but the character of the human beings" (329d).

Socrates suggests that most people would say it is his wealth that makes old age easy. Cephalus half agrees, with the story of Themistocles. A man from tiny Seriphos told Themistocles he was famous only because he was an Athenian. Themistocles answered that he would not have been famous if he had been a Seriphian, but neither would the other man if he had been an Athenian (329e–330a). Wealth alone does not make old age easy, but it helps.

What, then, is the greatest good his wealth has brought him? Cephalus' answer is the most personal speech in Book I:

> When a man comes near to the realization that he will be making an end, fear and care enter him for things to which he gave no thought before. The tales told about what is in Hades—that the one who has done unjust deeds here must pay the penalty there—at which he laughed up to then, now make his soul twist and turn because he fears they might be true. (330d–e)

The guilty man "wakes from his sleep in a fright as children do". The man with a clear conscience has, in Pindar's words, "Sweet hope ... a nurse of his old age" (331a). Money helps a decent man avoid "cheating or lying to any man against one's will" and avoid dying "frightened because one owes some sacrifices to a god or money to a human being" (331b).

Socrates turns this into a definition. Is justice simply "the truth and giving back what a man has taken from another"? Take a friend who lent you weapons while sane and now asks for them back while mad. Surely you should not return them, or tell him the whole truth (331c). Cephalus agrees, hands the argument over to his son, and goes off, laughing, "to look after the sacrifices" (331d). He never comes back.

**Annas** is hard on Cephalus. He is a resident alien from Syracuse who has given up citizenship to make money. Plato's first readers knew that the family's wealth did not protect it: after Athens fell, Polemarchus was executed and Lysias exiled. She finds "malicious touches" in the portrait, and calls Cephalus "a limited and complacent man". His compliment to Socrates implies that philosophy is a hobby for when bodily pleasures fade. His morality is "entirely external, a matter of rules to follow and duties to perform", and his motive is fear of punishment (ch. 2). Socrates' counter-example shows a general point that recurs in the early dialogues: "you cannot say what a virtue is by giving a list of kinds of action", because the same action can be just in one case and unjust in another.

### 3. Polemarchus: Helping Friends and Harming Enemies

"Am I not the heir of what belongs to you?" asks Polemarchus, and he inherits the argument (331d). He quotes the poet **Simonides**: "it is just to give to each what is owed" (331e). Pressed, he explains that friends owe friends good and enemies owe enemies harm. So justice means "giving benefits and harms to friends and enemies" (332d). This was traditional Greek morality, and Socrates attacks it in four steps.
1. **Justice is useless.** Every art gives something to something: medicine gives drugs to bodies, cooking gives seasoning to meat. What does justice give, and where is it useful? In war, says Polemarchus, and in peace in partnerships, especially money matters. But for any use of money, an expert in horses or ships is a better partner. Justice turns out to be useful only when money is deposited and not used: "Is it when money is useless that justice is useful for it?" (333a–d). So justice "wouldn't be anything very serious, if it is useful for useless things" (333e).
2. **The just man is a thief.** The man who is clever at guarding something is also clever at stealing it. So the just man "has come to light as a kind of robber", like Homer's Autolycus, who surpassed everyone "in stealing and in swearing oaths" (334a–b). Polemarchus gives up: "I no longer know what I did mean. However, it is still my opinion that justice is helping friends and harming enemies" (334b).
3. **We make mistakes about friends.** People often take bad men for good and good for bad. On this view they would have to help bad people and harm good ones (334c–e). Polemarchus revises the definition: a friend is someone who both seems good and is good (335a).
4. **Harming makes people worse.** Socrates' decisive argument runs as follows (335b–d):
   - A horse that is harmed becomes worse as a horse, and a dog worse as a dog.
   - So a human being who is harmed becomes worse in human excellence, and justice is human excellence. So harming someone makes them more unjust.
   - But musicians cannot make people unmusical by music, and just men cannot make people unjust by justice.

   Conclusion: "it is never just to harm anyone" (335e).

Polemarchus is delighted and offers to be Socrates' partner in battle against anyone who says otherwise. Socrates ends with a sharp guess about where the saying "help friends and harm enemies" really comes from: "Periander, or Perdiccas, or Xerxes, or Ismenias the Theban, or some other rich man who has a high opinion of what he can do" (336a). These were tyrants and power-brokers.

**Annas** defends these arguments against the charge that they are sophistical. The first one depends on treating justice as a skill (*technē*), with an end it serves. Polemarchus never questions this. On his view justice really is trivial, because it is "not something that structures their lives as a whole" (ch. 2). The last argument is a moral landmark in its own right: it breaks with the ordinary Greek ethic of revenge that Polemarchus inherited.

### 4. Thrasymachus Bursts In

Thrasymachus had been trying to break in for some time. Now, "hunched up like a wild beast, he flung himself at us as if to tear us to pieces" (336b). He calls the whole discussion "nonsense" and demands that Socrates give his own answer, and not say "the needful, or the helpful, or the profitable, or the gainful, or the advantageous" (336c–d). Socrates says that "if I had not seen him before he saw me, I would have been speechless" (336d). This alludes to the Greek belief that a man seen first by a wolf loses his voice.

Socrates replies that they are not giving way to each other on purpose. If they were searching for gold they would never do that, and justice is "a thing more precious than a great deal of gold" (336e). Thrasymachus laughs:

> "Heracles! Here is that habitual irony of Socrates. I knew it, and I predicted to these fellows that you wouldn't be willing to answer, that you would be ironic and do anything rather than answer if someone asked you something." (337a)

Socrates answers with a parable. Suppose someone asked what twelve is but forbade the answers "twice six" or "three times four" (337b). Thrasymachus wants a fee. Socrates has no money, so Glaucon says "We shall all contribute for Socrates" (337d). Finally Thrasymachus states his view:

> I say that the just is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger. Well, why don't you praise me? But you won't be willing. (338c)

### 5. "The Advantage of the Stronger"

Socrates pretends to misunderstand. Surely he doesn't mean that because the wrestler Polydamas is stronger and beef is good for him, beef is just for all of us (338c–d)? Thrasymachus explains. Cities are ruled by tyrants, democracies or aristocracies, and "each ruling group sets down laws for its own advantage", which it then calls just for the ruled. "In every city the same thing is just, the advantage of the established ruling body" (338e–339a).

Socrates finds the first crack. Rulers make mistakes. Sometimes they command what is bad for themselves, and the ruled are supposed to obey. Then justice is sometimes what is *not* to the advantage of the stronger (339c–e). Cleitophon tries to rescue Thrasymachus by saying he meant what the stronger *believes* to be to his advantage (340b). Thrasymachus refuses the help. He says a ruler, "in precise speech", never makes mistakes. A doctor who errs is not a doctor at the moment he errs, and "the ruler, insofar as he is a ruler, does not make mistakes" (340d–341a).

Socrates then turns the precise sense against him. A doctor in the precise sense cares for the sick, not for money; a pilot rules sailors for their safety. Every art seeks the good of its object, not its own: medicine the good of the body, horsemanship the good of horses. So "there is no kind of knowledge that considers or commands the advantage of the stronger, but rather of what is weaker and ruled by it" (342c–d). Every ruler, insofar as he is a ruler, considers the good of the ruled (342e).

### 6. The Great Speech: Justice Is Someone Else's Good

Thrasymachus does not answer. He asks whether Socrates has a wet nurse, since she lets his nose run and has not taught him to "recognize sheep or shepherd" (343a). Shepherds fatten sheep for their own and their masters' good, not the sheep's. So do real rulers. Then he gives the speech that has made him famous:

> You are unaware that justice and the just are really someone else's good, the advantage of the man who is stronger and rules, and a personal harm to the man who obeys and serves. Injustice is the opposite, and it rules the truly simple and just. (343c)

He backs this up with examples (343d–344c):
- The just man always has less. He gets less when a partnership is dissolved, pays more tax on equal property, and neglects his own affairs while in office, angering his relatives because he will not bend the rules for them.
- The clearest case is the tyrant, who takes everything "by stealth and force ... not bit by bit, but all at once". A petty thief is called a temple robber or kidnapper. A tyrant who enslaves the whole city "gets called happy and blessed".
- So the motive of people who praise justice is fear: "it is not because they fear doing unjust deeds, but because they fear suffering them, that those who blame injustice do so" (344c).

Then he tries to leave, "just like a bathman, after having poured a great shower of speech into our ears" (344d). The others make him stay. Socrates says the stakes are not "a small matter" but "a course of life on the basis of which each of us would have the most profitable existence" (344e). Thrasymachus asks whether he should "give your soul a forced feeding" (345b).

**Annas** shows that Thrasymachus has moved. "Justice is the advantage of the stronger" and "justice is obeying the rulers" sound like **conventionalism**: justice is whatever the laws say. Cleitophon offers him that position, and he rejects it. His real view, in the great speech, is that justice is "another's good" (*allotrion agathon*). This is a "revisionary and immoralist" view that praises injustice (ch. 2). Annas contrasts him with Callicles in the *Gorgias*. Callicles splits justice into "natural" and "conventional", so he can call the tyrant naturally just. Thrasymachus will not do that. He calls the tyrant unjust and admires him anyway, which is why he gets tangled in moral vocabulary.

### 7. Who Wants to Rule? Wages and the Penalty

Socrates returns to the shepherd. A shepherd as shepherd cares for sheep; if he fattens them for sale, he is acting as a money-maker. He adds that "no one wishes to rule voluntarily", since people demand pay for it. That shows ruling benefits the ruled. Each art produces its own benefit: medicine produces health, and the separate "wage-earner's art" produces wages (345c–346d). So there must be pay for ruling, "either money, or honor, or a penalty if he should not rule" (347a).

Glaucon speaks for the first time and asks what the penalty is. Socrates explains that the best men do not want money or honour, so only a penalty will move them:

> The greatest of penalties is being ruled by a worse man if one is not willing to rule oneself. It is because they fear this, in my view, that decent men rule, when they do rule. (347c)

"If a city of good men came to be, there would be a fight over not ruling, just as there is now over ruling" (347d). This is the first glimpse of the philosopher-kings of Book VII, who must be "compelled" to go back down into the cave.

Weiss finds the contrast with the later books troubling. In Book I, she argues, the mark of the best ruler is devotion to those in his care, like the doctor and the pilot. By Book VII the rulers "regard ruling as an utterly distasteful enterprise". So Book I is a warning against taking the later picture at face value (ch. 1).

### 8. Socrates' Three Arguments Against Injustice

Socrates says the bigger issue is Thrasymachus' claim that the unjust life is better (347e). Glaucon chooses the just life, and they agree to argue by question and answer, not long speeches. Thrasymachus now says outright that justice is "very high-minded innocence" (*euētheia*, simple-mindedness) and injustice is "good counsel" (348c–d). Socrates notes that this is new. If he had called injustice profitable but shameful, they could argue on ordinary terms. But he has put injustice "in the camp of virtue and wisdom" (348e). Socrates then gives three arguments.
- **The pleonexia argument (349b–350c).**
  - The just man does not try to outdo or "get the better of" (*pleon echein*) other just men, only the unjust. The unjust man tries to outdo everyone.
  - An expert musician tuning a lyre does not try to outdo another expert, only the non-expert. The same goes for doctors. It is the ignorant man who tries to outdo both the expert and the non-expert.
  - So the just man is like the wise and good, and the unjust man like the ignorant and bad.

  At this point, Socrates says, Thrasymachus "dragged his feet and resisted, and he produced a wonderful quantity of sweat, for it was summer. And then I saw what I had not yet seen before—Thrasymachus blushing" (350d).
- **The strength argument (350d–352c).** Even a band of robbers cannot act together if they wrong each other. Injustice "produces factions, hatreds, and quarrels", and justice "unanimity and friendship" (351d). The same is true inside one man: injustice makes him "at faction and ... not of one mind with himself" (352a). Successful unjust groups are only "half bad", with "a certain justice in them" (352c).
- **The function argument (352d–354a).**
  - Each thing has a work (*ergon*), "what it alone can do, or can do more finely than other things": seeing for eyes, cutting vine shoots for a pruning knife. Each has a virtue that lets it do that work well.
  - The work of the soul is "managing, ruling, and deliberating", and above all "living".
  - Justice is the soul's virtue, so the just soul lives well, and "the just man is happy and the unjust man wretched" (353d–354a).

By the end Thrasymachus is just agreeing to keep the peace. He says "All right" like a man listening to "old wives who tell tales" (350e), and calls the argument "the fill of your banquet at the festival of Bendis" (354a).

**Annas** judges these arguments severely.
- The pleonexia argument trades on an ambiguity in *pleon echein*, between having more and doing better. The deeper fault is that it treats the difference between just and unjust as a difference in expertise. The blush shows Thrasymachus humiliated, but "the reader is not satisfied that Socrates has in fact won".
- The function argument slides from the just *soul* living well to the just *man* being happy, without argument.
- She defends the idea of *ergon* against the charge that it implies everything was designed for a purpose. It means a thing's characteristic activity, not a designer's intention, and Aristotle took it over in *Nicomachean Ethics* I.

### 9. Socrates Admits Defeat

Book I ends with Socrates accusing himself:

> I am just like the gluttons who grab at whatever is set before them to get a taste of it, before they have in proper measure enjoyed what went before. Before finding out what we were considering at first—what the just is—I let go of that and pursued the consideration of whether it is vice and lack of learning, or wisdom and virtue. ... So long as I do not know what the just is, I shall hardly know whether it is a virtue or not and whether the one who has it is unhappy or happy. (354b–c)

This is the classic Socratic ending. The interlocutors have been refuted, but nobody, Socrates included, knows the answer.

### 10. Why Is Book I There?

Book I is in the style of Plato's early Socratic dialogues, while the rest of the *Republic* is mostly a long exposition. So readers have long asked whether it was written separately. Weiss lists three main views (ch. 1):

| View | Who | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | Taylor, Kahn, Guthrie | Book I was always meant as the introduction and is "proleptic" of what follows |
| Separate dialogue | Dümmler, Friedländer (the "Thrasymachus") | Book I was an earlier aporetic dialogue later attached |
| Repudiation | Vlastos and others | Plato includes it to show the weakness of the Socratic elenchus |

Annas adds one piece of outside evidence. The short dialogue *Cleitophon* has Cleitophon complain that Socrates only knocks down other people's accounts of justice and never says what it is. That complaint "makes no sense at all as a reaction to the complete *Republic*; but it does make sense as a reaction to Book I on its own" (ch. 2). Still, she thinks Book I makes an "entirely suitable introduction", whatever its origin.

The three scholars give different answers to what it is for.
- **Annas: three faults in ordinary morality.** Book I diagnoses ordinary moral thinking:
  - It breeds complacency: Cephalus and Polemarchus never doubt they are just.
  - It has no intellectual backing, so it collapses at the first question.
  - It leaves a void that "is all too plausibly filled by scepticism—it is not for nothing that Polemarchus is followed by Thrasymachus".

  The rest of the *Republic* has to rebuild justice from the inside, as a state of the soul.
- **Weiss: Book I is the true account.** The "lessons of Book 1" are that justice is "external", a matter of how one treats others. Its best rulers care for those in their charge, and the just man "neither harms nor wishes to harm anyone". In her view the famous Book IV definition does not improve on this. Justice as each part of the soul doing its own job uses the same musical images, the same idea of self-mastery and the same comparison to health as Plato's account of moderation. So "Socrates has not ... provided in Book 4 a new definition of justice; instead, he has described moderation under the name justice". Book I is there to keep readers suspicious.
- **Stauffer: Book I poses the problem of justice.** Stauffer reads Socrates' poor arguments as deliberate, perhaps meant to provoke Glaucon into the challenge of Book II. The second argument shows justice is needed for communities. The first and third try to show it is good for the individual. They do not meet. "What we ordinarily take to be justice is of questionable goodness for the individual, and what we might be able to show to be good for the individual is not so clearly justice." The question Book I leaves is whether there is "a genuine whole" that is both "directed to the common good" and "good for each of us as individuals" (conclusion). Stauffer also defends starting from ordinary opinion against Rorty and Walzer, who would only "stand in the cave".

### 11. Assessment

Book I's arguments are mostly weak, but it is not a weak book. Its strength is that it shows three living attitudes to morality, each tied to a person.
- **Cephalus** is decent, pious, frightened of Hades, and happy to leave the discussion to attend to his sacrifices.
- **Polemarchus** has the traditional code of loyalty and revenge. He is open enough to accept the revolutionary conclusion that "it is never just to harm anyone".
- **Thrasymachus** is the first great statement of moral cynicism in Western literature. Justice is a racket, praised only by people too weak to be unjust. He has been reborn many times since, in Machiavelli, in Hobbes's state of nature and in Nietzsche's genealogy of morals.

Two of Socrates' moves last beyond Book I. The argument that the true ruler serves the ruled, as a doctor serves the patient, is the root of the idea that power is a trust. The argument that injustice divides even a gang of thieves, and divides a man against himself, prepares the central claim of the whole *Republic*: justice is a kind of inner harmony, and injustice a civil war in the soul.

Book I's real gift is the question it leaves to Glaucon and Adeimantus at the start of Book II. Is justice good in itself, even if a man could be unjust and never be caught?

### Conclusion

Book I begins with a joke about force and persuasion and ends with Socrates confessing he does not know what justice is. In between, Cephalus defines justice as truth-telling and paying debts and walks away to his sacrifices. Polemarchus defines it as helping friends and harming enemies and is brought to see that the just man harms no one. Thrasymachus calls justice the advantage of the stronger and "someone else's good". Socrates refutes him with the craftsman who serves his object, the musician who does not try to outdo another musician, the robbers who need justice among themselves, and the soul whose virtue is to live well. Annas reads the book as a critique of complacent ordinary morality, Weiss as the *Republic*'s truest account of justice as concern for others, and Stauffer as the first statement of the problem of whether justice can be both good for others and good for oneself.
