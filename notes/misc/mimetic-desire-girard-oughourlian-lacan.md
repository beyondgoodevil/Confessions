Both Girard and Lacan reject the common-sense picture in which I know what I want and go for it. For both, desire comes **through another person**. They disagree about what that means: for Girard, I copy my model's desire; for Lacan, I want to be what the Other wants. And Girard thinks Lacan is still trapped in a myth (Narcissus).

This is the psychology behind [[Girard's Scapegoat Mechanism: Sacrifice, Myth and the Gospels]].

## 1. Girard: Triangular Desire (*Deceit, Desire and the Novel*, 1961)

**The triangle.** Desire isn't a straight line from subject to object. There is always a third point, the **mediator** or model, from whom I learn what to want.
- **Don Quixote** wants to be a knight-errant because he imitates Amadis of Gaul. "Chivalric passion defines a desire according to Another, opposed to this desire according to Oneself that most of us pride ourselves on enjoying."
- **Emma Bovary** wants what the romantic novels taught her to want. Jules de Gaultier called this "**bovarysm**": people with no inner direction, who "become something... through the suggestion which they obey".

**"Romantic lie" vs "novelistic truth".** Romantic writers keep the illusion of spontaneous, original desire. Great novelists (Cervantes, Flaubert, Stendhal, Proust, Dostoevsky) reveal the mediator. Girard's French title is *Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque*.

**External vs internal mediation**
- **External**: the model is far away and can't become a rival (Amadis for Quixote, Christ for the saint). The imitator admits it openly.
- **Internal**: the model is close, in the same world, wanting the same objects. Then the model becomes a **rival** and an **obstacle**, and the imitator hides the imitation even from himself. This is the realm of **envy, jealousy and vanity**: Stendhal's *vanité*, Proust's snobbery, Dostoevsky's underground man.
- Max Scheler's account of *ressentiment* (envy as impotent desire facing an obstacle) is a case of this.
- The closer the mediator, the more violent and miserable the desire. The history of the novel from Cervantes to Dostoevsky is the mediator getting nearer.

**Metaphysical desire.** In the end, what the subject wants is not the object but the mediator's **being**, which he imagines to be fuller than his own: "Men become gods in the eyes of each other" (ch. 2). The object is just the path to it.
- Proust compares the desire to be the Other with thirst.
- Possessing the object brings disappointment, because the being was never in it. So desire moves on to a new mediator, and Proust's successive "selves" are the stages of that descent.

**Double mediation** (ch. 4): each becomes the other's model and rival at once.
- **Coquetry**: she pretends indifference to excite his desire, which then excites hers.
- **Advertising** doesn't say a product is good. It says others want it.
- Enemy brothers, rival nations and political parties all become mirror images. "If the lovers are never in accord, it is not because they are too 'different'... but because they are too alike."

**Masochism and sadism** (ch. 8):
- The masochist seeks out whoever rejects him, because rejection proves the mediator's superiority.
- The sadist plays the role of the god he fancies the model to be.
- Both are "imitations of an imitation".

**The novelist's conversion** (ch. 12): every great novel ends in a conversion, usually at a deathbed. Quixote renounces chivalry; Julien Sorel in prison; Raskolnikov; Proust's *Time Regained*. The novelist has to go through the same renunciation of metaphysical desire before he can write the truth, and the image is usually Christian. "The conclusion of *The Red and the Black* remains a meditation on Christian themes and symbols."

## 2. From Literature to Anthropology (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 6; *Things Hidden* III.1)

- "Rivalry does not arise because of the fortuitous convergence of two desires on a single object; rather, the subject desires the object because the rival desires it."
- Once basic needs are met, "man is subject to intense desires, though he may not know precisely for what. The reason is that he desires **being**, something he himself lacks and which some other person seems to possess."
- The model teaches "not through words... but by the example of his own desire".
- **The double bind**: the model says "Imitate me!" and, as soon as I do, "Don't imitate me!", meaning don't take my object. (Girard borrows Bateson's term.)
- **Two kinds of mimesis:**
  - **apprenticeship**: the pupil copies the master and it's fine;
  - **rivalry**: the pupil gets close enough to compete.
  - There's no objective way to tell in advance which imitation will turn bad.
- **Modern conditions** make it worse. Without inherited differences (rank, caste) to keep models at a distance, everyone's model is internal. "The more people think that they are realizing the Utopias dreamed up by their desire... the more they will in fact be working to reinforce the competitive world that is stifling them."

## 3. Girard against Freud (*Violence and the Sacred* ch. 7; *Things Hidden* III.4)

- **The Oedipus complex is a misread triangle.** Freud saw that the boy takes his father as model ("identification") and that the father becomes a rival. But Freud fixed the object in advance (the mother) and thought the desire for her was intrinsic. Girard: the desire comes from the model; the object is whatever the model points to. "Mothers" and "sisters" become objects of rivalry "simply because they happen to be at hand".
- "Freud at his best is no more 'Freudian' than Marx at his best is 'Marxist'."
- **Narcissism**: Freud's "narcissistic woman", self-sufficient and indifferent, fascinating to men, is a Proustian mirage. She fascinates because she *seems* to need no model. Freud fell for it: "Narcissism: Freud's desire".
- **Masochism, latent homosexuality, the death drive** are the mimetic process misread as instincts:
  - "secondary masochism" is choosing the most insurmountable obstacle as model;
  - "latent homosexuality" is the rival's growing prestige;
  - the "death instinct" of *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* is the logic of a desire that seeks what defeats it.
- **Nietzsche too**: his "will to power" is "the ultimate expression of cumulative resentment". He tries to separate it from *ressentiment* and can't.

## 4. Oughourlian: Interdividual Psychology

Jean-Michel Oughourlian, a psychiatrist, worked with Girard on *Things Hidden* and turned the theory into a clinical psychology.

**The "self" is made by the relation, not the other way round.**
- Between two people runs a back-and-forth: **suggestion** from A and **imitation** by B, two sides of one movement.
- The self is a "desire-self". Desire "operates the self and creates it".
- "Interdividual" replaces "individual". There is no self first, then relations. Hence: "to speak of an 'authentic' self is really meaningless".

**Two universal claims.** Every desire denies its origin:
- "My desire is mine" (ownership).
- "My desire came first; the other's is the copy" (priority).

These two lies drive rivalry. Both rivals sincerely think the other started it.

**Three possible relations to the other:**
1. **model**: identification, peace, learning;
2. **rival**: conflict over an object;
3. **obstacle**: the other is now what stands between me and being. This becomes hatred, or worship, or both.

**Mirror neurons** (*Genesis of Desire* ch. 3; *Mimetic Brain* ch. 6): Rizzolatti and Gallese found neurons in monkeys that fire both when the animal grasps something and when it sees another grasp it. They fire for an intended **grasp**, not for an aimless hand movement. Oughourlian takes this as the physical basis of imitating **intention**, which is to say desire.

***The Mimetic Brain*: three brains**
- **first**: cognitive, the cortex
- **second**: emotional, the limbic system
- **third**: mimetic, mirror neurons and the relation to the other

In disorders the third leads and the others rationalize. Jealousy, many depressions and paranoid states are "**diseases of desire**" located in the relation, not in one person. "I am being followed" is the paranoid's claim of priority taken to the extreme.

**The Fall read mimetically** (*Genesis of Desire* ch. 2):
- God makes Eve "opposite" Adam (Gen 2:18), a partner, so that there can be relation, "psychological movement".
- **The serpent** introduces **comparison**. Its lie is that God *wants the tree for himself* and is keeping it from them out of rivalry: "ye shall be as gods" (Gen 3:5). God, the perfect peaceful model, is turned into a jealous rival.
- After eating, "they knew that they were naked" (3:7). Nakedness is cultural, not natural: the look of the other, comparison, shame.
- "Who told thee that thou wast naked?" (3:11). God's question asks *who the model was*.
- Then the blame game, each accusing the other (3:12–13): the first scapegoating.
- "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" names the same blindness.

**Clinical practice** (*Genesis* ch. 4, epilogue):
- Many couples' crises are rivalries in disguise: alcohol, affairs and depression are moves in a power struggle.
- Advice:
  - don't compare;
  - don't bring in third parties;
  - accept a "mutually agreed-on inequality", "a selective hierarchy";
  - let a **peaceful third**, transcendence, a shared good like the child's welfare, stand between you instead of a rival.

## 5. Lacan: The Mirror Stage and the Desire of the Other

**The mirror stage** ("The Mirror Stage", 1936/1949):
- A baby of 6–18 months, who can't yet walk or control its body, sees itself in a mirror and greets the image with jubilation.
- It "assumes" the image as itself. The **ego** is formed by identifying with an external, unified picture, while the felt body is still fragmented and helpless.
- So the ego is from the start a **misrecognition** (*méconnaissance*), built "in a fictional direction", an image of oneself that is really an other. Lacan's terms are the **imaginary** order and the small other (*petit autre*).
- **Aggressiveness** ("Aggressiveness in Psychoanalysis") comes with this. If my ego is an image I share with my counterpart, he is both me and my rival.
  - **Transitivism**: "A child who beats another child says that he himself was beaten; a child who sees another child fall, cries."
  - Lacan quotes **Augustine** (*Confessions* I.7): "I myself have seen and known an infant to be jealous even though it could not speak. It became pale, and cast bitter looks on its foster-brother." For Lacan this is the earliest aggressiveness.
  - The end of the mirror stage "tips the whole of human knowledge into being **mediated by the other's desire**".

**The Other with a capital O.** Beyond the small other of the mirror, Lacan places the big **Other**:
- language, the symbolic order, the law;
- first met through the mother's speech.

The subject is "**the discourse of the Other**". It's spoken before it speaks, and its unconscious is "structured like a language".

**Need, demand, desire** (Bailly ch. 7):
- **need** is biological (milk);
- **demand** is need addressed to someone in words. It is always also a demand for **love**: "the demand cancels the particularity of whatever is given by changing it into a proof of love";
- **desire** is what is left when you subtract need from demand. Nothing can fill it.

"Love is giving what one doesn't have."

**"Man's desire is the desire of the Other"** (*le désir de l'homme est le désir de l'Autre*), in "Subversion of the Subject". Lacan points out the double meaning of the genitive:
- I desire **what** the Other desires;
- I desire **as** an Other, through the Other's language;
- and above all I desire **to be desired** by the Other.

"Man's desire finds its meaning in the other's desire, not so much because the other holds the keys to the desired object, as because his first object(ive) is to be **recognized** by the other" ("Function and Field of Speech"). That comes from Hegel via Kojève: desire is the desire for recognition.

The question that haunts the subject is the Other's ***Che vuoi?*** "What do you want (of me)?"

**Objet petit a** (Bailly ch. 8) is the "object cause of desire". It isn't what I want but what makes me want: the unattainable something that lends an object its shine. Bailly's illustration is the *Symposium* again: Alcibiades sees in ugly, Silenus-like Socrates "divine and golden images" hidden inside.

## 6. Girard against Lacan (*Things Hidden* III.2, III.5)

- Lacan sees the **symmetry of doubles** better than Freud. But he explains it as "capture by the imaginary", the mirror stage, instead of reciprocal imitation.
- "The whole imagery of mirrors and the imaginary rests (as does the Freudian thesis) on the **myth of Narcissus**... The mirror stage is a naive resurgence of mythology."
- The mirror makes desire a desire for **the same**, one's own image. But Proust shows that even the most "narcissistic" desire is "a thirst for the greatest possible **difference**". I want the being I lack, not my reflection.
- **Hegel's desire for recognition** is only a "complication" of mimetic desire: desire according to the other's desire, which is not the same as desire *for* the other's desire. The need to be recognized follows from imitation, not the reverse.
- **Psychosis**: Lacan calls it the "foreclosure" of the symbolic, a static absence. Girard: in psychosis the symbolic doesn't vanish; it becomes "the very stake of the mimetic rivalry". It's a process in time, not a missing piece of structure.
- Girard on Lacanianism as a club: it "sets up a hierarchy of knowledge". "The discovery of the scapegoat mechanism is not an initiation."

## 7. Comparison

| | **Girard / Oughourlian** | **Lacan** |
|---|---|---|
| desire comes from | a model whose desire I copy | the Other: language, the parents' discourse, the wish to be recognized |
| what desire wants in the end | the model's **being** | to be the object of the Other's desire; the lost *objet a* |
| the self | "interdividual", produced by relation | a split subject; the ego is a mirror image, a misrecognition |
| rivalry and aggression | the core problem, which escalates into violence and scapegoating | built into the imaginary relation with one's counterpart |
| the key myth | none; myths hide the mechanism, the Bible reveals it | Narcissus (mirror), Oedipus (Name-of-the-Father) |
| the cure | conversion: renounce metaphysical desire, imitate a non-rival model (Christ) | "do not give ground on your desire"; analysis traverses the fantasy and separates the subject from the Other's demand |
| attitude to Christianity | the Gospel is the truth about desire | psychoanalysis as a secular substitute; Christian ideas reread structurally |

**What they share:**
- desire is **not autonomous**;
- the ego is **not master** in its own house;
- relations are **triangular**;
- **Augustine**, Hegel and the novelists saw more than modern psychology.

**The basic difference**: Lacan describes desire as **lack** built into the structure of the subject, with no exit, only a better relation to it. Girard describes desire as **imitation**, which can be pointed at a good model.

## 8. Christ as Model (*I See Satan* ch. 1)

- **The tenth commandment** ends the Decalogue with the root: "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house... nor any thing that is thy neighbour's" (Exod 20:17). The sins forbidden before it (murder, adultery, theft, false witness) all grow out of this desire.
- **"Imitate me"** without rivalry. Jesus "does not claim to possess a desire proper, a desire 'of his very own'". He imitates the Father: "The Son can do nothing of himself, but what he seeth the Father do" (John 5:19). So imitating Jesus means imitating someone who **doesn't grasp**, who can never become my rival. Paul: "Be ye followers of me, even as I also am of Christ" (1 Cor 11:1).
- **Scandal**: *skandalon* is the stumbling block, the obstacle that attracts the more it repels. The model-rival is a scandal. "Woe unto the world because of offences! for it must needs be that offences come" (Matt 18:7).
- Peter becomes a scandal when he imitates the world's desire: "Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offence unto me" (Matt 16:23).

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What rings true**
- The Fathers knew mimetic desire under other names. **Envy** (*phthonos*) is the passion that grieves at another's good. Cain kills Abel out of it; the devil fell by it; "through envy of the devil came death into the world" (Wisdom 2:24).
- **Vainglory** (*kenodoxia*) is desire for others' esteem, and Evagrius and Cassian place it among the eight. Climacus says it can attach to anything, even to fasting and humility. That's Girard's metaphysical desire: the object doesn't matter, the gaze does. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- **Augustine** is a common ancestor of Girard and Lacan, and they use him well: the jealous infant; the pears stolen not from hunger but for the company and the forbidden (*Confessions* II).
- **The Fall** as a lie about God (that he is a rival keeping something back) matches the patristic reading. God isn't jealous of man; he made man to share his life ("partakers of the divine nature", 2 Pet 1:4). The serpent offered by grasping what God meant to give as gift. Compare Phil 2:6: Christ "thought it not robbery" (*harpagmos*, something to be grasped) "to be equal with God", the exact reverse of Adam. See [[Adam and Eve Sin]], [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- The **practical** wisdom (don't compare; beware the closest rivals; fix desire on a model who cannot be a rival) is the wisdom of the spiritual father and the monastery.

**Where to push back**
- **Is all desire mimetic?** Girard says only "once basic needs are satisfied". But the patristic picture also has a desire that isn't borrowed: the natural longing for God, *eros* for the Good, which Maximus calls the **natural will**. Rightly ordered, it isn't copying anyone; it's the creature's movement toward its source. Girard's "desire for being" is that longing misdirected onto a human model. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
- **Lacan's lack** has no fulfilment, only analysis that makes it bearable. The Christian claim is that the restless heart *can* rest. "Thou hast made us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (*Confessions* I.1). It's the same diagnosis with a different end.
- **Selves and persons.** Oughourlian's "interdividual" self, made by relations, is close to the Orthodox idea of the **person** as communion (Zizioulas). But for him the relation seems to produce the self with nothing prior. In theology, persons are given being by God and then fulfilled in communion; they don't *arise* out of relations among creatures alone.
- **Clinical evidence.** Mirror neurons have been oversold in popular writing; their role in human imitation and intention is debated. The theory doesn't stand or fall on them.
