The inclusion of the *Filioque* (Latin for "and the Son") in the Nicene Creed was largely influenced by the Frankish Kingdom, particularly through the efforts of Charlemagne and his successors. The controversy over the *Filioque* began to take shape during the 8th and 9th centuries, especially as the Carolingian Empire sought to assert its theological and political independence from the Byzantine East.

1. **The Frankish Influence**: The *Filioque* clause was introduced into the Latin text of the Nicene Creed by the Church in the West. While the original 381 Nicene Creed said that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father (*ex Patre*), the Latin-speaking Church in the West added *Filioque*, meaning "and the Son," in an effort to affirm that the Holy Spirit proceeds from both the Father and the Son. This change was made in the context of combating Arianism, which denied the full divinity of Christ, and was also a political and theological move in favor of the Carolingian Empire's authority over the Church.
2. **Pope Leo III and Charlemagne**: Pope Leo III, who was a contemporary of Charlemagne, allowed the *Filioque* to be added to the Creed during Charlemagne's reign, though it was primarily a regional development in the Frankish territories. Charlemagne’s desire to solidify his control over religious matters in his empire and to counter the influence of the Eastern Orthodox Church in the East played a significant role in promoting the clause.
3. **The Eastern Response**: The Eastern Orthodox Church, on the other hand, rejected the *Filioque* as a theological innovation. It argued that the change had not been made in an ecumenical council and therefore was not valid. The Eastern Church maintained that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, a position it argues is rooted in the original wording of the Nicene Creed.

This theological and political dispute led to further division between East and West, and the *Filioque* controversy remains one of the theological differences between the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Churches today.
