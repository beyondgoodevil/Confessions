Craig Keener is a New Testament scholar best known for his large commentaries on Matthew, John and Acts. *Miracles* began, he says, as a footnote in the Acts commentary. He wanted to cite a few academic collections of modern eyewitness miracle claims against scholars who dismiss the miracles of Acts as legend. To his surprise he found almost none, so he began collecting the claims himself, and the footnote grew into 1,100 pages (pp. 4–5). The book has a narrow historical aim and a wider philosophical one. Its method is the historian's *principle of analogy*, long used against the Gospel miracles, now turned the other way. This note follows the argument in order. The note on [[Hume Against Miracles, and the Replies]] treats Hume's essay and the philosophical replies in more depth; this one concentrates on what Keener adds.

### 1. Two Theses

Keener states his primary thesis as "simply that eyewitnesses do offer miracle claims" (p. 1). Historians, he argues, should not dismiss eyewitness material in the miracle stories of the Gospels and Acts, "since large numbers of eyewitnesses can and do offer miracle claims, many of them quite comparable in character to the early Christian accounts" (p. 2). He expects no reader to dispute this by the end. The secondary thesis is more controversial: "we should not rule out the possibility of supernatural causation for some of these healing claims" (p. 3). He hopes scholars will accept "supernatural theism" as "at least one academically acceptable explanatory option rather than presupposing its exclusion" (p. 8).

He calls the book a prolegomenon (p. 9). It does not argue for any particular Gospel miracle. It asks a prior question: "Do we need to treat the miracles differently than, that is, as less authentic than, the rest of the narratives in which they occur?" (p. 16). Many scholars accept the Gospels as ancient biography and Acts as history yet reject the miracle reports in them. That inconsistency, he says, comes "not from a change in genre but from philosophic assumptions" (p. 14).

He is open about his position. He was once an atheist and is now a Christian, and holds that "a theistic bias is not more of a bias than an atheistic one, such as I once held" (p. 14). He also says the book is not triumphalist. He and his wife suffered eight miscarriages, and "signs are foretastes of the kingdom, not its fullness" (pp. 10–11).

### 2. Jesus as Healer: The Ancient Evidence

Every ancient source that touches the subject agrees that Jesus and his early followers worked miracles: Q, Mark, the special material in Matthew and Luke, John, Acts, the Epistles, Revelation, and hostile Jewish and pagan sources (p. 22). Keener singles out the "bedrock" Q saying about Chorazin and Bethsaida, villages judged for ignoring Jesus's works. He also points to Mark 6:5, where Jesus "could not" heal for lack of faith, something Mark would hardly have invented (p. 23). More than 31 per cent of the verses in Mark involve miracles (p. 24). Theissen and Merz put it this way: "Just as the kingdom of God stands at the centre of Jesus's preaching, so healings and exorcisms form the centre of his activity" (p. 24). The rabbis and Celsus concede the miracles and attribute them to sorcery. Josephus calls Jesus a doer of "startling deeds." No ancient source denies that the miracles happened (p. 25).

Paul supplies the earliest evidence. He reminds the Corinthians of the "signs and wonders" done among them (2 Cor 12:12; Rom 15:19). Appealing to his audience's own knowledge argues against fabrication (pp. 29–30). Several miracles in Acts fall in the "we" passages, where the narrator claims to have been present (Acts 20:9–12; 28:8–9) (p. 33). Comparing Luke with his source Mark shows that Luke kept the substance of the miracle stories and added acclamations rather than new miracles (pp. 32–33).

### 3. The Ancient Parallels

Most ancient healing claims came from sanctuaries of Asclepius, Serapis and Isis. Supplicants slept in the temple, dreamed of the god and woke cured (pp. 37–41). These share with the Gospels only the idea of healing by a deity (p. 65). The closer parallel would be an individual healer. Here Keener finds that the "divine man" of older scholarship is a modern composite; the traits were blended "only by the creativity of modern scholarship" (p. 51). Howard Clark Kee says that using Philostratus or the magical papyri as evidence for first-century events is "historiographically irresponsible" (p. 52). Philostratus wrote about Apollonius of Tyana more than a century after his death, whereas Mark wrote about forty years after Jesus. The parts of Apollonius's story closest to the Gospels are the late ones, which probably reflect Christian influence (pp. 53–56).

Jewish models are closer: Elijah and Elisha, and later Honi the Circle-Drawer and Hanina ben Dosa (pp. 58–60). Even here the contrasts are sharp. Eric Eve shows that early Jewish sources take little interest in healing. The "sign prophets" Josephus describes promised signs but performed none, and the rabbinic tradition about Honi comes "perhaps half a millennium" after him. Hanina petitioned God for power, whereas Jesus bore it himself (pp. 74–76). Keener concludes that Jesus is "unique in the surviving Jewish literature of his time as being portrayed as performing a large number of healings and exorcisms" (p. 76).

He makes a point about form. Pierre Benoit asks, "Is there any other way of relating a miracle?" Every healing story names the problem, the cure and the reaction. What distinguishes them is "the substance, the external authentication, the internal probability" (p. 77). The supernatural element is not a parallel at all, since believing in it is "a premise utterly unobjectionable to most societies in history" (p. 78). Celestial portents such as armies in the clouds before Jerusalem fell belong to a different category from healings done in front of witnesses (pp. 80–82).

### 4. Ancient Skepticism and Modern Antisupernaturalism

Ancient intellectuals were not credulous. Galen rejected Moses's God, who is "not bound by the orderly processes of nature" (p. 87). Polybius mocked sensationalist historians, and Thucydides promised fewer fables (pp. 89–90). Lucian describes how he made up marvels about the death of Peregrinus and then heard listeners claim to have witnessed them (p. 89). Yet ancient historians judged case by case. Tacitus would not collect "fabulous tales," but neither would he "dare to deny the truth of common tradition" (pp. 90–92). Plutarch warned against believing too much (superstition) and too little (irreligion) (p. 92). Only the radical Enlightenment rejected all supernatural claims in advance (pp. 105–106).

Modern skepticism is a starting point, not a neutral finding. To begin with a "guilty until proven innocent" stance toward a narrative is a form of poisoning the well (p. 97). Ramsay MacMullen, a historian of Rome, says that doubting the ancients' account of what they saw "could only be theology, good or bad" (p. 98). Walter Wink, a member of the Jesus Seminar, saw a large uterine tumour disappear after prayer and concluded: "I have no difficulty believing that Jesus actually healed people, and not just of psychosomatic diseases" (p. 103). Hume answered the story of the Siamese king who refused to believe that water could freeze hard by saying "the Indian prince ... reasoned justly". Keener replies that Hume's own example concedes that human experience is not uniform (pp. 104–105).

### 5. Hume Revisited

The chapter on Hume (ch. 5) is the philosophical core of the book. It covers much the same ground as Houston and Earman (see [[Hume Against Miracles, and the Replies]]), with some arguments of Keener's own. Its thesis is that "the assumption that suprahuman activity is impossible is an interpretive grid, not a demonstrated fact" (p. 107).

- **Context.** Hume's essay was not original. William Woolston's tract against miracles had sold perhaps thirty thousand copies, and Conyers Middleton's work overshadowed Hume's in his own day (pp. 118–119). Locke and Boyle were empiricists who affirmed miracles. The founders of modern science saw laws of nature as decrees of a Legislator who was free to change them; "only deists insisted that God always worked through such laws" (pp. 120–122).
- **Definition.** Defining a miracle as a "violation" of laws that cannot be violated wins the argument by definition. Hume's opponents never used the word (pp. 131–132). As one critic quoted by Keener puts it, "The miracle seems for ever frustrated in its attempts to violate; for as soon as it imagines that it has succeeded, it finds that there was nothing there after all to violate!" (p. 134). Aviezer Tucker adds that Hume's definition "does not encompass the most paradigmatic cases of miracles" (p. 133). Augustine had said that what seems contrary to nature is not so for God, "for him 'nature' is what he does" (p. 130).
- **Theism.** One "is entitled to adduce" natural law against miracles only "by presupposing a conclusively justified atheism, or presupposing belief in a non-miracle-working god" (p. 139). J. S. Mill thought Hume showed only that "if we do not already believe in supernatural agencies, no miracles can prove to us their existence" (p. 129).
- **Circularity.** "Despite claiming to argue inductively, Hume's argument against miracles is deductive" (p. 143). The appeal to uniform experience "begs the question at issue, which is whether anyone has experienced a miracle" (p. 144). And "If experience is reliable in knowing that water is normally not turned to wine, why would it not be reliable in recognizing when water is turned to wine?" (p. 145).
- **Testimony.** Applied elsewhere, Hume's standard would require us "to dismiss the vast majority of what we believe we presently know about the past" (p. 148). Hume did not follow it in his own historical writing, and courts could not work under it (pp. 149–151). Charles Babbage showed mathematically that several independent witnesses can overcome a very low prior probability (p. 152). Bayes's essay was published in 1763 by Richard Price, who used it against Hume. Hume called Price's objection "plausible and ingenious" but never revised his essay (p. 153).
- **Rare events.** A perfect bridge hand has odds of 1,635,013,559,600 to 1, yet people are dealt one (p. 157). In 1950 all fifteen members of a church choir happened to be late for a practice at a church that then exploded (p. 157). Price noted that we believe a newspaper's report of a lottery winner even though any one ticket is very unlikely to win (p. 158). The relevant question is "How frequently are reports of similar miracles, given in similar situations, true?" (p. 159).
- **The Jansenists.** Hume admitted that the cures at the tomb of François de Pâris were well attested and rejected them anyway. Keener adds a case close to Pascal. In 1656 his niece Marguerite Perrier was cured of a long-standing eye fistula at Port-Royal, and royal physicians examined her (pp. 163–165).

Keener ends the chapter with David Johnson's verdict (in a Cornell University Press monograph) that any good argument in Hume's essay "is simply a philosophical myth" (p. 169) and Keith Ward's that the arguments "are exceptionally poor and are found acceptable only by those who are (rightly) impressed by his general philosophical acuteness" (p. 170).

### 6. After Hume: Theology, History and Analogy

The consensus spread into theology. Paulus explained the dead whom Jesus raised as cases of "suspended animation," and Venturini had Jesus carry medicines (p. 176). Strauss had to date the Gospels to the second century to leave time for myths to form. When a friend of his was cured of lameness after visiting the Lutheran pastor Blumhardt, Strauss called it psychosomatic (pp. 176–177). Bultmann restated the philosophical assumptions of his day, and Wink calls the result "functional, methodological atheism" (p. 179).

In history, Ernst Troeltsch's *principle of analogy* held that events unlike anything known today should not be accepted for the past. Keener's central claim is that "this principle of analogy makes first-century miracle reports more rather than less plausible today" (p. 185). He quotes Brad Gregory, writing in *History and Theory* in 2006, that historians who assume metaphysical naturalism write "secular confessional history" (p. 190). Gregory also says that, for many academics, the impossibility of miracles "seems natural, normal, obvious, undeniable—rather like religious beliefs in close-knit, traditional societies" (p. 171). Since the academy prides itself on objectivity, Keener asks: "should we not grant greater credence to the word of a thousand eyewitnesses ... than to the insistence of a hundred thousand nonwitness colleagues merely restating unproved assumptions?" (p. 200).

Bultmann claimed that no one who uses electric light can believe in miracles. The polls say otherwise. Pew found that 79 per cent of Americans agree that "miracles still occur today as in ancient times," and 34 per cent say they have witnessed or experienced divine healing (p. 204). Karl Barth criticized Bultmann for dismissing the healings around Blumhardt (p. 206).

### 7. The Majority World and Hume's "Barbarous Nations"

Hume held that miracle reports "abound among ignorant and barbarous nations." Keener examines this claim (ch. 7). Hume's footnote "Of National Characters" said that he was "apt to suspect the Negroes ... to be naturally inferior to the whites." He compared Francis Williams, a Cambridge-educated Jamaican who wrote Latin verse, to a parrot. Kant cited Hume on race, and pro-slavery writers cited him regularly (pp. 223–225). Keener concludes that the argument from "ignorant and barbarous nations" "should never again be admitted; its origins are inseparable from his ethnocentrism" (p. 225).

The numbers are now large. A 2006 Pew survey of ten countries found that most Pentecostals, many charismatics and a good share of other Christians said they had "witnessed divine healings." Examples include 87, 78 and 47 per cent in Kenya, and 62, 46 and 28 per cent in the United States. Keener estimates about two hundred million claimants in those countries alone (pp. 237–238). "They are dismissing hundreds of millions of miracle claims—usually without having examined any of them" (p. 237). He also cites voices from the Majority World. Danny McCain, who has taught in Nigeria for two decades, calls outright rejection "arrogant and unprofessional" (p. 217). Hwa Yung, a Malaysian Methodist bishop, says that in Asia whether miracles are possible "is a non-question" (p. 264).

Anthropologists report the same thing. Edith Turner observed forty-six cures among the Iñupiat (p. 246). A Cambridge anthropologist was treated by a Makushi shaman in Guyana and could only say "a simple, stupefied, 'Yes'" (p. 247). Anthropologists, Keener notes, are more skeptical about their own anomalous experiences than other people are about theirs (p. 248). His conclusion: antisupernaturalism is "far from the majority view in the world and thus henceforth ought to argue rather than presuppose its case" (p. 263).

### 8. The Case Reports

Chapters 8–11 collect hundreds of claims. They come from published sources, about a hundred of Keener's own interviews and correspondence with more than a hundred others (p. 266).

| Region | Examples Keener reports |
|---|---|
| Philippines | David Dominong, an electrocuted welder told he might not walk for years, walked at a crusade; Keener saw his scars (p. 272) |
| India | A neurologist estimates that 80–85 per cent of new churches are planted through healings; a woman's fibroids disappear on ultrasound; an atheist businessman's angiogram blockages fall below 50 per cent (pp. 284–285) |
| China | Up to 90 per cent of new believers cite healing; pastors Keener met in 2007 describe a brain-stem tumour shrinking to the size of a grain of rice (pp. 297, 303–304) |
| Korea | A church commission confirmed the healings in Kim Ik-tu's ministry, and in 1923 the Korean Presbyterian Church gave up the doctrine that miracles had ceased (p. 291) |
| Africa | A Nigerian medical student's ulcerated arm closed overnight, as his classmate Dr Tonye Briggs attests; in Mozambique a deaf girl began to hear in front of a village of non-Christians, about five hundred committed themselves to Christianity and a church was founded the next day (pp. 326, 331–332) |
| Congo-Brazzaville | Keener's wife's family and their mainline Protestant circle: deacons and lay people with healing accounts, a French doctor at the hospital remarking "You pray to a true God" (pp. 333–338) |
| Latin America | Margarita Campos, a Chilean pastor, awoke from a meningitis coma after scans showed half her brain damaged and returned to pastoring; her doctors said "You must have good connections up there" (p. 355) |
| United States | Brad Wilkinson's atrial septal defect was gone by the time of surgery; the surgeon said "You can count this as a miracle" and the hospital's risk manager added "this was not a misdiagnosis" (pp. 430–432) |

Keener explains how he chose them. He offers samples, not his strongest cases. He screened out claims he found fraudulent, and an investigator who shared cases with him found two to be fraudulent (pp. 250–251). Claims made on the spot at public meetings are the least reliable, and long-term recoveries reported later are worth more (pp. 252–253). Most healings, by one survey, follow private prayer, not a healing evangelist (p. 253). He also covers church history (ch. 10). Augustine collected about seventy written testimonies at a shrine in Hippo within two years and was himself present at the healing of Innocentius (p. 359). In 1743 Mercy Wheeler, bedridden since 1726, rose and walked, and a critic who conceded the cure still refused to call it a miracle (pp. 381–382). The chapter also covers Lourdes, Blumhardt, Dorothy Kerin, the Anglican healer James Moore Hickson and early Pentecostalism. Keener does not hide the failures. Some healers opposed medicine, and in 1915 forty-five South African Pentecostal missionaries had died of malaria after refusing quinine (pp. 395–399).

### 9. Blindness, Paralysis, Death and Nature

Chapter 12 collects the kinds of cure that are hardest to explain psychosomatically. Keener's survey includes "more than 350 modern healings of blindness" (p. 522), many of them involving cataracts that witnesses saw disappear. Dr Bungishabaku Katho, now a university president in Congo, prayed with two colleagues for a blind woman in a village dominated by a witch doctor. After about two minutes she cried "I can see!" and she lived another ten years (pp. 516–517). For paralysis, Keener asks: "Is a psychosomatic cure the most inherently probable explanation for the majority of cases of paralysis? Normally, chronic paralysis is irreversible" (p. 536).

On raisings of the dead he writes that the claims are "fairly numerous today (though of course still extremely rare proportionate to the number of deaths)" (pp. 536–537). He argues three points:

- **Brain damage.** After about six minutes without oxygen, brain damage is normally irreversible. Many of those reported revived had been dead for hours and showed none (pp. 541–542).
- **Traditional societies.** To say they were only in comas underestimates "the ability of people in traditional societies, who live close to death, to detect respiration" (p. 543). And it would leave unexplained why so many deep comas ended during prayer.
- **His own family.** His wife's sister Thérèse was bitten by a snake at about two and stopped breathing for about three hours while her mother carried her over a mountain to an evangelist. She revived during prayer, without brain damage. Keener confirmed the story with his mother-in-law and with the evangelist (pp. 557–558). "Would Hume have been so convinced that miracles were against common human experience if he had such an account from his own in-laws?" (p. 558).

He reports physicians too. Dr Chauncey Crandall, a cardiologist, attended Jeff Markin, who had flatlined for about forty minutes, whose fingers and toes had turned black, and who was declared dead at 8:05 a.m. Crandall prayed and asked for one more shock. The heart restarted, and Markin recovered without brain damage: "In my more than twenty years as a cardiologist, I have never seen a heartbeat restored so completely and suddenly" (pp. 577–579). Keener also traced an early twentieth-century account, the raising of Eula Wilson in Wichita in 1907, back to local newspapers printed within days. They quote her doctor, and the later retelling turned out to be accurate, not embellished (pp. 547–548).

Nature miracles are rarer, both in the Gospels and today, but there are reports of storms stilled and rain falling at the time predicted. Emmanuel Itapson, a colleague of Keener's, says his father declared in the rainy season that a mocking village would stay dry for four days, and it did while rain fell all around (p. 594). Keener's comment on such reports: "Clearly they do not always happen, but given what we know of nature, they should never happen" (p. 598). Ancient parallels to walking on water are about mythical figures or were written centuries later. "What we lack are accounts of not-yet-deified human figures doing what the Gospels report Jesus doing, from reporters roughly contemporary" (p. 585).

### 10. Natural Explanations

Keener concedes a great deal to natural causes (ch. 13). The book's epigraph from George Gordon Dawson states the opposing view: "The facts need not be questioned. It was the explanation of the facts which was wrong. They were mental and faith cures" (p. 603). Many people are not healed, some recoveries are temporary, and everyone dies (pp. 603–605). The natural explanations he weighs:

- **Fraud.** It is real. A cult leader's claimed exorcism of epilepsy was exaggerated, and Tulane found that "no EEG readings satisfactorily established epilepsy" (pp. 612–613). But inferring from some frauds that all claims are false is guilt by association (pp. 614–616).
- **Emotional arousal.** Some people walk at a meeting and are carried out afterwards (pp. 617–618).
- **Religion and health.** Religious practice correlates with lower rates of depression and addiction and with longer life. The effect on mortality is comparable to regular exercise or statins (pp. 621–624). But such general benefits "would not explain ... instant healings of long-term blindness or deafness, the immediate repair of bones, or restoration to life after apparent death" (p. 628).
- **Placebo and suggestion.** These are powerful, and many folk remedies work through them (pp. 636–637). But they are "futile" for babies, raisings and storms (p. 638). Spontaneous remission of cancer is rare, roughly one case in sixty thousand to one in a hundred thousand, so it should not cluster in one circle (p. 632).

The same reasoning applies to Jesus. To call his cures psychosomatic, one has to suppose that in a short Galilean ministry he met mostly psychosomatic cases of blindness, deafness and paralysis, and never failed publicly on organic ones (pp. 632–634). Medical anthropologists such as Pilch and Crossan allow that he cured culturally defined illness, but "scholarship is permitted to discuss any hypothesis—so long as we do not invoke God" (p. 635). When Stevan Davies calls his exclusion of the supernatural "a premise I accept absolutely," Keener asks, as he asks his students, "Who made up that rule?" (p. 635).

### 11. Biased Standards

Chapter 14 argues that the usual tests are built to fail. The Vancouver study of 1925, still cited against healing, looked into the meetings of the evangelist Charles Price. Its committee had stated that God works only through natural causes. Most of its ministers had already opposed Price publicly, and it examined fewer than 6 per cent of those prayed for. A minority report protested (pp. 648–650). A 1956 British medical committee printed doctors' accounts of unexplained recoveries in its appendixes and still put them down to misdiagnosis or remission (pp. 652–653). Doctors who saw an epileptic's EEG become normal after prayer preferred to say she had been "misdiagnosed and mistreated for the past twenty years" (p. 655). Keener's rule: "When a doctor denies that a cure is miraculous based on a philosophic paradigm that excludes miracles ... he speaks not as a doctor but as an amateur philosopher" (p. 656).

He discusses several such tests:

- **Medical documentation.** Demanding it excludes most cures in places without doctors, along with every cure in the Gospels (pp. 656–663). Many people do not know how to get their records. Hospitals destroy them. Privacy law kept a speech pathologist from releasing the records of a stroke patient who, against all expectation, could swallow again (pp. 658–661).
- **Misdiagnosis.** If misdiagnosis explained most cures, the medical profession would be "very incompetent" (p. 667).
- **Lourdes.** Of more than half a million sick pilgrims, about five thousand claimed cures and only sixty-five have been declared miracles (p. 679). Cases are excluded if any medicine was taken, if the condition was not demonstrably organic, or if documents are missing. No cures were proclaimed between 1913 and 1946, although the records of 1925–46 list eighty-nine as medically inexplicable. "It is likely that many of Jesus's miracles ... would have been screened out had they occurred instead at Lourdes" (pp. 680–681). Alexis Carrel watched Marie Bailly recover from peritonitis there in 1902. The Lyons medical faculty rejected him in 1905, and he won the Nobel Prize in 1912 (p. 683).
- **Deferred explanation.** To appeal to "as yet unknown laws of nature" for raisings yields "a tremendous implausibility" (p. 699). It is a "naturalism-of-the-gaps," "except without any discernible gap" (p. 701). Skeptics "have laid out the rules of the game in such a way that they cannot possibly lose" (p. 703).
- **Controlled studies.** Prayer studies give mixed results. Keener cites C. S. Lewis: "I do not see how any real prayer could go on under such conditions" (pp. 708–710).

He adds evidence about the academy. Religious scientists often keep quiet about their faith. Andrew Greeley says "the academy had its own dogmas at least as rigid" as his pre-Vatican II seminary's (p. 689). Michael Polanyi recalled that the French Academy denied that meteorites fall, and museums in five other countries threw theirs out (pp. 691–692). Only about 15 per cent of elite scientists accept the conflict model of science and religion (p. 696).

### 12. The Strongest Cases

Chapter 15 gathers the evidence. In a 2004 survey of 1,100 American physicians, 73 per cent believed miracles can occur today, and 55 per cent said they had "seen treatment results in their patients that they would consider miraculous" (p. 721). Crandall describes a man's flesh-eating ulcer, eaten to the bone and due for amputation, healing within a week of prayer. "It can't happen on its own ... Impossible." Crandall's own son died of leukaemia (pp. 720–721). The philosopher Robert Larmer interviewed Irene MacDonald, who had terminal multiple sclerosis, could not leave her bed, and walked out of church and stayed well for more than twenty-five years (pp. 730–731).

Keener then gives his own testimony, because Hume denied that there are credible witnesses with something to lose. "By laying my integrity on the line, I join those who challenge Hume's denial of the existence of credible witnesses with something to lose" (p. 733). He saw an elderly woman who had not walked in years lifted from her wheelchair "in the name of Jesus." He was "terrified" she would fall, and he saw her walking every week afterwards (pp. 737–738). He adds a cyst that disappeared, failed kidneys that started working again and his own damaged ankle. "Yet on other occasions of equally intense prayers, no suprahuman cures were forthcoming. No formula" (pp. 738–739). He sets out a table of cases from people he knows. For each he asks how he knows the witness, whether a psychosomatic cure is possible, how often such events happen naturally, and whether a supernatural explanation is more plausible (pp. 751–756).

His last argument is about clustering. Even if someone pronounced dead revived without help once in five hundred thousand cases, Keener found a raising account in almost every circle he asked. These included a Nigerian minister, one of the fifteen Filipinos he knew best, a Cuban nurse, an Ethiopian friend, his wife's family and six others in their circle, and a family from his brother's church (p. 757). "Multiple extraordinary events like raisings in the same circle seem massively improbable as pure coincidences" (p. 758). In his survey he counted about four hundred claimed healings of blindness and nearly three hundred raisings, from more than 150 sources (pp. 748–750).

### 13. Exorcism and Possession

The first two appendixes take up exorcism, which the main text largely leaves aside. Ancient views of *daimones* ranged from spirits of the dead to intermediaries between gods and humans. Jewish demonology grew after the exile. Exorcism mostly involved incantations, roots, rings and fumigation; Josephus saw a demon drawn out through a man's nose with Solomon's root in front of Vespasian (pp. 769–786). The Gospels omit such techniques, and Justin contrasted Jewish and pagan "fumigations and incantations" with the name of Jesus (p. 785).

Anthropology shows that possession behaviour is found worldwide. Erika Bourguignon found possession beliefs in 360 of 488 societies sampled, 74 per cent (pp. 791–792). Denying the phenomena is like "being a 'flat-earther'" (p. 790). Raymond Firth wrote that at times "it has been hard for the anthropologist to persuade himself that it is really the same person" (p. 797). Edith Turner, attending a Zambian healing ritual in 1985, saw "a giant thing emerging out of the flesh of her back" and concluded that spirit affliction "isn't a matter of metaphor and symbol, or even psychology" (pp. 831–832). The psychiatrist Scott Peck thought the 350-year separation of natural and supernatural might one day be recognized "as having been a gigantic mistake" (p. 839). Keener adds experiences from his own family in Congo. He ends the appendix by allowing that "Africa may yet know some things that the West has forgotten" (p. 856). His conclusion is limited: possession is documented so widely that its presence in the Gospels and Acts does not mark them as legend.

### 14. Hagiography, Natural Law and Dreams

The last three appendixes are short.

- **Hagiography.** Some critics read the Gospels through medieval saints' lives. Keener quotes an older writer: "the miraculous element is exhibited in these medieval biographies of saints very much in proportion to the interval of time between the events and the date of writing" (p. 858). The legend of the Ethiopian saint Takla Hâymânôt was written two centuries after him. In it he praises the Trinity at three days old, dies and returns forty times, and raises a thousand men dead for twenty-five years (pp. 863–865). The Gospels come from within a generation, so modern eyewitness claims are the better analogy. "Western children of the Enlightenment are not entitled to blend all miracle accounts together" (p. 866).
- **Natural law.** In Stoic, Ciceronian and Philonic use, natural law was a universal moral and rational order. If the gods acted, their acts belonged to nature. The Enlightenment developed the phrase "in a distinctive direction," as mechanical rules (pp. 867–869).
- **Dreams and visions.** These are not miracles, but many cultures treat them as revelation. Mark Noll says African Christians "are not surprised when Jesus speaks to them in dreams and visions" (p. 876). Keener records some of his own dreams and admits that "out of thousands of dreams ... some will coincide" (p. 882).

### 15. Weighing the Argument

Keener sets limits on his own claims. Most people are not healed. Natural factors explain many recoveries. Fraud and exaggeration are real. Cessationists who hold that miracles have become rarer since the apostles can still use the stronger modern cases (pp. 260–262). He is not a physician and relies on those who are. His cases are samples, many known to him through his wife's family or his students, and most lack medical records. He also asks his critics to state their own standard. Rex Gardner, a physician, put it this way: "If you do not accept those two statements, you may ask yourself what evidence you would be prepared to accept" (p. 426).

The two theses carry different weight. The primary thesis is hard to dispute, given the surveys, the interviews and a history running from Augustine to the present. It removes a common reason for treating the Gospel miracles as late legend. The secondary thesis depends on one's starting point. Keener concedes that someone committed to naturalism can always find another explanation. His reply is that "a possible explanation is not always the most plausible or probable explanation," and that "multiple raisings following prayer in the same circles would seem to defy any reasonable probability of mere accident" (p. 763). The appendix on possession goes beyond what the main argument needs, as Keener himself says, and readers who accept his theism may still reject his openness to other spirits (p. 837).

### Conclusion

Keener's main claim is historical: eyewitnesses today report the kinds of event the Gospels report, so "there seems no reason, based on the principle of historical analogy, to deny that first-century eyewitnesses could have believed that they saw Jesus heal blind eyes, make paralytics walk, or raise the dead" (p. 761). The principle of analogy that Troeltsch and Bultmann used against the miracles now supports them. Hume's appeal to uniform experience cannot stand when "hundreds of millions of claims would have to be satisfactorily explained in nonsupernatural terms" (p. 764). For his secondary claim, Keener argues that some cases are better explained by divine action than by chance, misdiagnosis or suggestion, and that refusing even to consider this is a presupposition, not a finding. He ends with G. B. Caird, who said that Luke, though often accused of credulity, should be commended "for his faithful reproduction of one of the major constituents of early Christianity" (p. 765). His postscript describes men in Brazzaville with legs "like shriveled sticks." For him, he says, the question is no longer whether God heals but why so many who need healing go without it (pp. 766–767).
