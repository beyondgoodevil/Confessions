In "The Origin of the Work of Art," Heidegger argues that a great work of art is not a pretty object or a copy of something but a place where **truth happens**: it opens up a **world**. His most famous example, a pair of shoes in a painting by Van Gogh, started one of the great quarrels in art theory. **Meyer Schapiro** showed the shoes were Van Gogh's own, not a peasant woman's, and **Jacques Derrida** asked why both men needed to give the shoes back to an owner at all. Here's how it works:

### 1. The Circle

Heidegger starts with a puzzle. "The artist is the origin of the work. The work is the origin of the artist. Neither is without the other." Both depend on a third thing, **art**. But we can only learn what art is from works, and only recognise works if we know what art is. "Thus we are compelled to follow the circle. This is neither a makeshift nor a defect. To enter upon this path is the strength of thought" (pp. 17–18).

He then distinguishes three kinds of being:

1. **Mere things** (a stone, a clod of earth).
2. **Equipment** (a jug, an axe, shoes): things made for use, a matter shaped by a form.
3. **Works** of art.

The usual way of thinking about all things as "formed matter," he argues, really comes from equipment, so it hides what both things and works are.

### 2. The Peasant Shoes

To describe equipment "without any philosophical theory," he takes "a well-known painting by Van Gogh, who painted such shoes several times" (pp. 32–33). At first there is nothing to see: no soil, no field, "a pair of peasant shoes and nothing more. And yet—"

> "From the dark opening of the worn insides of the shoes the toilsome tread of the worker stares forth ... In the shoes vibrates the silent call of the earth, its quiet gift of the ripening grain and its unexplained self-refusal in the fallow desolation of the wintry field." (p. 33)

- **Reliability.** The peasant woman doesn't think about her shoes; she just wears them, and "she knows all this without noticing." Usefulness rests on something deeper, ***reliability***: "by virtue of the reliability of the equipment she is sure of her world." Equipment belongs to the **earth** and is protected in the **world** of the peasant woman (pp. 33–34).
- **The painting did it.** We learned this "not by a description and explanation of a pair of shoes actually present ... but only by bringing ourselves before Van Gogh's painting. This painting spoke." It "would be the worst self-deception" to think we projected it all into the painting (p. 35).

### 3. Art as Truth, Not Copying

> "Van Gogh's painting is the disclosure of what the equipment, the pair of peasant shoes, is in truth ... The nature of art would then be this: the truth of beings setting itself to work." (p. 36)

- **Truth as unconcealment.** "The Greeks called the unconcealedness of beings ***aletheia***." Truth here is not correctness (a statement matching a fact) but things **coming out of hiddenness** into the open.
- **Not imitation.** Heidegger rejects the "fortunately obsolete view that art is an imitation and depiction of reality." He also rejects the idea that the work copies a general essence: "Who could maintain the impossible view that the Idea of Temple is represented in the building?" (pp. 36–37). Compare Plato's painter "third from the king" in [[Plato's Quarrel With the Poets: Republic X Read Three Ways]]: Heidegger turns the charge round. The work isn't a copy of truth; it is where truth first happens.

### 4. The Temple: World and Earth

To get away from representation altogether, he chooses a work that depicts nothing.

> "A building, a Greek temple, portrays nothing. It simply stands there in the middle of the rock-cleft valley." (p. 40)

1. **It opens a world.** The temple lets the god be present and "gathers around itself the unity of those paths and relations in which birth and death, disaster and blessing, victory and disgrace ... acquire the shape of destiny for human being." That open context is "the world of this historical people" (pp. 40–41). "The world worlds": it isn't a collection of things but the ever-unobjective setting of our decisions. "A stone is worldless" (pp. 43–44).
2. **It sets forth the earth.** Standing on the rock, the temple "draws up out of the rock the mystery of that rock's clumsy yet spontaneous support." Holding against the storm, it "first makes the storm itself manifest"; its gleaming stone "first brings to light the light of the day." The Greeks called this emerging *phusis*; Heidegger calls the sheltering ground the ***earth*** (p. 41). The sculptor uses stone without using it up, and the painter's colour "only now comes to shine forth" (p. 46).
3. **Strife.** "The world grounds itself on the earth, and earth juts through world." The world, opening, can't bear anything closed; the earth, sheltering, draws everything back in. Their opposition is a **striving**, but not mere discord: each raises the other into its own nature. The work doesn't settle it "in an insipid agreement, but so that the strife may remain a strife" (pp. 47–48).

- **Beauty.** Where does beauty fit? "This shining, joined in the work, is the beautiful. Beauty is one way in which truth occurs as unconcealedness" (p. 54). Beauty isn't the goal of art; it is truth shining.

### 5. Art Is Poetry; Preservers; Hegel

- **"All art ... is, as such, essentially poetry"** (p. 70). He means *Dichtung* in the broad sense of founding: art breaks open "an open place, in whose openness everything is other than usual." "The nature of art is poetry. The nature of poetry, in turn, is the founding of truth" (p. 72).
- **Preservers.** A work needs people who let themselves be moved out of routine into what it discloses: not connoisseurs who appreciate it "aesthetically," but people who stand in the truth it opens.
- **Hegel's verdict.** In his epilogue Heidegger quotes Hegel's *Aesthetics*: art "is and remains for us, on the side of its highest vocation, something past." He says the question isn't settled by the fact that new art keeps appearing: "is art still an essential and necessary way in which that truth happens which is decisive for our historical existence?" (pp. 78–79).

### 6. Schapiro: Whose Shoes?

In 1968 the art historian Meyer Schapiro asked Heidegger which painting he meant. Heidegger wrote back: one he saw in an exhibition in Amsterdam in March 1930, which Schapiro identifies as de la Faille no. 255.

1. **They aren't a peasant's shoes.** Of eight shoe paintings, only three show "the dark openings of the worn insides," and "they are clearly pictures of the artist's own shoes, not the shoes of a peasant," painted in Paris in 1886–87, when Van Gogh was "a man of the town and city" (pp. 296–297).
2. **Heidegger projected.** Turning his words back on him: "Alas for him, the philosopher has indeed deceived himself." The associations with peasants and soil "are not sustained by the picture itself but are grounded rather in his own social outlook with its heavy pathos of the primordial and earthy. He has indeed 'imagined everything and projected it into the painting'" (p. 297).
3. **The painting adds nothing.** "I find nothing in Heidegger's fanciful description ... that could not have been imagined in looking at a real pair of peasants' shoes." So the example doesn't support his theory of art (p. 297).
4. **The painting is a self-portrait.** Heidegger missed "the artist's presence in the work." Van Gogh turns his own worn shoes toward us, "individual and wrinkled ... veridical portraits of aging shoes," "a piece from a self-portrait." Schapiro compares Knut Hamsun's narrator in *Hunger*, who sees his shoes as "a ghost of my other I" (pp. 297–298).
5. **Gauguin's story.** Gauguin remembered a pair of muddy hob-nailed shoes in Van Gogh's studio in Arles. Vincent told him he had worn them when, as a young preacher, he went to the miners of the Borinage, where he nursed a burned miner back to life. "For van Gogh the shoes were a piece of his own life" (pp. 299–300).

### 7. Derrida: Restitutions

Derrida's dialogue (from *The Truth in Painting*, 1978) begins by agreeing the passage is "ridiculous and lamentable": a great thinker suddenly turns into a local guide on a coach tour, pointing out of the window at the fields. But then he turns on Schapiro.

1. **Heidegger wasn't analysing a picture.** At that stage of his argument, "real shoes ... or shoes drawn vaguely in chalk on the blackboard would have rendered the same service." What he says about equipment would hold equally for town shoes. The "peasant" detail is a secondary, if telling, projection.
2. **Schapiro does the same thing.** Heidegger gives the shoes to a peasant woman; Schapiro "restitutes" them to Van Gogh. Derrida finds "three dogmas": painted shoes can belong to a real, nameable owner; shoes are simply shoes, fitted to feet; feet belong to a body. All three, he says, "can't stand up to the slightest question." The word "evidently," the words "his own," hammer in a clarity that isn't there (p. 310).
3. **Are they even a pair?** Both men assume "a pair." But shoes are detachable from feet and from each other, and painted shoes belong to no one. Derrida even asks why Heidegger's peasant is usually a **woman**, which Schapiro never notices.

- **Significance:** Preziosi presents the three texts as a "polylogue." Heidegger claims art discloses truth, Schapiro brings it back to history and the artist's life, and Derrida shows that every interpretation, the philosopher's and the historian's alike, wants to **attach** the painting to something outside it.

### 8. Assessment

- **Schapiro wins on the facts.** The shoes were very probably Van Gogh's, and the peasant pathos belongs to Heidegger. Schapiro's mention of Heidegger's "social outlook" also hints at the darker context of the "historical people" and its "native ground" in 1935.
- **Heidegger still has a point.** Derrida is right that the argument about equipment doesn't depend on whose shoes they are. And the temple section, where nothing is represented at all, survives Schapiro untouched. The idea that great works **open a world** rather than copy one is Heidegger's lasting contribution.
- **A theological echo.** Heidegger's temple "lets the god himself be present." That is close to what the Orthodox say of the **icon**: not a portrait for information but a presence that opens the world of the Kingdom (see [[Theology of the Icon]]). The difference is that Heidegger's temple is a work only "as long as the god has not fled from it" (p. 42), and he doubts the gods are still present, while the icon depends on the Incarnation (see [[Heidegger and the God Beyond Being]]).

### Conclusion

Heidegger wants to free art from aesthetics. A work is not a beautiful object or a copy but a happening of truth: it opens a world and sets forth the earth, and holds them in strife. Beauty is how that truth shines. His example of Van Gogh's shoes made the point vivid but, as Schapiro showed, rested on a projection: the shoes were the painter's own, a piece of self-portrait bound up with his life. Derrida's reply is that the deeper error, shared by both, is the urge to restore the shoes to an owner. The temple, which portrays nothing, remains Heidegger's best argument.
