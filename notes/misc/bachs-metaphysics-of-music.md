Bach didn't just write music *for* the church; he seems to have thought that **music itself**, when it is composed and performed as perfectly as possible, **reflects and even makes present the order of God**. John Butt calls this Bach's **"musico-centric"** view, and the best way to understand it is to put it next to Luther's theology of music and the rationalist philosophers of Bach's own age, **Leibniz, Wolff and Spinoza**. Here's how it works:

### 1. Luther: Music Next to Theology

Bach's theological world was **Lutheran**, and Leaver stresses that this matters. Lutheranism wasn't the same as the Calvinist or Anglican Reformations, and a lot of English writing on Bach quietly reads him through the author's own church.

- **Bach signed up to it formally.** Before becoming Thomaskantor in Leipzig he was examined in theology, and on 13 May 1723 he subscribed to the **Formula of Concord**, twice: once affirming Lutheran doctrine and once rejecting non-Lutheran beliefs.
- **Music is a gift of God (*donum Dei*), not a human invention.** Unlike the other Reformers, who were wary of music, Luther wrote (preface to Rhau's *Symphoniae iucundae*, 1538) that "next to the Word of God, music deserves the highest praise." In the *Table Talk*: "I place music next to theology and give it the highest praise."
- **Luther even does theology in musical terms.** He compares the **Gospel** to the unwritten adjustments singers make in performance and the **Law** to the written notes, which must give way to them. Josquin's music, he says, flows freely and isn't cramped by rules, "like the song of the finch."
- **The liturgy kept music central.** Luther kept the shape of the Latin Mass but had the congregation sing **vernacular hymns** (chorales), including chorale versions of the Kyrie, Gloria, Creed and Agnus Dei, plus a hymn for each part of the Catechism. These are the melodies Bach set again and again; *Clavier-Übung III* has organ settings of the Kyrie and Gloria chorales and two complete cycles on the catechism hymns.

**Law and Gospel shape the cantatas.** Leaver points out a pattern: the opening chorus states the problem (we stand under the Law's condemnation), the recitatives and arias explore it, a movement then gives the **Gospel** answer, and the closing chorale endorses it. So many cantatas are little sermons with a plot.

### 2. Was Bach Actually Pious?

There have been two opposite pictures of Bach, and Leaver thinks both are wrong.

1. **The "Fifth Evangelist"**: the late nineteenth century turned Bach into the supreme pious Lutheran cantor, practically an author of the New Testament.
2. **The agnostic Bach**: in 1962 Friedrich Blume argued that Bach worked for the church only because it was the most convenient place for his talents. "The staunch Lutheran," he said, "is a legend." New datings of the cantatas seemed to show that Bach stopped writing church music around 1730.

What undid Blume was Bach's own copy of **Abraham Calov's annotated Bible** (with Luther's commentary), which was shown publicly in 1969. His notes in it were written for nobody but himself. Next to 1 Chronicles 29:21 he wrote that this was splendid proof that music, along with the rest of worship, "was instituted by the Spirit of God through David." Next to 2 Chronicles 5:13 (the glory of the Lord filling the Temple when the singers and trumpeters praised God):

> "NB. Where there is devotional music, God with his grace is always present."

Add his large theological library (two editions of Luther's collected works) and the fact that he was still working on the **B minor Mass** at the end of his life, and Leaver's conclusion is that both pictures are **constructs of later ages**. Bach was a fairly typical middle-class Lutheran with an unusual attachment to Luther's writings.

### 3. The Problem: Is Music Holy in Itself?

Here is where it gets interesting. Butt argues that the Lutheran tradition never quite settled **what music is**.

- **On the one hand**, music was praised as part of God's ordered creation. Writers kept quoting **Wisdom 11:21**, that God ordered everything by measure, number and weight, and some called music a **foretaste of heaven**.
- **On the other hand**, worship music was officially *adiaphora* (neither commanded nor forbidden), and **the text came first**. One defender of music, Frick (1631), thought good music with a bad text could be rescued by giving it a good text. Butt's point: music is then a **"promiscuous signifier"** that can serve God or the devil equally well.
- **Pietists** went further and attacked elaborate, operatic church music altogether, and even Orthodox musicians like Kuhnau (Bach's predecessor) were suspicious of arias and recitatives.

Bach's own position shows up in the **thoroughbass primer of 1738**, paraphrasing the theorist Niedt: the aim of all music is "the praise of God and the recreation of the soul," and where that is forgotten "there is no true music, only a devilish bawling and droning." Notice that this is a book about **keyboard harmony**, not texted church music. So Bach seems to have held the old **Pythagorean** view that well-made music as such is sacred.

> [!definition] Musico-centric (Butt)
> The view that "the very substance of music both reflects and embodies the ultimate reality of God and the Universe." Music can carry a text and persuade like rhetoric, but it is **not subservient** to the text.

**The danger:** to say outright that God is *immanent* in music would have sounded like **pantheism** to an Orthodox Lutheran, since it blurs the line between God and the world, and it puts music almost level with Scripture. Butt thinks the Calov note above ("God with his grace is always present") comes closest to saying exactly that.

### 4. The Scheibe–Birnbaum Quarrel

In 1737 Bach's former pupil **Johann Adolph Scheibe** criticised him in print. Bach's music had too much art, it was turgid (*Schwulst*, a word borrowed from Gottsched's attacks on overwrought Baroque poetry), every ornament was written out, and all the voices were equally difficult so that no principal voice could be heard. He called Bach a *Musikant*, a practical musician rather than a learned *Musicus*.

The reply came from **Johann Abraham Birnbaum**, a Leipzig lawyer probably working with material from Bach himself. His defence shows what Bach's circle thought music was:

1. **The rules of music are part of nature.** "Where the rules of composition are most strictly observed, there without fail order must reign."
2. **Composition is craft, not genius.** Birnbaum is the first source for Bach's saying that what he had achieved by industry and practice, anyone with tolerable talent could achieve.
3. **A work should realise all its "perfections"** (*Vollkommenheiten*). A theme carries a whole set of possibilities, and the piece is complete when they have been worked out. C. P. E. Bach tells how his father, listening to a fugue, would predict which contrapuntal devices could be applied and nudge his son happily when they came.
4. **Art doesn't just imitate nature, it improves it.** Nature comes to us misshapen, and art gives it the beauty it lacks.

- **Significance:** neither Scheibe nor Birnbaum mentions God at all. Butt's reading is that God is behind every line anyway, as the source of music's rules and the model of perfection: God is "so immanent that he virtually no longer needs to be mentioned."

### 5. Leibniz and Wolff: "A Mind Unconscious That It Is Calculating"

Bach's associate **Lorenz Mizler**, a follower of Leibniz and Wolff, printed Leibniz's famous definition in his *Musikalische Bibliothek*:

> "Music is the hidden arithmetical exercise of a mind unconscious that it is calculating."

Butt is careful to say there's no evidence Bach read the philosophers. The claim is that **Bach thought musically the way they thought metaphysically**:

1. **Leibniz's complete concept.** For Leibniz the concept of a subject already contains all its predicates; God chose Adam with all his posterity at once. Bach treats a **theme** the same way: everything the piece will do is already implied in it, partly by necessity (the rules of counterpoint) and partly by free choice.
2. **Variety with order.** The *Monadology* (§§57–58) says God's world gives "as much variety as possible, but with the greatest order possible." Butt thinks this sums up Bach exactly: the pull towards variety (all the styles, forms and forces of the *St Matthew Passion*) against the pull towards unity.
3. **Dissonance makes order shine.** Leibniz used music to explain evil: a mix of dissonance and consonance is more pleasing than bland consonance, because the restored order stands out. J. G. Walther, Bach's Weimar colleague and cousin by marriage, wrote that "Dissonances are the night, consonances are the day."
4. **Wolff's perfection.** Wolff (the most widely read German philosopher of the time) defined a work as a thing that has fulfilled its possibilities. True pleasure comes from seeing its perfections; enjoying it without understanding gives only "apparent pleasure." Butt suggests Birnbaum took Wolff's idea of perfection as **many correspondences**, while Scheibe took Wolff's idea of perfection as **fitness for a purpose** (clear text, proper style). Same philosopher, opposite verdicts on Bach.

Walther's night and day, in the simplest possible form. This is my own example, not Bach's: a **4–3 suspension**, where a note held over from one chord clashes with the next and then resolves.

### 6. Spinoza: God as a Verb

Butt's boldest claim is that **Spinoza** fits Bach even better than Leibniz, although Bach could never have admitted it (Spinoza was excommunicated by his synagogue and shunned by mainstream thought). Leibniz's God is a supreme mathematician who sets things up and waits. **Spinoza's God is wholly immanent**, the indwelling cause of all things: "a Verb" rather than a fixed subject.

- **Method.** Spinoza's *Ethics* claims to follow by necessity from its first definitions; the process matters as much as the conclusions. Bach's pieces work out the necessities of their themes in the same way, and both men thought the result was hard because it was rare: "all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare."
- **Perfection is reality.** For Spinoza, the more perfection a thing has, the more real it is. Bach's endless **revising** of finished pieces and his drive to make **complete collections** (cantata cycles, the late variations, fugues and canons) look like the same instinct.
- **One substance.** Spinoza has one substance with many modes. Bach treated every style of his day, from the lightest galant to strict *stile antico*, as modes of **one thing called music**. Butt even suggests treating Bach's whole output as a **single work**, with the separate compositions as literally "pieces".
- **Intuition.** Spinoza's highest kind of knowledge is intuition, seeing a truth at once instead of calculating it, which leads to "the intellectual love of God." Bach could apparently grasp an idea and all its consequences in a single moment.
- **Emotion transcended.** Bach's music is as emotional as any of its time, but the affects become part of the musical substance, controlled and worked through. Hence the very Lutheran habit of setting the believer's acceptance of **death** to cheerful, dance-like music.

Butt ends by refusing to claim influence. Using Leibniz's own image, Bach and the philosophers agree like two perfectly made clocks, set to the same time by God, "or is it culture?"

### 7. The Signature: B-A-C-H

Bach marked church works *J.J.* (*Jesu juva*, "Jesus, help") at the start and *S.D.G.* (*Soli Deo gloria*) at the end, and some secular ones too. But his most famous signature is in the notes. In German note names B is B-flat and **H is B-natural**, so his name spells a four-note theme. In the last fugue of ***The Art of Fugue***, a set of fugues and canons all built from one main theme, Bach introduces a new subject spelling **B-A-C-H**, and the manuscript breaks off before he finishes working it out (DK, *The Art of Fugue*).

```abc
Q:1/4=60
K:C
_B A c =B |]
```

My own reading, putting this together with Butt: it fits the "musico-centric" Bach that his name ends up **inside the counterpoint**, as one more subject to be worked out by the rules, rather than on a title page. The composer becomes part of the order he was serving.

### 8. An Orthodox Assessment

- **What's right.** Bach's instinct that music is part of the **ordered goodness of creation**, and not just decoration for a text, goes back past Luther to the Pythagorean and patristic idea of a cosmos built on number and harmony. Luther's "music next to theology" is close to the Fathers' sense that creation itself praises God.
- **The false dilemma.** Butt frames the choice as either a **transcendent** God (so music is only a tool) or an **immanent** God (which slides towards Spinoza's pantheism). Orthodox theology has a third option: God is unknowable in his **essence** but truly present in his **uncreated energies**, and Maximus speaks of the **logoi** of creation, the divine intentions in each created thing. On that view, "where there is devotional music, God with his grace is always present" doesn't need Spinoza. Grace is God himself, present without the creature becoming God.
- **The limit.** In Orthodox worship the **word** stays primary (chant serves the text, and instruments aren't used), so the Lutheran worry about music competing with Scripture isn't wrong. Bach's untexted music is probably best seen as **natural theology in sound**, praising God through the order of creation, rather than as revelation.

### Conclusion

Bach's piety was real (the Formula of Concord, the Calov Bible notes, the B minor Mass), and Luther gave him a theology in which music stands next to the Word. But Bach went further than his tradition was comfortable with: he treated music's own substance, its rules, perfections and resolved dissonances, as reflecting and even making present the order of God. Leibniz gives the best analogy for how he composed (a theme containing all its consequences, variety held in order), and Spinoza for how he saw music as a whole (one substance, perfection as reality, God immanent in the process). Whether that is theology, philosophy or just how a supreme craftsman thinks, Butt leaves open, and so does the unfinished fugue on Bach's own name.
