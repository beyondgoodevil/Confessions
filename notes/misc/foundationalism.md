### What is Foundationalism?

Foundationalism is an epistemological theory that holds that knowledge and justified beliefs are ultimately based on **basic beliefs** (or **foundational beliefs**) that do not require further justification. These foundational beliefs serve as the bedrock upon which all other beliefs are built.

### Examples of Foundationalist Principles:

1. **Law of Non-Contradiction** – "A proposition cannot be both true and false at the same time in the same sense."
2. **Reliability of Sense Perception** – "Our senses provide us with generally accurate information about the external world."
3. **Existence of the Self** – "I exist as a thinking subject (e.g., Descartes' *cogito, ergo sum*)."

### Critique of Foundationalism:

1. **Self-Evidence is Not Universal** – Foundationalism claims that basic beliefs are self-evident, yet history shows that these so-called "self-evident" principles have been **debated for over two millennia**. If a belief were truly self-evident, it would be universally accepted without question. The fact that foundational principles have been subject to deep philosophical dispute suggests they are not inherently self-evident.
2. **Dependence on Prior Assumptions** – Foundationalist principles often rely on **unstated prior assumptions** rather than being truly self-justifying. For example, the belief in the reliability of sense perception assumes that our cognitive faculties function correctly and are not systematically deceived. This assumption itself requires justification, which contradicts foundationalism’s claim that certain beliefs need no further support.
