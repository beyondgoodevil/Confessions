> "It was not for you that I was waiting here in these mountains." (Zarathustra to his guests, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* IV, "The Welcome")

> "'Pity! Pity for the superior human!' he cried out, and his visage was transformed into bronze. 'Well then! That– has had its time!'" (IV, "The Sign")

The fourth part of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* is the strangest. Zarathustra is old and white-haired. He has taught no one the eternal return. On a single day a cry of distress brings a crowd of visitors to his mountain: two kings with an ass, a scientist who studies the brain of the leech, an old magician, the last pope, the ugliest man, a beggar who preaches to cows, Zarathustra's own shadow, and the soothsayer who announced them. Zarathustra calls them "higher men" (*höhere Menschen*, which Parkes renders "superior humans" and Lampert "superior man"). He feeds them a "Last Supper", preaches to them, leaves them alone, comes back to find them worshipping the ass, blesses the festival, sings them the midnight song, and the next morning abandons them when a laughing lion appears. Nietzsche called the part "perhaps unprintable" and a "blasphemy" written "with a buffoon's whim" (letter to Gast, 14 February 1885, quoted in Meier, p. 129). It was printed privately in forty-five copies. Its readers disagree about almost everything: whether it is an afterthought or the book's real ending, whether the higher men are Nietzsche's contemporaries or pieces of Nietzsche himself, whether the Ass Festival is a defeat or a cure, and what Zarathustra's "last sin" actually was. This note goes through the figures and the events, then compares six readings: Laurence Lampert, Stanley Rosen, Leo Strauss, Heinrich Meier, Matthew Meyer (who also reports Paul Loeb's), and Douglas Burnham with Martin Jesinghausen. Quotations from the text follow Parkes unless a commentator's translation is given.

### 1. The Plan of Part IV

**The temptation.** The motto of Part IV comes from Part II ("On the Pitying"): God died of his pity for mankind. In *Ecce Homo* Nietzsche named the plot: "I have composed a case, as the 'Temptation of Zarathustra,' in which a great cry of need reaches him, in which pity sets upon him like a last sin that wants to entice him away from himself" (quoted in Meier, p. 130). In the second chapter the soothsayer says outright that he has come "to seduce" Zarathustra to his last sin, and that its name is pity. Meier calls this the "experimental design": "Will Zarathustra resist the temptation of pity and retain his heights, or will he make out like the God who died of his pity for men?" (p. 135).

**The shape.** Meier sets out the architecture (p. 133, n. 171). Part IV is the longest part by pages and the shortest by time: three days. Chapter 1 ("The Honey Sacrifice") is a prologue on the first day. Chapter 2 ("The Cry of Distress") is a prelude. Chapters 3–9 are a first group of seven encounters, in the forests below the cave. Chapter 10 ("At Midday") closes the first half. Chapter 11 ("The Welcome") opens the second. Chapters 12–18 are a second group of seven, inside the cave. Chapter 19 ("The Night-Wanderer's Song") closes the second half at midnight, and chapter 20 ("The Sign") is an epilogue on the third morning.

**The genre.** Meier writes that "the drama's fourth act necessarily becomes comedy" (p. 129). Meyer reads Parts I–III as a tragic trilogy and Part IV as the satyr play that followed three tragedies at the Dionysia (p. 266). Burnham and Jesinghausen agree that the satyr play, "the deflating fourth part of Greek tragic tetralogies, is the appropriate form" (p. 170). Strauss, by contrast, calls Part IV "in a way an anticlimax": "the whole substance ... has been developed in the first three parts" (p. 229). Lampert calls it an "interlude" added "as an afterthought" (p. 7).

**The honey and the fisherman.** Part IV opens with a ruse. Zarathustra tells his animals he will make a honey sacrifice on the mountaintop. Alone, he laughs: the sacrifice "was merely a ruse of my speech and, verily, a useful foolishness" (Meier's translation, p. 131). The honey is bait. He will fish for men with "a golden fishing rod" instead of going down to them. Rosen calls the ruse "a trick and a useful folly (like a noble lie)" and notes the echo of Christ the fisher of souls (pp. 209–10). Lampert calls it a mask worn "as an act of courtesy" even before his animals (p. 292). Meyer links the fishing to Zarathustra's self-description as "a raiser, cultivator and disciplinarian" who once told himself "Become who you are!" (pp. 264–65).

### 2. Who Are the Higher Men?

The text never defines the term, and the commentators' definitions differ.

- **Burnham and Jesinghausen.** They are "'superior' or 'higher' in that they stand out from the all-too-many through having internalised, made part of their mode of life, some aspect of Zarathustra's teaching" (p. 169). They are "the first fruit of Zarathustra's teachings", who share his "great disgust" at the present (p. 173). But "they are also self-portraits", modes of humanity Zarathustra has overcome in himself, and "thus enemies, too" (p. 173).
- **Lampert.** "All who appear in part IV are above the mass, but each in his own way bears indelible traces of the elevation of the mass" (p. 293). They are "superior contemporaries who are still suffering from the modern world" (p. 310).
- **Meyer, against Loeb and Tinsley.** Paul Loeb and Ryan Tinsley (2022) define higher humans as those able and eager to rule others, and read the self-loathing of Part IV's visitors as failure to rule. Meyer finds this unconvincing. He reverses the causation: "These ill-constituted (*missrathen*) higher humans are incapable of ruling precisely because they are filled with self-loathing" (p. 268). Higher humans differ from "the good and the just", who enforce convention, and from "last humans", who show "a cow-like contentment". Higher humans long "for something more" and can therefore suffer existential nausea. By that test Zarathustra is a higher human too, but a well-constituted one, because he has learned to love himself (pp. 268–69). The others "want Zarathustra to provide them with the love they cannot feel for themselves. In short, they want Zarathustra's pity and love" (p. 269).
- **Rosen.** "Zarathustra's guests are fragments of his own nature who have to be purified and in that way reunited into his own destiny" (p. 216). And "Zarathustra himself encompasses in his own soul all types of the higher man; but he himself suffers thereby from their defects" (p. 227).
- **Meier.** "The higher man that the soothsayer had announced is not One" (p. 153). Each guest "carries something of him", in agreement or conflict, and helps clarify "what Zarathustra is, what he wants to be, and what he does not want to be" (p. 137).

### 4. The Encounters

**The soothsayer.** He appears as a second shadow beside Zarathustra's own. Rosen reads the two shadows as "two possible outcomes of Nietzsche's teaching" (p. 211). He announces the cry of need and claims there are no more blessed isles. Zarathustra answers "No! No! Three times no!" and goes to look for the crier. Burnham and Jesinghausen read the scene as "a mirror image" of the Devil tempting Christ in the wilderness: here the tempter wants Zarathustra to fall back into the old virtue of pity (p. 172). They add that Zarathustra's eagerness to protect everyone "in his realm" already expresses "a virtue already dangerously close to pity" (p. 173).

**The kings.** Two kings lead an ass laden with wine. The king on the right rages against the "rabble" and the court's false manners: "Ah, disgust! disgust! disgust! What do we kings matter any more!" They want to bring the ass to the higher man, "for the highest man should also be the highest ruler on earth" (Meier's translation, p. 137). Meier hears Plato's philosopher-kings behind this (*Republic* 473c) and notes that the cry of disgust repeats Zarathustra's own in "The Convalescent" (pp. 137–38). Lampert says the kings introduce "the political theme of the rule of mass man in modern democracy, one of the main themes of part IV", and that the ass recalls the one brought to Jesus for his entry into Jerusalem (p. 293). Meyer reads the kings' disgust as the shame of rulers "not worthy of ruling", not as frustration at lacking power (p. 270). Rosen calls them "reactionary spokesmen who represent a partial and distorted reflection of Nietzsche's critique of modernity" (p. 214).

**The leech.** Zarathustra treads on a man lying in a swamp, his arm bleeding from leeches. He is "the conscientious in spirit", whose world is the brain of the leech: "How long have I been pursuing this one thing, the brain of the leech, that the slippery truth might here no longer slip away from me!" He took his motto from Zarathustra: spirit is "the life that itself cuts into life". Strauss: "he was his own leech", and this is "the man of science in the highest sense", whose "intellectual honesty at all costs" is "in a way the highest of which man of the nineteenth century is capable". But there is "infinitely less science than in Lucretius's poem. There is no view of the whole anymore" (pp. 230–31). Rosen: "science sucks out the life blood, whereas Zarathustra restores it. ... He is a false master" (p. 215). Meier sees "a caricature of himself" in the scene and draws the lesson: "Probity is not enough" (pp. 139–40). Meyer notes that this probity was once part of Nietzsche's own free-spirit project, which he later overcame (p. 272).

**The magician.** An old man thrashes on the ground and sings a lament to "the unknown God", his "hangman-God", his "pain" and "last happiness". Zarathustra beats him with his stick and calls him an actor and a counterfeiter. The magician admits he was playing the "penitent in spirit", then confesses: "I am weary, my arts disgust me, I am not great" (Meier's translation, p. 141). The commentators agree that this is Wagner. Lampert calls the scene "Nietzsche contra Wagner ... artistically transformed into Zarathustra contra the Old Sorcerer, though Nietzsche ensures that what happens between Zarathustra and the Old Sorcerer turns out better for both", and an instance of "the old rivalry between poetry and philosophy" (p. 294). The song itself is not disowned. Nietzsche later published it as "Ariadne's Lament" in the *Dionysus Dithyrambs*, with the god named as Dionysus (Lampert p. 295; Meier p. 141 n. 179; B&J p. 175). Meier asks: "Is the seeking of greatness Zarathustra's true temptation?" (p. 141).

**The last pope.** He served "this old God until his last hour" and now wanders "retired from service, without a master, and yet not free". He sought the forest saint of the Prologue and found him dead. Now he seeks "the most pious of all those who do not believe in God". Lampert calls the pair "two old theologians of the death of God" (p. 296). The pope says God choked on his own pity. Zarathustra adds that "when gods die, they always die many kinds of death". He also charges the God who "took revenge on his pots and creatures for having bungled them himself" with "a sin against good taste" (Meier, p. 143). The pope replies that Zarathustra's unbelief is itself a piety. Meier notes that only here does Zarathustra take a stranger's hand and look at it "for a long time with admiration" (p. 142). Burnham and Jesinghausen read the pope's single eye through the *Theologia Germanica*, in which the right eye looks on eternity and the left on time. The pope is blind in both: the left from neglect of the world, the right "because there is nothing to see" (p. 176).

**The ugliest man.** In a dead valley where old green serpents go to die, Zarathustra sees something "shaped like a man and yet hardly like a man, something inexpressible". He blushes with shame and turns to leave. The thing gurgles a riddle: "What is the revenge on the witness?" Pity fells Zarathustra "like an oak tree", but he rises at once and answers: "you are the murderer of God!" The ugliest man then gives the longest speech of the encounters. He fled the pity of everyone else: "Your shame, O Zarathustra, did me honour!" And he explains the murder: "He saw me always: on such a witness I wanted to take revenge– or else not live myself. The God who saw everything, even the human: this God had to die!"

- *Strauss*: if there is a perfect being, man "stands naked in his imperfection before God. He cannot be proud." Because "the positing of God is itself an act of the spirit of revenge, the action of the ugliest man is the revenge on the spirit of revenge. It is therefore an imperfect form of Zarathustra's own teaching" (p. 233).
- *Meier*: Zarathustra's single blush, which appears nowhere else in the book, "marks the noble trait in the prophet's constitution that conflicts with the philosopher's passion of knowledge" (p. 145). The ugliest man is the only interlocutor who deciphers the subtitle: Zarathustra warned against pity for "not All, not None, but you and your kind" (p. 146).
- *Lampert*: "shame honors the Ugliest Man, whereas pity shames him" (p. 297).
- *Rosen*: the murder is "an act of desperation rather than of overcoming"; "what he requires is not forgiveness, but the purification of acceptance" (p. 221).
- *Burnham and Jesinghausen*: "the murdering of God is not a redemption of the ugliest man, but the summation of his ugliness" (p. 177).
- *Meyer*: he resembles the pale criminal of Part I, unequal to his own deed. He has not become a god to justify the murder (GS 125) (p. 276).

Zarathustra leaves musing that he has found no one "who has despised himself more deeply" and wonders whether this was the higher man whose cry he heard.

**The voluntary beggar.** On a hill, a "sermonizer on the mount" sits among cows, having given away great riches. He preaches: "Except we be converted, and become as cows, we shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven" (Meier's translation, p. 147, parodying Matthew 18:3). The poor have become a mob in revolt, and he hopes to learn rumination from the cows as a cure for "the great disgust". He greets Zarathustra as "the man without disgust". Meier calls him "a caricature of Jesus", instructed by history that Christianity "has not overcome disgust, but has instead increased it" (p. 147). Burnham and Jesinghausen see "Jesus as social egalitarian", aimed at both socialism and the "religious egalitarianism of the Strauss and Feuerbach kind" (p. 178). Meyer reads the cows through the second *Untimely Meditation*: they live unhistorically, and the beggar wants to escape the "it was" by becoming a cow (p. 278). Strauss calls the scene a "travesty on Zarathustra's return to nature", with cows in place of eagle and serpent (p. 233).

**The shadow.** The last visitor runs after Zarathustra: his own shadow, a wanderer "without goal, also without home". He followed Zarathustra into every forbidden place and took as his slogan "Nothing is true, everything is permitted." He has lost "the innocence of the good and their noble lies". Zarathustra calls him "free spirit and wanderer" and warns him that "finally even a prison seems bliss" to such unsteady ones: "Beware that a narrow belief does not capture you in the end" (Meier's translation, p. 149). Strauss calls him "the nihilist" and, with the next chapter, "the centers of the fourth part" (p. 233). Meyer notes that "free spirit" and "wanderer" are Nietzsche's own earlier self-descriptions (*Human, All Too Human*, *The Wanderer and His Shadow*). The shadow is what Nietzsche would have been without a home in himself (p. 279). Meier judges the warning about narrow beliefs, "looking back over a century or two, ... clear-sighted" (p. 149).

### 5. Midday, the Welcome, and the Last Supper

**At Midday.** Between the two groups of seven, Zarathustra lies under a tree wrapped in a grapevine and feels that "the world is perfect". Meier calls this "the philosopher's return to himself" and stresses that this judgment "finds expression in no other passage" (pp. 149, 151). The happiness of the perfect, natural midday differs from the "great midday" of the prophet's historical hope (p. 149). Then Zarathustra forces himself awake.

**The Welcome.** Back at his cave he hears the cry of distress again, now made "of many voices". He finds all his guests inside, the ugliest man wearing a crown and two purple belts. Zarathustra recognises that they are the higher men, and tells them that what they need is "a good gay buffoon, a dancer and wind and wildcat, some old fool" (Meier, p. 153). The king on the right praises him and calls him "the last remnant of God among men". Zarathustra answers, in Meier's phrase, "not with the laughter of a buffoon, but with the anger of a prophet" (p. 154): "It was not for you that I was waiting here in these mountains. ... I need clear smooth mirrors for my teachings; on your surfaces even my own image is distorted." He waits for others: "laughing lions must come!" Meier draws the implication: the distorted image Zarathustra saw in the child's mirror at the start of Part II was due "not so much to the work of his enemies as to the insufficiency of his friends" (p. 155). Burnham and Jesinghausen point out that the guests are distressed only when Zarathustra is absent. Zarathustra withdraws "in fright" when he sees their dependence on him (p. 182).

**The Last Supper.** The soothsayer demands food. The kings have wine, but there is no bread. Zarathustra has two lambs slaughtered. Meier: "once again outbidding Jesus, who had one lamb prepared for his Last Supper of thirteen" (p. 156). Lampert counts the twelve: the guests plus Zarathustra's two animals and the ass (p. 300). The beggar refuses meat and wine, and Zarathustra answers: "I am a law only for my kind, I am no law for All." Meyer, following Kathleen Higgins, reads the scene as a parody of both Jesus' Last Supper and Plato's *Symposium* (p. 286). Burnham and Jesinghausen note that the German lays *Abendmahl* (Last Supper) over *Gastmahl* (the usual title of the *Symposium*) (p. 170). Meier adds that, unlike Socrates' friends in the *Republic*, the guests are not cheated of their dinner (p. 156). Rosen reads the replacement of bread and wine by meat and water as "the cruelty and sobriety of nature" replacing "the pity and intoxication of religion for the masses" (p. 229).

### 6. "On the Higher Man"

The after-dinner speech has twenty sections, against the thirty of "On Old and New Tablets" in Part III. Lampert lists the contrasts: that speech was for young imaginary followers and this is for old men "whose ways are set", and it ends "on laughing and dancing" (p. 300).

- **From the market to the few.** It begins with the old mistake: "when I spoke to All, I spoke to None" (Meier's translation, p. 157). The mob says "we are all alike ... before God", but God has died. Meier hears a parody of Christian eschatology: "It is only since he lies in the tomb that you have been resurrected" (p. 157). The overman, absent from the rest of Part IV, appears here four times (p. 158).
- **Demand, not pity.** "For you suffer from yourselves, and have not yet suffered from the human." Meier: "Zarathustra's love for the 'higher men' is essentially demand" (p. 158). Rosen reads the speech as a request that they destroy themselves: "the last supper is truly to be the last for them" (p. 234). He finds Nietzsche hiding "by shouting the truth at the top of his voice" (p. 236).
- **Laughter.** Then the tone changes. The higher men have failed like a bad throw of dice: "Be of good courage: what does it matter! How much is yet possible! Learn to laugh at yourselves, as one has to laugh!" Zarathustra charges Jesus with the greatest sin, the saying "Woe unto those that laugh now" (Luke 6:25), and crowns himself: "This laugher's crown, this rose-wreath-crown: I myself have set this crown upon my head; I myself have pronounced my laughter holy." Meyer reads the rose crown against the crown of thorns: "it is laughter, not Christ, that washes away the sins of the world" (p. 289). Rosen adds that Socrates laughs twice in the *Phaedo* and Christ never laughs (p. 237). Meier notes that Zarathustra no longer tells them "become hard!" but "learn to laugh!" (p. 161).

### 7. Melancholy, Science and the Desert

Zarathustra steps out for air, and the magician seizes the moment. He sings "Only fool! Only poet!", a song that reduces every truth-seeker to a liar. Lampert: in Zarathustra's absence the magician "revives his rivalry" and tries to persuade the guests "that Zarathustra is only a poet" (p. 301). All fall into his net except the conscientious one, who seizes the harp. Fear, he says, is man's basic feeling, and science is fear grown spiritual. Zarathustra returns and throws roses at him: courage, not fear, "seems to me man's whole prehistory", and "this human courage with eagles' wings and serpents' wisdom" is today called... (Kaufmann's translation, read in Strauss, p. 237). The guests shout "Zarathustra!" before he can finish.

- Strauss makes these three chapters the centre of Part IV, with "On Science" at its middle: "In a way, science is the center; in a way, science is the problem for him." The answer the reader expects is "science", but "Science is replaced by Zarathustra". The highest, elusive truth belongs to "the individual creative contemplator" (pp. 235–37).
- Lampert reads the scientist as "an ascetic, amusical Socratic", the defender of civilisation against a new barbarism. Zarathustra's ambitions for philosophy's leadership of science depend on refuting him (pp. 302–3).
- Meyer suggests, with Grätz, that the missing word might be "gay science" (GS 327). Comic laughter "is the means to overcome the opposition between deceptive art and truth-seeking science" (p. 292).
- Meier is cooler: "None of them seeks, in the company of Zarathustra, more insecurity." Zarathustra is not talking to philosophers or revolutionaries, so "his attention is focused on dietetics" (p. 163).

The shadow then sings "Among Daughters of the Desert". It is a mock-oriental song in which a moral European in an oasis cannot stop roaring morality at two girls, ending with Luther's "I can do no other, God help me!" Strauss: Europe is "a continent of doubt and a continent of moral indignation", and "the intellectual honesty of the leech is the unity of doubt and morals" (pp. 238–39). Lampert reads the song as a parody of the "apostle of chastity" (p. 304). Both songs later went into the *Dionysus Dithyrambs*.

### 8. The Ass Festival

The cave fills with laughter. Outside, Zarathustra tells his animals that the spirit of gravity is retreating: "it is still not my laughing that they learned" (Meier's translation, p. 164). Then comes silence, the smell of incense, and the sight of all the higher men kneeling to the ass. The ugliest man leads a litany. It ends each verse with the ass's "Yea-Ah" and borrows from Revelation 7:12, Philippians 2:7 (the form of a servant), Genesis and the Gospels. The ass is praised for saying only yes to the world he created, for creating it "as stupid as possible", and for a kingdom "beyond good and evil" (Meier, p. 166). Meier notes that the litany mocks Zarathustra as well as the Christian God, and that the model is the medieval Feast of the Ass (*festum asinorum*), quoted by Nietzsche in BGE 8 (p. 166 n. 202).

Zarathustra brays louder than the ass and interrogates them. The pope: "Better to worship God thus, in this form, than in no form at all!" The one who said "God is a spirit" took "the biggest step and leap so far on earth toward unbelief". The shadow: the old God lives again, for "with Gods, death is always just a prejudice". The magician: "it was a stupidity—it has been difficult enough for me too". The conscientious one: an eternal God has time, so should be as slow and stupid as possible. The ugliest man: "whoever wants to kill most thoroughly, laughs" (Meier's translation, pp. 167–68).

Zarathustra calls them "rogue-fools" and "my new friends", tells them they need "new festivals, ... a small, brave nonsense, some worshipping service and ass festival", and adds: "do it also for love of me! And in remembrance of me!" (Meier, p. 169).

- **Lampert:** the festival is an exorcism. "The superior men are still sick from Christianity and its dead God, and they need such parody ... as an exorcism to purge themselves of its melancholy" (p. 308). The present religion is the religion of pity (BGE 222), and the festival breaks its hold through "a festival of derision". Zarathustra, "the shocked moralist", is the one fooled (p. 306).
- **Meyer:** the ugliest man staged it so the higher men could "laugh about their own latent desire to worship something and their secret wish to revert to the 'metaphysical comforts' of the old god ... This, I think, is the meaning and purpose of the ass festival; it is here that we can clearly say, 'incipit parodia'" (p. 294).
- **Rosen:** a relapse. "The convalescence of the higher men is in fact death, if a death masked by carnival" (p. 241). He compares Moses finding the golden calf, and warns: "The carnival is one step removed from the madhouse" (p. 242).
- **Burnham and Jesinghausen:** "the satirical peak where the solemn doctrine of overhuman transformation is mocked". The asses being celebrated "are the superior humans themselves" (p. 193). Their convalescence remains "naggingly fragmentary", and "they still depend too heavily on Zarathustra" (p. 194).
- **Strauss:** the ass's "Ja" makes it "a Ja-sayer", "a comical prefiguration of the true 'yes' of eternal return" (p. 239).

### 9. The Night-Wanderer's Song and the Sign

Outside at night, the ugliest man speaks: "For the sake of this day—I am for the first time content that I lived the whole of life" (Meier's translation, p. 169), and then "'Was that– life?' I will say to death. 'Very well! One more time!'" The guests rush to thank Zarathustra. The soothsayer dances, perhaps drunk, and some say even the ass danced.

- **Meier:** the ugliest man's Yes "is tied to Zarathustra. He says *da capo* for the Counter-Jesus's sake. Not the teaching of the Eternal Return, but Zarathustra's presence" (p. 170). Then Zarathustra staggers "like a drunken one", and Meier identifies this as the real temptation: "It is pity, assailing him anew. ... His pity relates not so much to the need as to the happiness of the 'higher men.' Their happiness infatuates and softens him" (p. 171). A rejected draft for "The Sign" confirms it: "your happiness was my danger—: pity for your happiness, this—he did not guess!" (p. 179 n. 219).
- **Lampert:** Zarathustra becomes "for the superior men what the animals had said he would become, the teacher of eternal return" (p. 309). But they resume their cry the next morning. They are not "the future lords of the earth" (p. 310).
- **Rosen:** "The whole scene is not and cannot be a genuine transfiguration. ... the affirmation of life is part of the caricature" (pp. 242–43). The higher men "are marked by a leg, not a wing" (p. 243).
- **Meyer:** the "Once more" is a real, if temporary, affirmation. This is his main argument against Loeb's "analeptic" reading, which places Part IV before the end of Part III. If it were so, the ugliest man would affirm recurrence before Zarathustra does (p. 296).
- **Burnham and Jesinghausen:** the ugliest man's word "contented" (*zufrieden*) is "a less than overwhelming choice". His may be "a hurdy-gurdy version" of the thought, like the animals' in Part III (p. 195).

In the song itself Zarathustra repeats, line by line, the midnight poem from the end of Part III. Woe wants heirs and children. Joy "wants itself, wants eternity, wants recurrence". Meyer notes that he addresses the guests as "you failures" and says joy longs even for what has failed: "once more" includes the recurrence of the ill-constituted (p. 301).

**The Sign.** At dawn Zarathustra greets the sun with the first words of the book. The higher men are still asleep: "they are not my right companions". A cloud of doves surrounds him and a lion lays its head on his knee and laughs. The higher men come out, the lion roars, and they flee crying out "as if with one mouth". Zarathustra understands: "'Pity! Pity for the superior human!' ... 'Well then! That– has had its time! My suffering and my pitying– what does that matter! Am I striving then for happiness? I am striving for my work!'" He leaves the cave "glowing and strong, like a morning sun".

- Meier: "The last thing we hear from the old people, like the first thing Zarathustra heard from them, is one cry." Afterward, "No higher men. No doves anywhere. No laughing lion far and wide" (p. 179). Neither prophet nor poet speaks of going-under (p. 180).
- Burnham and Jesinghausen hear Socrates leaving the *Symposium* at dawn with everyone else drunk under the table (p. 198). The children "are not represented in the text – they are represented in the future readers of the text" (p. 198).
- Meyer reads the lion as the power "to command or legislate within society", and Part IV as a political comedy between the tragedy of Parts I–III and the later works. He compares it to the *Symposium* placed between the *Phaedo* and the *Republic* (pp. 302–3).
- Rosen: "despite the events and speeches recorded in the entire work, everything lies ahead. ... As for ourselves, the last residents of the modern age, we are left with the braying of the ass" (p. 244).

### 10. The Six Readings in Brief

**Lampert.** Part IV is Nietzsche's private spectacle of "instructing the best, albeit needy, men of his age in his most important teaching" (p. 310). The higher men are real contemporaries above the mass. The festival is a legitimate cure. Zarathustra overcomes the temptation to despair of his work, and the laughing lion's laugh is "a peaceable and not a vindictive laugh" (p. 311).

**Rosen.** The guests are fragments of Zarathustra and of Nietzsche. Zarathustra must overcome his pity for them because his teaching requires their destruction: "Just as the prophet cannot himself enter into the promised land, neither can the generation that receives his prophecy" (p. 222). The carnival is meant to hypnotise, and the honourable response is "keeping our wits about us" (p. 233).

**Strauss.** Part IV adds nothing to the teaching and returns to "the question of Zarathustra and present-day man" (p. 229). Its centre is science: the leech's honesty, the conscientious one's fear, and the replacement of "science" by "Zarathustra".

**Meier.** Part IV is the comedy that the tragedy had to become. The test is pity, and Zarathustra fails it for one night, not out of compassion for their need but out of infatuation with their happiness, which he caused. The book ends with the prophet in "the state of world-historical advent", still waiting for children who never appear (p. 155).

**Meyer.** Part IV is a satyr play and an interlude before the "great politics" of the later works. The higher men are ill-constituted. Zarathustra is already a well-constituted one who has affirmed recurrence, and he gives them "at least a temporary convalescence" (p. 296). He rejects Loeb's thesis that Zarathustra dies at the end of Part III and that Part IV is an "extended flashback" (pp. 26–27, 296, 299).

**Burnham and Jesinghausen.** The higher men are "ass's heads in a fool's gallery" (p. 169), satirical portraits of contemporaries and of Nietzsche's own past. Zarathustra plays "a hybrid between an earth-bound Socrates and an atheistic Jesus" (p. 169). In the end "Zarathustra is failing here, and he admits it" (p. 169).

The readings agree on more than first appears. All six treat Part IV as a comedy or satire that sets Zarathustra among broken representatives of the old Europe: its politics, science, art, religion and free-thinking. All agree that the higher men are not the companions Zarathustra wants, and that the book ends with the sign of others still to come. They divide over two questions. The first is whether the evening does the guests any good. Lampert and Meyer think it does, at least for a night. Rosen and, more gently, Burnham and Jesinghausen think the cure is a caricature, and Meier leaves it open. The second is what exactly Zarathustra overcomes at the end. Meier's answer is the most textually grounded, because it rests on the parallel between the ugliest man's two speeches and on Nietzsche's own draft: the danger was not their distress but their happiness, and his own pleasure in having caused it.

### Conclusion

The higher men are what is left of greatness in an age that believes "we are all alike". Each has learned something from Zarathustra or shares his disgust: the kings at the rabble, the scientist at half-knowledge, the magician at his own art, the pope at a dead God, the ugliest man at pity, the beggar at riches, the shadow at every faith. None of them can live with what he has seen, and all of them come looking for a master or a saviour. Zarathustra gives them a supper, a sermon, a festival and a song, and for one night they laugh and say yes to life. In the morning they cry out again and run from the lion. The commentators read this as an exorcism (Lampert), a necessary destruction (Rosen), a lesson about science (Strauss), a temptation through happiness (Meier), a satyr play before the politics of the later books (Meyer), or a gallery of portraits of Nietzsche's contemporaries and of Nietzsche himself (Burnham and Jesinghausen). What they share is the recognition that the book closes on an absence. The higher men are dismissed, the children have not arrived, and Zarathustra goes out "glowing and strong" towards a work that the book does not show.
