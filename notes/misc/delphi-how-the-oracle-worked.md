For about a thousand years Greeks and foreigners climbed the slopes of Parnassus to ask **Apollo** what to do, and the god answered through a woman seated on a tripod, the **Pythia**. Three questions run through this note. How did a consultation actually work? Why was it believed? And did the oracle really steer events, as Herodotus' pages full of oracles suggest? H. W. Parke's *Greek Oracles* gives the history and the procedure. Walter Burkert reads Delphi as a place of **sacrifice** before it was a place of prophecy. Robin Osborne argues that most oracle stories of the archaic age are **tradition**: the past as cities later needed it to have been.

### 1. Before Apollo: The Earth Goddess and the Dragon

- **A newcomer god.** Parke argues that Apollo entered Greece during the Dark Ages. He is not named in the Linear B tablets, though Dionysus is. His likely origins are eastern: his seventh-day birthday, his link with Lycia, and the name of his mother Leto, which may come from the Lycian word for woman. In the *Iliad* he is the most consistent enemy of the Greeks.
- **Male prophets elsewhere.** At Apollo's other archaic shrines the god spoke through a man: at Claros a prophet in verse, and at the Ptoion a prophet who once answered a Persian agent in Carian (Herodotus 8.135). The Pythia was the exception. Parke's hypothesis is that Delphi took over a female medium from an earlier cult of **Ge**, the earth goddess. He rejects the older view of Rohde and Bouché-Leclercq that her ecstasy was Dionysiac: the Bacchants never prophesy, and the oracle was closed in the winter months when Dionysus ruled Delphi.
- **The dragon.** In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the god kills a she-dragon at a spring. Parke reads this as a memory of a "forceable acquisition of an oracular centre". Later Delphic theology, he argues, reversed the story to give the oracle a pedigree. The Pythia's prayer in the *Eumenides* honours "Earth, the primal prophetess", then Themis and Phoebe, and only then Apollo, who receives the seat as a gift. The dragon became male, acquired the name **Python** and was made the god's enemy. Against Dodona's claim to be older and to speak for Zeus, Delphi taught that Apollo was Zeus' prophet. In the Delian hymn the newborn god cries, "I shall prophesy to men the unerring will of Zeus", and in the *Eumenides* "Loxias is the mouthpiece of his father, Zeus". Parke calls this "an elaborate piece of theological propaganda".
- **Archaeology.** There was a poor Mycenaean village with one Minoan stone vessel shaped like a lioness's head, then a "fresh start in the early eighth century". Osborne adds that votive offerings increased sharply in the last quarter of the eighth century, from all over Greece and including Cretan tripods and shields found in no other mainland sanctuary.

### 2. A Sanctuary of Sacrifice (Burkert)

Burkert begins not with prophecy but with killing. "The god spoke at Delphi... However, the worldly action that gave rise to the oracle, and which we can grasp, was a special form of sacrificial ritual."

- **The Delphians' knives.** In the *Hymn*, Apollo tells his Cretan priests, who fear they will starve on the barren slope: "Each of you should carry a knife in your right hand and slaughter sheep continually; for they will be there in abundance... But guard my temple and receive the tribes of men." In practice the Delphians stood round the altar with knives and cut off what they could, so that the man offering the sacrifice was "often left empty-handed". A proverb told visitors to bring extra meat. Burkert stresses that "no one ever tried to reform" this unseemly custom: "Delphi was the only Greek community to make religion its main occupation."
- **Neoptolemus.** The son of Achilles was killed at Apollo's hearth. Burkert sees this as the myth that matches the rite: the outsider who sacrifices and the natives who "steal" the meat. Pindar keeps the hero in the sanctuary "seeing to law and justice in the heroes' processions" (*Nemean* 7.44–47).
- **Wolves.** Delphi is full of wolves. Autolycus, "the werewolf", is the first inhabitant of Parnassus in Greek literature. A bronze wolf stood by the altar, Lycoreia was the Delphians' place of origin, and there is Apollo Lykeios. In seizing the meat, the Delphians behaved "distinctly wolflike".
- **The Pythia as victim.** Before a consultation, a goat was sprinkled with cold water and had to tremble. Burkert reads the trembling as fear, not consent. The goat corresponds to the Pythia: "When the Pythia mounted the tripod, she was offering herself up to death in an expiatory act... She too would shudder, her entire body would quake, but the divine presence welled up out of the anguish and fear: Apollo would be there and would speak."
- **The Septerion.** Every eighth year a boy set fire to a hut built like a king's palace. All then fled, and Apollo went to Tempe in Thessaly to be purified, after which the laurel for the Pythian victors was brought back from there. Burkert concludes: "The violent act at the 'place of putrefaction'... was surmounted and overcome once and for all through luminous order, through the beauty of art. But the order and the art themselves were suspended over an abyss of dread... Apollo would speak only through the raving woman sitting on top of the covered tripod."

### 3. How a Consultation Worked

Parke admits that no ancient author describes a consultation from start to finish, because everyone knew how it went. Herodotus could write of a Thracian oracle that it worked "just as at Delphi and without anything more elaborate".

1. **The Pythia.** She was a woman over fifty dressed as a girl, "of blameless life", who stopped living with her husband once appointed. Plutarch says she was "a very ordinary peasant woman" with no special family or training. Parke suggests she came from the women who kept the eternal fire on the temple hearth. At the oracle's busiest there were three Pythiae, two working in shifts and one in reserve.
2. **The calendar.** Originally the oracle spoke once a year, on the seventh of Bysios (February–March), Apollo's birthday, which suited colonists about to sail. It later spoke monthly, and it was closed for the three winter months: about **nine days a year**. Hence the value of *promanteia*, the honour of consulting early in the queue.
3. **The fees.** A sacred cake set the minimum price. Around 420 BC Phaselis agreed to pay about ten Attic drachmas for a state enquiry and a tenth of that for a private one. Parke puts the private fee at about two days' pay for an Athenian juror. On top came the sacrifice of sheep or goats inside the temple.
4. **The adyton.** This was a small room, roughly sixteen by nine feet, which no woman except the Pythia could enter. The enquirer sat at one end and was told to "think pure thoughts and speak well-omened words". The Pythia was probably hidden from him. A male official, the **Prophetes**, put the question and brought back the answer. Her voice was often said to cry or shout. It was the Prophetes who "make sense from them and to reproduce them in verse", using the ready-made conventions of oral epic.
5. **No interpretation.** Once the answer had been given, Delphi took no further responsibility for it. Interpreting it was up to the enquirer and his own experts, "for... it was popularly accepted that the god answered in riddles". When the Pythia said "I", she meant Apollo.

### 4. Vapours, Chasms and Trance

- **The ancient rational account.** Diodorus tells of a goatherd, Coretas, whose goats leapt about near a chasm, and of a tripod set over it to keep the prophetess from falling in. Parke calls it "highly ingenious" and obviously built out of the goat ritual. In *On the Decline of Oracles* Plutarch explained the decline of Delphi by a weakening of the vapour. Later, as a priest at Delphi, he spiritualised it into an occasional sweet scent in the room where enquirers waited.
- **What the excavators found.** "There can never have been any deep subterranean chasm in the rock at this point" (Parke). The water of the Cassotis spring was channelled around the temple, though in a way that gave the impression it flowed from beneath the adyton. Burkert agrees: "there is simply no trace of a chasm or any volcanic activity whatsoever beneath the temple". The vapours round the tripod came from burning laurel and barley. Osborne calls the vapour and laurel-chewing stories without "basis in fact". (A geological study published in 2001, later than all three books, reopened the gas question. Nothing in these sources addresses it.)
- **Parke's explanation.** "The ecstatic prophecies which she uttered were simply the result of suggestion bringing on a trance." A peasant woman raised to believe that Apollo would enter whoever sat on his tripod would respond as expected, and would remember nothing afterwards, which confirmed to everyone that the god had spoken. Parke adds that the ancients "found it extremely difficult to imagine a spiritual force", so inspiration had to be pictured as a physical vapour.
- **Burkert's explanation** is ritual, not psychological: the trance is the climax of a sacrifice in which the woman takes the victim's place.

### 5. The Lot Oracle and the Private Enquirer

- **Two beans.** An inscription setting fees for the island of Sciathus mentions a charge for consulting "by the two beans". This proves that Delphi also answered by **lot**, presumably a bean of one colour for yes and another for no, on any day that was not inauspicious. The verb for the Pythia "giving a response", *anhairein*, literally means "to pick up", the word used for drawing a lot. The Pythia drew from a hundred names the ten heroes for Cleisthenes' new Athenian tribes. A line attributed to her says: "many are they that throw lots, but few are the men who are prophets."
- **Socrates.** Parke thinks Chaerephon, "notoriously poor", asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates by lot, since Xenophon says the answer came "in the presence of many". "Yet it is strange to surmise what a difference might have been produced in the history of Greek philosophy, if the Delphic oracle had not, by whatever accidental means, given Socrates an impulse."
- **Xenophon's mistake.** Before joining Cyrus, Xenophon asked Apollo only which gods he should sacrifice to for a safe journey. Socrates rebuked him for not first asking whether he should go at all: "since you have asked in that way, you must do what the god has commanded" (*Anabasis* 3.1.5–7). Osborne draws the general point: questions came from those with the authority to act, and they were usually put as a choice. Croesus asking the god to foretell the future was "a mark of his not knowing how to use oracles".
- **Ordinary business.** Parke insists that "the ordinary private enquirer must have made up numerically the largest part of the oracle's business": childlessness, illness, which god to pray to. The historians simply did not record these consultations. At Dodona, the lead tablets that survive carry questions written by individuals, and they are almost all private.

### 6. Colonies: Did Delphi Send the Greeks Overseas?

- **The stories.** Parke shows how colonial foundation stories were "worked up" by folk tradition. The founder comes to Delphi about a stammer and is sent to found a city. Riddles define the site. Syracuse and Croton are founded in the same year after their founders choose between wealth and health. Battus of Cyrene was told, "you have come for a voice". Herodotus already saw that "Battus" was the Libyan word for king, and Pindar knew the founder's real name was Aristoteles. A nine-line version of the oracle is "a piece of propaganda" of the late-sixth-century Cyrenean kings. The basic question was probably just "is it better and more good" to found a colony at such a place.
- **Not an information centre.** Parke and Osborne both reject the idea of Delphi as a clearing-house of geographical knowledge. Parke: "the initiative, the selection of the founder and the bulk of the information all came originally from the mother city."
- **Osborne on Cyrene.** Herodotus gives two versions (4.150–59). The Theran version stresses that the idea came from outside, that the island suffered years of hardship first, and that settlers were chosen fairly. The Cyrenean version stresses Battus' royal descent, which suited the Battiad dynasty. "It is vain to seek historical truth from either account... both parties were telling stories of the past to suit the present." Even the names of the characters fit their roles: Etearchus means "True ruler", Phronime "Sensible woman".
- **What the oracle was for.** "A story of Delphic intervention provided the ideal way of covering up the fact that a group had been thrown out, or of turning a pioneering individual into a state representative." This is why, for Osborne, "Delphi's role was above all a political one". The Spartan tradition that Lycurgus' laws had a Delphic mandate, not the story of Battus, shows what made Delphi great. Tyrtaeus already spoke of Spartans who "brought from Pytho homeward the oracles of god".

### 7. Kings, Tyrants and Croesus

- **Tyrants.** Cypselus of Corinth built the first treasury at Delphi under his own name. The lines greeting him as "blessed" may have been delivered after he took power, with a prophecy limiting his dynasty added later. Parke notes the later "conventional picture" of the Pythia as anti-tyrant, and concludes that the Delphians were "content to let the oracle's supposed attitude correspond to popular feeling".
- **Croesus' test.** Croesus supposedly tested all the oracles at once, and only Delphi saw that he was cooking a tortoise with lamb in a bronze cauldron: "But I know the number of grains of sand and the measures of the sea, and the dumb I understand and the speechless I hear." Parke calls the test "an invention of the Delphic priesthood built on the superb grandeur of Croesus' dedications". These included a gold lion of about a quarter of a ton, though Herodotus heard that Croesus gave Branchidae gifts just as rich. The list of oracles in the story fits about 500 BC, not 550.
- **"A mighty empire".** Herodotus gives the famous ambiguous answer in prose. Parke thinks the real reply "offered Croesus the victory which he desired with no great hesitation. The favourable oracle will have been suppressed after the event". This was not bribery. To a small town, the Lydian's wealth simply looked invincible. The fall of Croesus was the oracle's first catastrophic mistake, and it produced "a policy of playing for safety" towards Persia.

### 8. Purification and the Maxims

- **Blood-guilt.** Parke follows Dodds's shift from a "shame-culture" to a "guilt-culture". In Homer a killing was settled between families by payment or vengeance. In the archaic age killing came to pollute, and Delphi spread the remedy of **purification**. In the *Odyssey* Orestes avenges his father with no oracle. In tragedy Apollo commands the deed and then purifies the matricide. When Callondes, who had killed the poet Archilochus in battle, came to consult, the Pythia drove him out: "You have slain the Muses' servant. Leave the temple." Parke accepts the story because it is so improbable.
- **The maxims.** "Know thyself", "nothing too much" and the rarely quoted "go bail and destruction is nigh" were probably first inscribed on the Alcmaeonid temple of the late sixth century. The tradition of the Seven Sages who supposedly dedicated them is later.
- **Glaucus.** Glaucus asked whether he might perjure himself to keep a deposit. The answer: "the child of an oath has no name and it has no hands or feet. Yet it is swift to pursue until it seize a whole family". He was then told that "to tempt the god and commit the deed were equally heinous" (Herodotus 6.86). Even if this last part was added later, Parke sees in it the recognition "that guilt was a matter of intention, not only of act, and this was a great advance in Greek thought".
- **Parke's general view.** "Delphi instead taught the late archaic period that restraint and modesty which was to be the foundation on which the glorious achievement of classical art and literature was based"; the oracle's sayings "served to crystallise and express in memorable form the ideas of their time."

### 9. The Persian Crisis and the Wooden Walls

- **Appeasement.** Delphi told the Cnidians not to cut through their isthmus: "Zeus would have made it an island, if he had wished it so." It condemned Miletus, "deviser of wicked deeds", for the Ionian Revolt. In 480 it advised the Argives and Cretans to stay neutral.
- **The Athenian consultation** (Herodotus 7.140–44). Before the envoys had even asked, the Pythia Aristonice cried, "Miserable men, why are you sitting idle? Leave in flight the furthest dwellings of your land". On the advice of a Delphian, Timon, the envoys returned as suppliants and received the second oracle: "Zeus of the broad heaven gives to the Tritoborn a wooden wall, alone to remain undestroyed... O divine Salamis, you will destroy the sons of women".
- **Parke: authentic, and revealing.** The detail (the names of the Pythia and of Timon) and the discredit to Delphi make it unlikely to be invented. The first response is unambiguous defeatism. The second gave "the barest minimum", something Themistocles could read as a call to the ships, while leaving Delphi "free to reinterpret the oracle whatever the outcome". After Salamis the oracle "had in effect changed sides" and received the serpent column of the Greek victory.
- **Osborne: a tradition built in living memory.** "The only Athenians who knew what actually happened at Delphi were the ambassadors." What changed minds at Athens was the public debate that Themistocles won, and the story then grew to put the oracle at its centre. Having the Pythia speak before she is asked hides what the Athenians actually asked. It also highlights Athenian tenacity and denies other Greeks the excuse that Delphi told them to submit. "The forces which led to the Decree of Themistokles and the forces which led to these twenty-four lines of Delphic hexameters were not essentially different." And "oracles were persuasive devices, but they could not persuade all the people all the time": some Athenians still held the old wooden wall of the Acropolis and died there (8.41, 51).

### 10. Why Oracles Disappear from History

- **Parke: decline.** In 432 the Spartans were promised Apollo's help "when sought or unsought". This was "perhaps the last occasion on which the Delphic oracle was to be consulted by a Greek state on a major political issue". Delphi became a pawn between Athens and Sparta. Euripides attacked Apollo, and Athens turned to Dodona and Ammon. Lysander was said to have tried to bribe three oracles to back a change in the Spartan constitution, and Parke observes that "no one would be greatly surprised or shocked" at such chicanery. In 388 King Agesipolis asked Zeus at Olympia, then asked Apollo whether he agreed with his father: "how artificial the whole business was becoming". Then came the landslide of 373, the Phocian seizure and the stripping of the treasures in the Third Sacred War, and Demosthenes' jibe that "the Pythia has philippised".
- **Osborne: a change in evidence and in politics.** Archaic history "overflows with oracle stories", while classical history has few, and not only in the rationalist Thucydides. Two reasons:
  1. *Democracy.* It "depended upon confidence that the best way to get the right answer was to discuss it in a mass meeting". Consulting an oracle after a vote would make the decision look provisional and "lay open the possibility that the oracle might overturn the democratic decision". So Athens consulted only on matters of cult, and did so before deciding.
  2. *Writing.* After 479 "events began to become history before they became tradition", through inscriptions, speeches and Thucydides' notes. Before then, Delphi, "precisely because it stood outside the direct powers of any city, was an ideal instrument with which oral tradition could think about the past of a city. Citing a Delphic oracle lent an inevitability to the events described."
- **Orwell.** Osborne compares archaic tradition to the rewriting of old newspapers in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*: "Just as the regime of Orwell's Oceania believes it to be important for its own ongoing credibility that accurate predictions were made in the past, so archaic Greek traditions incorporated Delphic oracles which revealed accurately, if inexplicitly, events to come, and thereby justified the status quo." "Classical Greece had two pasts, the actual past and the past it shaped for itself."

### 11. Assessment

- **Three readings, mostly compatible.** Parke tells us what the institution was and how it ran, and his evidence for procedure (fees, calendar, lots, the Prophetes) is the firmest material in the subject. Burkert tells us what the place meant in ritual terms: a sacrificial site whose order rested on violence. Osborne tells us how to read the stories. The three conflict only where Parke takes a famous oracle at face value, and on those points Osborne's caution is usually right.
- **Where Osborne is strongest.** His principle that people consult oracles "not in order to discover the future, nor in order to get ideas, but in order to get a decision made for them or to get their own way" explains a great deal. It explains the yes-or-no questions, Socrates' rebuke of Xenophon, Agesipolis' trick question, and why the verse riddles appear in stories rather than in inscriptions. Parke himself concedes most of this: the words come from "popular tradition" and Delphic guides, and an invented foundation oracle "would not be regarded as forgery any more than if he had been an ancient historian who had composed a suitably rhetorical speech".
- **Where Parke still matters.** Osborne's scepticism can go too far. Some responses are awkward for Delphi and therefore credible: the defeatist first answer to Athens, the advice to Cnidus, the Demaratus scandal, in which the Pythia Perialla was removed from office. Parke's picture of a small, unprotected town playing for safety beside its treasures, "very conscious that they had no physical resources capable of protecting the vast treasures of their sanctuary", is a sober explanation of Delphi's politics that Osborne's account does not replace.
- **Burkert's limits.** Burkert's reading of the Pythia as a sacrificial substitute is suggestive. It ties together the goat, the tripod, Neoptolemus and the Septerion. But it is an interpretation of structure, not something any ancient source says. Parke's plainer appeal to suggestion and trance accounts for the same evidence with fewer assumptions.
- **The private oracle.** All three books agree that the oracle that mattered most to most Greeks is the one we can hardly see: the farmer asking about a child or a field, answered by a bean. Parke's closing judgment fits all three: the oracles' "high repute... rested in the end less on the claim to have achieved a few startling forecasts. It was rather the outcome of a human need."

### Conclusion

The Pythia sat on Apollo's tripod about nine days a year, in a small room filled with laurel smoke, while a male Prophetes turned her cries into hexameters. On other days beans did the work. Behind the oracle, Burkert finds a sacrificial cult of knives, wolves and a woman offered up to the god. Parke shows an institution that colonists and lawgivers used for authority, that misjudged Croesus and Xerxes, and that taught restraint through its maxims and its purifications. Osborne shows why its role in history looks so large: in a world without records, an oracle was the best way to make the past seem fated, and once events were written down as they happened, the oracles faded from the record. Delphi did not steer Greek history so much as give it a voice, and the Greeks heard in that voice the decisions they had already made, or wished they had.
