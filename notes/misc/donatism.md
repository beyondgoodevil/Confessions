**Donatism** was a theological and ecclesiastical movement that arose in North Africa in the early 4th century. It centered on the belief that the validity of the sacraments, particularly **baptism** and the **Eucharist**, depended on the moral character and purity of the clergy administering them. Donatists argued that clergy who had betrayed the faith or committed serious sins could not validly perform sacraments, as their personal unworthiness invalidated their ministry.

## Key Aspects of Donatism

1. **Historical Background**:
    - Donatism emerged during the aftermath of the **Diocletian persecution** (303–311 AD), when some Christian clergy, under threat of death or torture, handed over sacred texts or renounced their faith to save their lives. These individuals were called **"traditores"** (those who "handed over").
    - The controversy began when **Caecilian**, a bishop of Carthage, was consecrated by a bishop who was accused of being a traditor. A group of North African Christians rejected his consecration and chose a rival bishop, **Donatus**, from whom the movement took its name.
2. **Core Beliefs**:
    - **Sacramental Purity**: Donatists held that sacraments administered by sinful or unworthy clergy were invalid. In their view, the holiness of the Church depended on the moral purity of its members, especially its leaders.
    - **Church as a Community of Saints**: They believed the Church should consist only of those who were morally upright and had not betrayed their faith.
    - **Re-baptism**: Donatists insisted that converts baptized by clergy outside their movement, or by clergy they considered impure, needed to be re-baptized upon joining the Donatist Church.
3. **Opposition to Donatism**:
    - **St. Augustine of Hippo** was the primary theological opponent of Donatism. He argued that the validity of the sacraments did not depend on the personal holiness of the minister but on **Christ Himself**, who works through the sacraments.
        - Augustine taught: *"The sacrament is holy by virtue of the One who instituted it, not by virtue of the one who administers it."*
    - He emphasized the unity of the Church, arguing that schism was a grave sin, and the Church remains holy despite the presence of sinners within it.
4. **Condemnation**:
    - The Donatist position was condemned by various Church councils, including the **Council of Arles (314 AD)** and later reaffirmed at the **Council of Carthage (411 AD)**.
    - The Roman Empire, under Emperor **Constantine** and later emperors, also sought to suppress Donatism, sometimes using force.
5. **Legacy**:
    - Although Donatism was officially condemned and gradually declined, its ideas persisted in some North African communities until the Islamic conquest of the region in the 7th century.
    - The controversy raised important theological questions about the nature of the Church, the sacraments, and the balance between moral integrity and ecclesiastical authority.

## Summary

Donatism taught that the validity of sacraments depended on the moral purity of the clergy. It was opposed by figures like **St. Augustine**, who upheld that sacraments derive their efficacy from Christ, not the personal worthiness of the minister. Donatism was condemned as a heresy but left a lasting impact on discussions about the Church's nature and unity.
