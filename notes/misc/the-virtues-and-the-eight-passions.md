What purification (the first stage of [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]]) is purifying *from*, and what grows in its place. The process of how a single thought turns into a sin is in [[How a Thought Becomes a Sin: The Stages of Temptation]]. This note is the map of the passions themselves.

## 1. Virtue and Vice

- **Virtue** (*arete*, Latin *virtus*): a settled readiness of the powers of soul (and body) to do good. "Habitual excellence".
    - *Virtus* first meant manly courage. *Arete* is wider: the excellence of anything (a knife's sharpness, a horse's speed).
    - It has to be practised, so **perseverance** is the virtue all the others need.
- **Vice**: a settled disposition toward sin. In the Eastern tradition usually called a **passion** (*pathos*): something that has a hold on you, that you "suffer".
- Neither is inborn. Virtue is reached gradually, by grace and effort together. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]].

## 2. Image and Likeness

St. John of Damascus:

- **Image** = reason and freedom. Can't be lost, even by sin.
- **Likeness** = becoming like God by virtue. Depends on choice, damaged by sin.

So the virtues aren't extras added to human nature. They're God's own qualities, given at creation and restored in Christ. Growing in virtue *is* growing into the likeness. See [[Image and Likeness]].

## 3. The Eight Thoughts (*Logismoi*)

First found in **Evagrius Ponticus** (4th c., Egyptian desert), handed to the West by **St. John Cassian** (*Conferences* 5, *Institutes* 5–12).

| # | Passion | Greek | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gluttony | *gastrimargia* | disordered appetite for food and drink |
| 2 | Lust | *porneia* | disordered sexual desire |
| 3 | Avarice | *philargyria* | love of money and possessions |
| 4 | Anger | *orge* | rage, resentment, remembering wrongs |
| 5 | Sadness / dejection | *lype* | worldly grief, self-pity, sorrow over what you couldn't have |
| 6 | Despondency / acedia | *akedia* | listlessness, boredom with spiritual things, "the noonday demon" (Ps 91:6 LXX) |
| 7 | Vainglory | *kenodoxia* | wanting to be seen and praised, even for virtue |
| 8 | Pride | *hyperephania* | self-exaltation; crediting yourself and not God |

**Cassian's grouping**

- **1–6 form a chain.** Each excess feeds the next: gluttony → lust → avarice → anger → sadness → despondency. Overeating weakens control of the body; lust, unsatisfied, leads to grasping; frustrated grasping to anger; anger to brooding sadness; sadness to despair of any effort at all.
- So the fight starts at the bottom, with the stomach. That's why **fasting** comes first in Orthodox ascetic practice.
- **7 and 8 are a pair apart.** They don't come from the first six. Worse: they grow *stronger* as the first six are beaten. You conquer gluttony and then feel proud of your fasting.

My summary: the first six are passions of the body and lower soul; the last two are passions of the spirit, and they feed on success.

## 4. Eight vs Seven

Pope St. Gregory the Great (6th c.) reworked the list into the West's seven:

| Eastern eight | Western seven |
|---|---|
| gluttony | gluttony |
| lust | lust |
| avarice | greed |
| anger | wrath |
| sadness | *(merged into sloth)* |
| despondency | sloth |
| vainglory | *(merged into pride)* |
| pride | pride |
| — | **envy** (added) |

- Gregory put pride at the root of all the others, not at the end of the list.
- The East keeps *lype* (sadness) separate, which I think is psychologically sharper: there's a "worldly sorrow that worketh death" (2 Cor 7:10) that isn't the same as laziness.

## 5. The Answering Virtues

The traditional pairing (Cassian, the Philokalia, St. John Climacus). Each passion is healed by its opposite, not by willpower in the abstract.

| Passion | Remedy | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Gluttony | **temperance** / self-control | fasting |
| Lust | **chastity** | guarding the eyes and thoughts; fasting |
| Avarice | **non-possessiveness**, generosity | almsgiving |
| Anger | **meekness**, patience | forgiving; not answering back; prayer for enemies |
| Sadness | **joy** in God, hope | thanksgiving; spiritual reading |
| Despondency | **diligence**, perseverance | manual work; a fixed rule; "stay in your cell" |
| Vainglory | **hiddenness** | doing good in secret (Matt 6:1-6) |
| Pride | **humility** | self-reproach; obedience; attributing everything to God |

**Humility** is the hinge of the whole list. Seeing yourself as God sees you. St. Isaac the Syrian calls it "the garment of the Godhead".

## 6. Virtues

Listed (as "fruits of the Spirit", cf. Gal 5:22-23):

- **Faith**: weakened by sin, impurity and pride
- **Hope**: confidence in God's outcome
- **Knowledge** and **wisdom**: knowing God is the aim of life
- **Honesty**
- **Humility** and **meekness**
- **Obedience**
- **Patience** / diligence
- **Courage**: freedom from fear, to the point of martyrdom
- **Faithfulness**
- **Temperance** / chastity
- **Generosity**
- **Gratitude** / contentment
- **Love**: the greatest (1 Cor 13:13), and loving God is impossible without loving others

The classical **cardinal** virtues (prudence, justice, courage, temperance) and the **theological** virtues (faith, hope, love) aren't used as a framework in the Eastern sources the way they are in Aquinas. See [[Virtue Ethics: Aristotle and the Fathers]].

## 7. Why the Body Too

- A person is body and soul together, so both have to be trained.
- Fasting and self-control are called the "primary source" of virtue. Cassian's chain explains why: the first link is the stomach.
- This is the opposite of Neoplatonic contempt for the body. The body isn't the prison. It's half of the one being trained. See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]] sec. 5.
