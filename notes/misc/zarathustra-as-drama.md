> "Jesus himself began to be about thirty years of age." (Luke 3:23)

> "Whosoever shall deny me before men, him will I also deny before my Father which is in heaven." (Matthew 10:33)

> "Ye shall be sorrowful, but your sorrow shall be turned into joy." (John 16:20)

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (four parts, 1883–85) is usually mined for slogans: the overman, the last man, "God is dead", eternal recurrence. But it's the only book N. wrote with a **plot**: characters, places, events, a hero who changes. Lampert's warning is the right starting point. Pull the speeches out of the story and you turn a drama into a treatise, and you lose the main event, which is **what happens to Zarathustra**. The interpreters disagree about what that is: an education (Lampert), a failure (Rosen), a tragedy that is also a parody of the Gospels (Meier), a Greek tragic trilogy with a satyr play attached (Meyer). Here's how it works:

N. = Nietzsche, Z = *Zarathustra* (citations by part and chapter title, Kaufmann's translation unless noted), GS = *Gay Science*, EH = *Ecce Homo*.

## 1. What Kind of Book Is It?

- **"A Book for All and None."** The subtitle is also the plot, Lampert says: Zarathustra's audience shrinks from **everyone** (the crowd in the market) to disciples, to a few guests, to **no one** but himself and his animals.
- **"Music."** In EH N. says perhaps the whole of Z "may be reckoned as music" (quoted in Meyer). It came after his reconciliation with music in 1881 and his *Hymn to Life*, set to a poem by Lou Salomé.
- **The Bible's voice.** N. wrote Z in the style of **Luther's Bible**, which he called the best German book (BGE 247). Meier notices a tiny tell: N. uses the old form *sahe* ("saw") eleven times, the way Luther does in Genesis 1:4, "And God *sahe* the light". The narration is in verses, with "Thus spoke Zarathustra" closing each speech as the Gospels close a discourse.
- **"Incipit tragoedia" / "incipit parodia."** The opening of Z first appeared as GS 342, titled *Incipit tragoedia*: "the tragedy begins". It follows GS 340 (the dying Socrates, who thought life a disease) and GS 341 (the demon of eternal recurrence). In the 1887 preface to GS, N. added: "*incipit parodia*, no doubt". So which is it? Meier: **both**. It's a tragedy whose hero is a parody.
- **The name.** Zarathustra (Zoroaster) was, says EH ("Destiny" 3), the first to see the struggle of good and evil as "the wheel in the machinery of things". He created the error of morality, so he must be the first to recognise it. Lampert adds what this means: the founder of the linear, moral view of history comes back to undo his own work.

## 2. The Plot

**Prologue** (the descent)
1. At thirty, Zarathustra goes up into the mountains. After ten years he speaks to the **sun**: you'd have no happiness without those you shine on. Like the bee with too much honey, he needs hands to receive his overflow. "Thus began Zarathustra's going-under."
2. In the forest he meets an old **saint** who loves God and not men. Walking on, Zarathustra says to himself: "Could it be possible? This old saint in the forest has not yet heard anything of this, that God is dead!"
3. In the **market** a crowd is waiting for a **tightrope walker**. Zarathustra preaches the overman: "Man is a rope, tied between beast and overman, a rope over an abyss." The crowd laughs. He tries the opposite, the **last man** who has "invented happiness" and blinks. The crowd shouts: give us the last man, you can keep the overman.
4. A **jester** leaps over the tightrope walker, who falls and dies at Zarathustra's feet. Zarathustra carries the corpse away and buries it in a hollow tree.
5. Lesson: "companions I need, living ones", not corpses and not herds. He'll speak to the few, not the people.

**Part I** (making disciples)
- Twenty-two speeches in the town called "the Motley Cow": the three metamorphoses, the despisers of the body, the new idol (the state), the thousand and one goals, free death...
- The last, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue", is a farewell. He gives his disciples a staff with a serpent coiled round a sun, and then: "Now I bid you lose me and find yourselves; and only when you have all denied me will I return to you." He promises to come back a third time for the "great noon".

**Part II** (the discovery)
- He returns because, in a dream, a child shows him a mirror and he sees a devil's grimace: his teaching is being distorted.
- On the Blessed Isles, three songs (Night Song, Dance Song, Tomb Song), then "On Self-Overcoming", addressed to "you who are wisest": wherever there is life, there is **will to power**.
- "On Redemption": the will is a prisoner of the past, "it was". Revenge is the will's ill will against time. Only by willing backwards can the will redeem the past and create every "it was" into a "thus I willed it". Then Zarathustra stops short, "like one who has been severely frightened".
- "The Stillest Hour": a voice without a voice tells him he knows his thought but won't say it. He weeps and goes back to his solitude. **Part II ends with a refusal.**

**Part III** (the thought)
- At sea, Zarathustra tells the sailors a riddle ("On the Vision and the Riddle"). Climbing with the **dwarf**, the spirit of gravity, on his shoulders, he comes to a gateway called **Moment**, where two eternal paths meet. Must not everything that can happen have happened already? Then a dog howls, and he sees a young **shepherd** with a black snake hanging from his mouth. "Bite! Bite!" The shepherd bites off its head, spits it out, and rises **laughing**, "no longer human".
- Travels among the small and the virtuous, the apostates, the "Return Home" to solitude, the old and new tablets.
- "The Convalescent": he summons his "most abysmal thought", collapses, and lies seven days as if dead. His **animals** speak the doctrine for him: "you are the teacher of the eternal recurrence, that is your destiny!" He doesn't answer them. He is talking with his soul.
- Ends with three songs: "On the Great Longing", "The Other Dance Song" (with **Life**, a woman, to whom he whispers something in her ear) and "The Seven Seals": "For I love you, O eternity!"

**Part IV** (the afterthought)
- Written later and first printed privately for a few friends (1885). Years have passed; Zarathustra's hair is white. He goes "fishing for men" with honey ("The Honey Sacrifice").
- The soothsayer brings news of a **cry of distress**: the "higher man" calls for help. Zarathustra goes searching and finds a whole cast: two kings, the conscientious man of the spirit (the leech), the magician (the old enchanter), the last pope ("retired from service"), the **ugliest man** (the murderer of God, who couldn't bear being seen), the voluntary beggar who preaches to cows, and Zarathustra's own **shadow**. He sends them all to his cave.
- The **Last Supper** parody ("The Supper"), then "On the Higher Man", a long sermon telling them to laugh. Then the **Ass Festival**: Zarathustra comes back to find them worshipping a donkey, with a litany ("Amen! And praise and honor and wisdom and thanks and glory and strength be to our god", the doxology of Rev 7:12) answered each time by the ass's "Yea-Yuh". He scolds them, then lets it pass as a festival of convalescents.
- "The Drunken Song" at midnight: "Was *that* life? Well then! Once more!" The ugliest man is converted to life.
- **"The Sign."** Morning. A **laughing lion** comes with a flock of doves. The higher men flee. Zarathustra realises what the soothsayer's trap was: **pity** for the higher men, his "final sin". "My suffering and my pity for suffering, what does it matter? Am I concerned with happiness? I am concerned with my **work**." Last words: "this is my morning, my day is breaking: rise now, rise, thou great noon!" He leaves the cave "glowing and strong as a morning sun".

## 3. The Cast

| Figure | Where | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| The sun | Prologue 1; IV end | Zarathustra's model: overflowing, going-under, rising again |
| The saint in the forest | Prologue 2; IV "Retired from Service" | Old piety that hasn't heard God is dead (and the last pope who has) |
| The tightrope walker | Prologue 3–6 | Man as a "rope"; a bridge that falls. Jung: N.'s own shadow |
| The jester | Prologue 6, 8 | The one who "leaps over", a caricature of overcoming |
| The last man | Prologue 5 | Comfort, safety, blinking: the crowd's real ideal |
| The dwarf / spirit of gravity | III "Vision and Riddle" | The cheap version of the thought: "time itself is a circle" |
| The shepherd | III "Vision and Riddle" | Zarathustra himself, choking on nausea at "the small man" recurring |
| Eagle and serpent | Throughout | Pride and wisdom. They speak the doctrine he won't |
| Life and Wisdom | II "Dance Song"; III "Other Dance Song" | Women he loves and quarrels with |
| The higher men | IV | Those for whom the old values have died but no new ones live: each a broken piece of the past |
| The ass | IV | What the higher men worship once God is dead: "Yea-Yuh", a yes to everything |
| The laughing lion and doves | IV "The Sign" | The sign that his "children" are near |

## 4. Lampert: The Education of Zarathustra

Laurence Lampert, *Nietzsche's Teaching* (1986).
- Z exists to carry the thought of **eternal return**, and the plot is how Zarathustra grows into its teacher.
- **Part I**: herald of the overman, maker of disciples. Its peak is "On the Thousand Goals and One", where the will to power is named but not yet seen. **Part II**: the discovery of will to power ("Self-Overcoming") and the spirit of revenge ("Redemption", "the most astonishing chapter"). **Part III**: he wills eternal return in solitude and becomes a philosopher in N.'s sense, "the man of the most comprehensive responsibility" (BGE 61). **That's the end of the book.** Part IV is "an interlude".
- **Failure as teacher?** Moving from All to None looks like failure, but the disciples are waiting for his return. The book shows him succeeding as a **learner**, which is what comes first. "It is the aim of the book to create the audience that it shows Zarathustra failing to find."
- **Why a fable?** It lets N. tell how he reached his teaching without the bad taste of praising himself (BGE 283), by giving the task to one "younger and stronger". And it lets him use an **esoteric art of writing**, tempting rather than preaching.
- Lampert's own sympathy is plain: he reads Z as the founding of a new, earthly religion of Dionysus and Ariadne, and he thinks it succeeds.

## 5. Rosen: The Mask and the Noble Lie

Stanley Rosen, *The Mask of Enlightenment* (1995). The most critical of the readings.
- N. is a **Platonist** in one sense only: the philosopher as **prophet and lawgiver** who knows human nature and ranks its types. He has no Ideas to take his bearings from, though. Being is **chaos**.
- **Double rhetoric**: "he who would create must first destroy". The rhetoric of destruction radicalises Enlightenment scepticism (it reduces even mathematics to interpretation). The rhetoric of creation radicalises Renaissance humanism, with a dose of the pre-Socratics and of "Wagnerian melodrama".
- The trouble: "he must destroy those whom he wishes to persuade that they are creators". If values are noble lies spun out of chaos, so are the overman's. And eternal return contradicts creative freedom.
- So Z is "at once a handbook of revolution and a confession by its author that revolutions must always fail". The drama proves it: "all disciples are inferior", and supermen can't be produced by doctrines.
- Rosen's conclusion: at bottom there are two doctrines. Life is an illusion, chance a mask for necessity. And since humanity can't live on that truth, it must be given a **noble lie**. They contradict only when we fall for the lie ourselves.

## 6. Meier: Philosopher or Prophet?

Heinrich Meier, *What Is Nietzsche's Zarathustra?* (German 2017).
- **Not "who" but "what".** Heidegger asked *who* Zarathustra is and answered "the teacher of eternal return". But that's what the **animals** call him. We never see him teach it. And even if he did: is that a knower or a legislator, a tempter or a religion-founder, a philosopher or a prophet?
- *The Antichrist* (54) calls Zarathustra a **sceptic**, ruled by one "great passion", which Meier identifies as the passion of knowledge: a philosopher. But he bears the **name of a prophet**, and the book ends with talk of his "thousand-year empire". **The drama tests whether one man can be both.**
- **A true Counter-Jesus.** The parody's real target is not the Gathas but the **Bible**, especially the Gospels. Jesus at thirty goes into the wilderness for forty days and then teaches. Zarathustra at thirty goes into the mountains for ten years and comes down at forty. Jesus is named only once in the book (I, "On Free Death": he died too early, and would have recanted his teaching had he lived to Zarathustra's age). The end of Part I parodies the disciples and the Last Discourse.
- **Tragedy through parody.** The parody can't leave its hero untouched. By playing the Counter-Jesus, he's loaded with what "None can accomplish": to unite in himself what can't be united, Two as One. That may be the tragedy.
- Meier also reads the sun speech closely: Zarathustra wants to give because "he is not sufficient unto himself". He *loves* men as recipients of his gift, as what they could become. Love, gift and demand together make the **prophet**.

## 7. Meyer: Tragic Trilogy and Satyr Play

Matthew Meyer, *Routledge Guidebook* (2025).
- **Parts I–III are a tragic trilogy, Part IV a satyr play**, the comic piece that followed three tragedies at the Dionysia. That explains Part IV's tone: the ass, the farce, the drunken song.
- Evidence: the 1881 notebook sketch for a four-part work, with Beethoven's Ninth, *Chaos sive natura*, Prometheus bound; the Dionysian dithyramb in the fourth part; GS 342 *Incipit tragoedia*; amor fati opening GS Book IV; the *Hymn to Life* ("if you have no more happiness to give me, well then! you still have suffering"). See [[Nietzsche: Amor Fati and Becoming What You Are]].
- The tragedy is about **fate**. Naturalism and recurrence mean we can't be otherwise. Like Oedipus, Zarathustra must come to terms with that, and the goal is to restore "the innocence of becoming", curing us of sin, guilt and the bad conscience.
- Z is also N.'s entry in Plato's "ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry", on the side of poetry. Before Z he had tried to write an *Empedocles* tragedy, on Hölderlin's model.

## 8. Jung and Strauss

**Jung** ran a five-year seminar on Z (1934–39), reading it like a patient's dream.
- The **tightrope walker** is a "shadow aspect" of Zarathustra, and so of N.: "a sorry sort of saltimbanque", in a very risky profession where "one easily slips and falls down dead". Jung thinks N. didn't realise the identification, and that the book later contains an unconscious **prophecy of his own fate**.
- The method is reductive, but as a description of the book's emotional logic it's often sharp. If you meet yourself in a dream as someone else, it's because you don't know that side of yourself.

**Strauss** (1959 Chicago course, published 2017) reads Z as an attempt to **restore nature** as an ethical standard and, in Part IV, to **restore the sacred**. Lampert says Strauss in the end judged N. the philosopher who "replaces Plato".

## 9. Comparison: Gospel and Counter-Gospel

| | The Gospels | *Zarathustra* |
|---|---|---|
| **Beginning** | At about thirty, Jesus goes into the wilderness for forty days (Luke 3–4) | At thirty, Zarathustra goes into the mountains for ten years |
| **Descent** | *Kenosis*: "emptied himself" (Phil 2:7) | *Untergang*: "going-under", from an overflow of wisdom |
| **First audience** | "The common people heard him gladly" (Mark 12:37) | The crowd laughs and wants the last man |
| **Temptation** | The devil, in the wilderness | The soothsayer: pity for the higher men, "my final sin" |
| **Disciples** | "Lo, I am with you alway" (Matt 28:20) | "Lose me and find yourselves... when you have all denied me will I return" |
| **Supper** | The Last Supper | "The Supper", then the Ass Festival |
| **Liturgy** | "Blessing, and glory, and wisdom, and thanksgiving..." (Rev 7:12) | The same doxology, sung to an ass, answered "Yea-Yuh" |
| **The dead** | Lazarus raised | The tightrope walker buried in a hollow tree |
| **Climax** | Gethsemane and the Cross: suffering *for* others | The Convalescent: seven days prostrate under his own thought |
| **End** | Resurrection; "I go to prepare a place for you" | "Rise now, rise, thou great noon!", alone, with a lion |

## 10. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What reading it as drama shows**
- It's a better book than its slogans. The doctrines are put into mouths that are *wrong* in instructive ways: the dwarf's easy circle, the animals' "hurdy-gurdy song", the higher men's ass. Read as drama, Z is full of **self-criticism**, and Part II ends with its hero refusing his own thought.
- Rosen's point that "all disciples are inferior" is plainly true *inside the book*: N. couldn't imagine a single worthy follower, only the higher men, who are pathetic. Lampert's "the audience the book creates" is a promissory note. Meier's question (philosopher *or* prophet) is the honest one, and the book doesn't answer it.

**The parody and its original**
- Meier is right that the book is written against the Gospels, sentence by sentence. And a parody depends on what it mocks. Z keeps telling you what it's *not*, and that makes its original stand out more sharply.
- **The descent.** Zarathustra descends because he has too much honey and needs takers. Meier catches it: "he is not sufficient unto himself". Christ's descent is the opposite: he lacks nothing and empties himself (Phil 2:6–7). Zarathustra's gift is a **demand** ("What have you done to overcome him?"). Christ's is **grace**.
- **The disciples.** "When you have all denied me will I return to you" is a direct inversion of Matt 10:33 and of Peter's three denials. And it's sad in a way N. may not have intended. Christ returned to Peter *after* the denial, to restore him: "lovest thou me?" (John 21:15–17). Zarathustra makes denial the *condition* of his return. One is the logic of forgiveness. The other is the logic of a teacher who can't bear to be followed.
- **Pity as the final sin.** The book ends with Zarathustra conquering the temptation of **compassion**. In the Gospel, compassion isn't the temptation, it's the point: "he was moved with compassion on them, because they were as sheep having no shepherd" (Mark 6:34). Christ's "work" and his compassion are the same thing. Zarathustra has to choose between them, and chooses work.
- **The Ass Festival** is the funniest and most revealing part. N. sees that when the old faith dies, the higher men don't become free. They worship the first thing that says yes to everything. That's a sharp prophecy of modern idolatry: politics, celebrity, technology. It's the old observation that people who stop believing in God don't believe in nothing, they believe in anything. But N. has no cure except laughter and a lion.
- **Tragedy without resurrection.** Meyer is right that this is a tragedy about accepting fate. The Christian story isn't a tragedy, because it ends in **Resurrection**, and the cross isn't fate accepted but love freely given ("No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself", John 10:18). "Rise now, rise, thou great noon!" is a resurrection line without anyone being raised. The tightrope walker, the only one who dies, stays in his hollow tree. See [[1 Corinthians 15:26: The Last Enemy That Will Be Destroyed Is Death]].

**What I take from it**
- Read Z like *Faust* or *Moby-Dick*, not like a manifesto. Its hero is a great, lonely, magnificent failure, and that's more honest than any of its slogans. See [[Moby-Dick and the Problem of Evil: Ahab, Ishmael and Job]].
- The crowd that wants the last man is us. The test of any teaching is whether it can love the crowd without despising it. Zarathustra couldn't. That's the difference between a prophet and the Shepherd.
