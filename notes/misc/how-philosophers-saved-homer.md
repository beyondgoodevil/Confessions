For the Greeks, Homer was something like Scripture. Every boy learned him by heart: in Byzantium, thirty lines a day, fifty for the best pupils. Psellus had read the whole *Iliad* by the age of nine. But Homer's gods lie, commit adultery, quarrel and deceive. So the question that runs through a thousand years of Greek thought: **what do you do with an authoritative text that says unworthy things about God?** The main answer was allegory, and the Christians faced the same problem with the Old Testament.

## 1. The Attack

- **Xenophanes** (6th century BC): "Homer and Hesiod have attributed to the gods all things that are shameful among men: stealing, adultery, deceiving one another." He mocks anthropomorphism: if oxen had hands they would draw gods like oxen.
- **Heraclitus**: Homer "deserves to be expelled from the contests and flogged". He couldn't even solve a children's riddle about lice.
- **Pythagorean legend**: Pythagoras saw Homer and Hesiod being punished in Hades for their lies about the gods.
- **Stesichorus' palinode**: Helen never went to Troy; only a phantom (*eidōlon*) did. This later became a philosophical symbol of illusion.
- **Plato's *Republic***:
  - Book 2–3: banish the stories of Zeus's deceptions and of theomachies; strike out Achilles' "I would rather be a serf" so that the guardians won't fear death.
  - Book 10: poetry is an imitation of an imitation, and Homer is "the first of the tragedians".
  - Plato also refuses the allegorical escape: "the young cannot judge what is allegory (*huponoia*) and what is not".
  - But he loves Homer: about 150 quotations. His own myths (Er, the cave) are meant to replace Homer's: "not a tale told to Alcinous, but of a courageous man".

## 2. The First Defences

- **Theagenes of Rhegium** (c. 525 BC) is said to be the first allegorist. He read the battle of the gods (*Iliad* 20–21) as a battle of elements (water against fire) and of moral forces. From the start, allegory goes with **linguistic study**: he also discussed variant readings.
- **The Sophists**: the old poets were "sophists in disguise", hiding wisdom under a popular dress. The "hidden sense" (*huponoia*) is the earlier word for allegory.
  - **Metrodorus of Lampsacus** read the whole *Iliad* as Anaxagoras' cosmology. Agamemnon was the aether, Achilles the sun, Hector the moon. Philodemus called such readings the work of maniacs.
  - **Moralizing readings** were more sober. Socrates (in Xenophon): Circe turned the men into swine through gluttony, and Odysseus was saved by self-restraint.
- **Aristotle** is the decisive non-allegorical answer (*Poetics* 25, *Homeric Problems*):
  - **"Poetry is not subject to the same criteria as other arts."** If a scene works dramatically, minor improbabilities don't matter. Achilles chasing Hector three times round Troy is impossible on stage, but overwhelming in narrative.
  - Morally offensive things can be explained by historical custom, or as what people say, or as idealization.
  - So the measure is no longer "is it literally true?" but "is it effective and plausible?" Homer becomes "the master of fiction" and the father of tragedy.

## 3. Stoic Allegory: Gods as Nature

- The Stoics identified Homer's gods with **cosmic elements and forces**, mainly by **etymology**:
  - Hera = *aēr* (air);
  - Zeus = the fiery aether, or the life (*zēn*) of all;
  - Athena = wisdom;
  - Apollo = the sun.
- Richardson doubts they allegorized whole poems. They used particular passages to support their philosophy.
- **Cornutus**' *Greek Theology* (1st century AD) is "a dry handbook" of this method, with "no real interest in poetry for its own sake".
- **Crates of Mallos** (2nd century BC, Pergamon) read Homer as a source of **cosmology**:
  - Agamemnon's shield is a "representation of the cosmos", its ten circles the heavenly circles, its bosses the stars;
  - Nestor's cup and its doves are the Pleiades;
  - Hephaestus' fall is a measurement of the universe;
  - Homer knew the earth was a sphere.

  He broke his leg in a Roman drain in 168 BC, and his lectures while he recovered started Roman literary scholarship.
- **Eratosthenes**' deflating remark: "you will find the places visited by Odysseus when you have discovered the name of the cobbler who sewed up the bag of the winds".
- **Heraclitus the Allegorist** (*Homeric Problems*, 1st century AD): "if he meant nothing allegorically, he was impious throughout". He reads Apollo's plague arrows as the sun's heat in summer, the shield of Achilles as a cosmos, and Ares and Aphrodite's adultery as the union of strife and love (Empedocles).
- **Ps.-Plutarch**, *On the Life and Poetry of Homer*: Homer is the source of every philosophical school. Seneca's answer: if he is master of all conflicting doctrines, he is master of none.

## 4. Platonist Allegory: Homer the Theologian

Lamberton's thesis: from the 2nd to the 5th century AD the Platonists made Homer a **theologian** (*theologos*) whose poems hide a full **map of reality** and of **the soul's journey**. They read him the way Philo read Moses, and the way the Christians read both.

- **The Pythagorean background**: Homer as a sage; the "Homeric" underworld mapped onto the cosmos (Hades as the Milky Way, where souls gather; milk as souls' first food on entering birth).
- **Philo** (1st century AD, a Jew of Alexandria) allegorized Moses with Greek tools: Adam the "establisher of names". He set the method for Clement and Origen.
- **Numenius** (2nd century), the key figure. He believed one truth had been revealed by Moses, Pythagoras, Plato and Homer alike: "What is Plato but Moses speaking Attic Greek?" He allegorized a Gospel story about Jesus without naming him (Origen reports).
- **Plotinus** treats Homer's myths like Plato's. Kronos is Mind and Zeus the World-Soul. His most famous use: "**Let us flee to the beloved fatherland**" (*Enn.* 1.6.8), Odysseus fleeing Circe and Calypso as the soul fleeing sensible beauty for the One.
- **Porphyry**:
  - *Homeric Questions*: sober philological work, and the principle that **Homer is his own best interpreter**, "Homer explains himself from himself";
  - *On the Cave of the Nymphs* (*Odyssey* 13.102–12), "the earliest surviving interpretive critical essay in the European tradition" (Lamberton).
- **The *Cave of the Nymphs* reading.** At the harbour of Ithaca there is a cave sacred to the nymphs, with stone bowls where bees make honey, stone looms where nymphs weave sea-purple robes, two springs, and two gates, north for mortals and south for gods. At its head is an olive tree.
  - The **cave** is the material cosmos (as in Plato's cave).
  - The **nymphs** are souls descending into birth.
  - Their **sea-purple robes on stone looms** are flesh woven over bone.
  - The **bees** are just souls; honey is purity.
  - The **two gates** are the entrance and exit of souls: north (Cancer) to birth, south (Capricorn) to the gods.
  - The **olive** is Athena's wisdom governing the world.
  - **Odysseus** sitting under it, laying aside his treasures and made a beggar, is the soul that must strip itself of material things and plan with Wisdom how to destroy the passions (the suitors).
  - Odysseus wanders until he reaches the land "where men know not the sea" (Tiresias), when the soul is freed from the material world.
- **Proclus** (5th century), the culmination. In his *Commentary on the Republic* he answers Plato from Plato.
  - **Two kinds of poetry**: inspired, symbolic poetry that conveys divine truth, and merely mimetic poetry. Homer's "scandalous" myths are **symbols** (*sumbola*), not imitations. They must be read "according to the secret doctrine", or they really do harm, as Socrates feared.
  - The **unseemliness is the point**: shocking surface images force the mind beyond the surface. (Compare the Areopagite on "dissimilar similitudes".)
  - **The *Iliad***: Helen is the beauty of the sensible world; Troy (*Ilion*) is matter (*hulē*); the Greeks are souls fighting to return; the ten years are ten millennia of reincarnation.
  - The theomachy shows the gods are one and divided.
  - Even Achilles dragging Hector is defended by realism and tradition.
- **Lamberton's afterword** asks whether this is nonsense. "Garbage in the tradition's computer seems, against all odds, to have generated not garbage out but the *Paradiso*." The Neoplatonist reading passed through Macrobius, Boethius and Fulgentius to the Latin Middle Ages and **Dante**. Ancient "allegorical interpretation" is really just "interpretation", and every modern reader brings presuppositions too (Gadamer).

## 5. The Byzantines (Brisson; Tzetzes)

- **Homer stayed the schoolbook of a Christian empire.** A 13th-century monk was horrified to find "the prophet Achilles" painted in a Thessalian church. A courtier greeted the emperor's mistress with the Trojan elders' line about Helen.
- Paganizing revivals were punished: John Italos (condemned 1082), Gemistos Plethon.
- **The golden chain** (*Iliad* 8.18–27) is the test case. Zeus challenges the gods to hang a golden rope from heaven and try to pull him down; he could pull them all up, earth and sea included.
  - **Plato** (*Theaetetus*): the chain is the sun, which keeps all things moving.
  - **Eustathius** (archbishop of Thessalonica, 12th century) collects the readings: the bond of the four elements; the chain of days; the planetary spheres; the Stoic world-conflagration. One reading is **political**, "an enigma of the monarchy, that everything depends on a single one". Compare [[The Case for One Ruler: Aquinas, Dante, Maistre, Kuehnelt-Leddihn]].
  - **The Neoplatonists**: the "golden chain" of causes linking the One to the lowest things. The phrase became standard for the Platonist succession of teachers and for the chain of being.
- **John Tzetzes**' *Allegories of the Iliad* and *of the Odyssey* (1140s–50s) are written in verse for the German-born Empress Irene (Bertha von Sulzbach), as an easy introduction to "the greatest poet of her adopted country".
  - **Three kinds of allegory**: **physical** (elements), **psychological** (passions and virtues) and **historical/pragmatic** (the gods as ancient kings, or as human qualities). Kronos is historically an infanticide king, physically time or primeval matter, psychologically the ignorance of the intellect.
  - Allegory, he claims, was "a discovery of the Egyptians".
  - For him, Homer "knew how rare wisdom was" and hid it.
- **Psellus** (11th century) went furthest. He tried to harmonize Plato, the Chaldean Oracles, the Orphic hymns and Christian theology, via the theory that the Greeks stole from Moses. He opposed theurgy, but was suspected of paganism anyway. He also used Stoic physical allegory (Tantalus as the aether).
- The Hesychast controversy (Palamas against Barlaam) marks the limit. When philosophy claimed to judge the vision of God, the Church sided with the monks. See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]].

## 6. Pagan Allegory and Christian Typology

The Fathers used the same tools, and some of the same images:
- **Odysseus bound to the mast** passing the Sirens becomes the Christian bound to the Cross passing the world's pleasures: Clement of Alexandria, "Pass by the song; it works death... bound to the wood [of the Cross] thou shalt be freed from destruction" (*Protrepticus* 12); Maximus of Turin.
- **Basil the Great**, *Address to Young Men on Greek Literature*: take from the poets what is useful, as **bees** take from flowers. Praise the virtues in Homer (Odysseus naked before Nausicaa, clothed in virtue), and stop your ears to the rest like Odysseus to the Sirens.
- **Origen** reads Scripture in three senses (body, soul, spirit), on the model of Philo. He is attacked by Celsus for doing to Moses what Greeks did to Homer, and replies by attacking Greek allegory.

**But the Church drew a line between allegory and typology:**

| | **Neoplatonist allegory of Homer** | **Christian typology of Scripture** |
|---|---|---|
| the text's literal sense | often an embarrassment to be escaped ("if he meant nothing allegorically he was impious") | true history, which God himself shaped to point forward |
| what it points to | timeless metaphysics (the soul's descent and ascent, the hypostases) | a **person and events**: Christ, his death and resurrection, the Church |
| who authors the second meaning | the poet's hidden wisdom, or the reader's ingenuity | God, the Lord of history, who "spake by the prophets" |
| the end | the soul's flight from matter and the body | the transfiguration of creation and resurrection of the body |
| control | the school tradition | the Church's rule of faith and liturgy |

- **The Antiochenes** (Diodore, Theodore, Chrysostom) criticized Alexandrian allegory for dissolving history. They insisted on ***theōria*** grounded in the literal sense.
- Paul's own "which things are an allegory" (Gal 4:24) concerns real persons, Sarah and Hagar, whose history God made to signify.

## 7. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's right in the allegorists**
- **They saw that a sacred text has depth.** Not every surface word is the whole meaning. The Fathers agree: the Song of Songs isn't only a love poem, and the Psalms are Christ's prayer.
- **Proclus' point** that shocking images can be deliberate symbols, forcing the mind upward, is close to Dionysius' "dissimilar similitudes". God is described as a worm, a drunken man, a bear robbed of her cubs, because no one will mistake those for literal descriptions. See [[Anthropomorphic Language in the Context of God and the Bible]].
- **Porphyry's principle**, "Homer explains Homer", is the same as the Fathers' "Scripture interprets Scripture".
- **Basil's bee** remains the right Christian attitude to pagan literature: neither burning it nor canonizing it.

**Where it went wrong**
- **Allegory as escape.** Much pagan allegory existed to save an indefensible surface: Zeus's adulteries "really" mean the mixing of elements. The Christian doesn't need to say that David's adultery "really" means something else. Scripture tells the sin plainly, and that is part of its truthfulness. The difference lies in the God the stories reveal: Homer's gods themselves needed rescuing; the God of Israel is holy, and the human sins recorded are judged.
- **Allegory without history** makes everything mean the same thing: the soul's ascent from matter. Every story becomes Plotinus. Christian typology is anchored in particular events (the Exodus, the Cross) and points to a particular person.
- **The body.** Neoplatonist allegory reads Odysseus' homecoming as escape from the body. Christian reading of the same image (the voyage home, the Church as ship, the Cross as mast) ends in the resurrection, not disembodiment. See [[Homer on Life and Death]], [[On the Soul and the Resurrection]].
- **Syncretism.** Numenius' "Plato is Moses speaking Attic" and Psellus' harmonizing of all theologies are the ancestors of perennialism. See [[Thomism Is Perennialism: All Ways Lead to God]] and [[Neoplatonism: Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus]]. The Church kept Homer in the school and out of the sanctuary.
