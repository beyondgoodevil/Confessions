After 11 September 2001 Islamist propaganda called Westerners "crusaders", Western commentators asked whether the crusades explained the present, and popular books answered with the old picture: greedy younger sons and fanatics, Europe's "first colonial wars", "a long act of intolerance in the name of God". Thomas Madden calls the crusades "one of the most misunderstood events in western history" (Madden, p. viii). This note examines the three labels most often attached to them, **holy war**, **pilgrimage** and **colonialism**, using the classic study of the crusade idea (Carl Erdmann), a modern defence (Madden), a study of how crusades were actually organized (Christopher Tyerman), and the history of medieval Europe's expansion (Robert Bartlett).

### 1. A Word That Did Not Exist

"Crusade" is "a modern, not a medieval, word", from *crucesignati*, "those signed by the cross", a term used only occasionally after the twelfth century (Madden, p. 1). The men of 1096 spoke of an *iter*, an *expeditio* or a *peregrinatio* (Baldwin, in Erdmann, p. xv). Any label imposed from outside is therefore an interpretation. The historiography has swung between them:

- religious readings, in which the goal is Jerusalem or aid to the Eastern Christians;
- political readings, with the crusade as "the foreign policy of the papacy";
- economic and colonial readings, in which the crusader states were "colonies" and the movement was driven by trade and land hunger. The colonial theme comes from René Grousset (Baldwin, pp. xvi–xvii).

Over the twentieth century the field moved "away from the political or diplomatic interpretation of the First Crusade to one giving prominence to the religious mentality of the individual crusader" (p. xxvi).

### 2. Holy War: Erdmann's Thesis

Erdmann's book (1935) changed the question. In his preface he writes that "two forces affecting the human spirit came into play in the crusading movement: the idea of pilgrimage to the sites of primitive Christianity and the idea of holy war—knightly combat in the service of the church." The prevailing view had traced the crusade from pilgrimage: peaceful journeys to the Holy Sepulchre turned into armed conquest, and crusades against heretics or pagans were treated as later "aberrations". "This view is erroneous. The 'aberrations' had long been there, and the 'genuine' crusade proceeded from them". What was historically essential was "the 'general' idea of crusade, which was oriented to ecclesiastical objectives as such and not tied to a specific locality, such as Jerusalem" (Erdmann, p. xxxiii).

Erdmann traces how the Church moved:

- from hostility to war, to Augustine's just war;
- then to "mission war";
- then to war in defence of the Church, culminating in the reform popes Leo IX, Alexander II and Gregory VII, who blessed knights fighting under the banner of St Peter (Baldwin, p. xx).

Clermont completed a process in which the profession of arms itself was sanctified. The chroniclers' versions of Urban's sermon put it plainly. Fulcher of Chartres: "Now will those who once were robbers become soldiers of Christ [*Christi milites*]; those who once fought brothers and relatives will justly fight barbarians; those who once were mercenaries for a few farthings will obtain eternal reward." Baldric of Dol: "either abandon the profession of arms or go boldly forth as *Christi milites*". Guibert of Nogent says God "in our time has introduced the holy war so that the knighthood... might have a new way to win salvation. They need not choose the life of a monk" (Erdmann, pp. 336–40). Radulf of Caen's Tancred "was of two minds before and hesitated between the Gospel and the world. But when the profession of arms was carried over to the service of Christ, then this dual reason for fighting inflamed him" (p. 337). Urban proclaimed the Peace of God at the same council. The crusade turned outward the violence of a knightly class that the Church had long failed to tame (pp. 337–39).

Hence Erdmann's famous distinction: Jerusalem was the *Marschziel*, the immediate destination, while the liberation of Eastern Christianity was the *Kriegsziel*, the fundamental aim of the war (Baldwin, p. xxi). He also notes that the sources prefer military language. The army called itself *exercitus Dei*, *militia Christi*, more often than *peregrini* (pp. 340–41).

### 3. Pilgrimage: The Case Against Erdmann

Later scholarship has largely kept Erdmann's holy war and restored the pilgrimage he played down:

- **Hans Eberhard Mayer** called the *Marschziel/Kriegsziel* distinction "perhaps an oversubtle interpretation". He stressed the eleventh-century pilgrimage movement and the departure charters of crusaders as evidence that they saw themselves as pilgrims (Baldwin, p. xxviii; Erdmann, p. 342 n.).
- **H. E. J. Cowdrey** argued that "Jerusalem was from the beginning at the heart of Urban's thinking" (p. xxix).
- **Paul Alphandéry** read the First Crusade as "more a spontaneous popular movement concentrated on Jerusalem than an official ecclesiastical project", charged with eschatological longing (p. xxv).
- **E. O. Blake** saw the crusade as the merger of a popular strand (pilgrimage and meritorious war) with an official one (papal policy). The decisive act was Urban's "granting of the pilgrimage indulgence to a papally directed war" (p. xxx).

Madden and Tyerman stand with the synthesis. Urban "did not overturn the teachings of Augustine but rather blended the Just War theory with other well-established principles such as Christian charity and pilgrimage". Each crusader took a pilgrim's vow to reach the Holy Sepulchre, received remission of sins as a pilgrim would, had his property placed under the Church's protection, and wore a cross sewn on his shoulder. "Crusaders usually referred to themselves as 'pilgrims' or 'cross bearers'" (Madden, pp. 9–10). Tyerman: the Holy Land "existed as a virtual reality in the mentality of late eleventh-century western European Christians, a terrestrial relic, a metaphor for Heaven... the scene of the end of time" (Tyerman, p. 31). Madden: "The goal was now Jerusalem. Byzantium was simply on the way" (p. 8).

**The indulgence** joined the two strands. The Clermont canon was carefully worded: "that journey accounts for all penance [*iter illud pro omni poenitentia reputatur*]", a remission of the penalties imposed by the Church. "But the world took no account of this distinction." The chroniclers, and Urban himself, spoke of "the remission of all sins". Death on the march was soon spoken of as martyrdom (Erdmann, pp. 343–44). Tyerman calls it "a uniquely generous privilege" that "addressed perceived and probably real anxieties among the arms-bearing ruling elite, whose culture of violence, on which their status depended, theoretically clashed with the tenets of their faith" (p. 30).

### 4. What Urban Actually Did

The precedents are clear. Gregory VII planned an expedition to help Byzantium in 1074, with a possible visit to the Holy Sepulchre. But he "did not characterize this planned expedition as a holy war. Instead, he saw it as an errand of mercy and an act of charity" (Madden, p. 7). Tyerman says it failed for want of what Urban later supplied: "there were no specific spiritual privileges... no vows creating a protected status and sense of special community; no distinctive ritual such as taking the Cross; and no international populist campaign of propaganda" (p. 32).

Urban's appeal combined several elements:

- **The Eastern Christians.** "We were stimulating the minds of knights to go on this expedition since they might be able to restrain the savagery of the Saracens by their arms and restore the Christians to their former freedom" (Urban's letter, Tyerman, p. 30; Erdmann, p. 336).
- **Christendom as one body.** "It is no virtue to rescue Christians from the Saracens in one place, only to expose them to the tyranny and oppression of the Saracens in another", he told the Catalans (Tyerman, p. 30).
- **The Cross.** It made "'soldiers of God'... no longer a metaphor" (p. 30).
- **Atrocity stories.** In Robert the Monk's version of the sermon, the Turks desecrate churches and torture Christians (Madden, pp. 8–9).

Tyerman adds that Urban suppressed the Byzantine emperor's appeal at Piacenza. It survives in a single chronicle, so the crusade appeared as a universal papal initiative rather than a favour to Constantinople (p. 33). The response was overwhelming: "perhaps as many as 100,000" took the cross (p. 33). Madden gives about 150,000 taking the cross and about 40,000 marching (p. 12).

### 5. Who Went, and Why

The colonial reading rested on a picture of the crusaders as Europe's castoffs: "aristocratic second or third sons with no claim to their father's lands or title, robber barons, highwaymen, ne'er-do-wells", taking the cross "merely as a pious pretext to enrich themselves". Madden argues this reflected the historians' own age: "Historians who lived through Europe's scramble for empire or the subsequent dissolution of imperialism naturally saw the crusades in that familiar light" (p. 11).

Jonathan Riley-Smith's study of the charters reversed it. "The vast majority of these knightly crusaders were not spare sons but instead the lords of their estates. It was not those with the least to lose who took up the cross, but rather those with the most." Crusading cost "five or six times" a knight's annual income. Families sold or mortgaged land, and most survivors "returned to Europe with neither riches nor land" (Madden, p. 12). Madden concludes that "most noblemen who joined the crusade did so from a simple and sincere love of God", but they were "pious and idealistic, but also crude, arrogant, and, at times, savage" (p. 13).

Erdmann, writing long before the charter studies, had already refused both extremes: "While the thesis that the church's call was their only motive is self-evidently false, the opposite view that its call was ineffectual and a mere façade is equally untrue." What moved the knights "was not only the prospect of payment, booty, and new land, but also that of heavenly reward and the forgiveness of sins" (Erdmann, p. xxxiv).

### 6. Reason and Planning: Tyerman

Tyerman attacks a different myth, that crusades were "hare-brained, feckless, extravagant mirages... the blind leading the deluded". He argues "in almost all respects this image is false" (pp. 1–2).

- **Planning.** Crusades were planned like other wars: propaganda, diplomacy, recruitment "based on pay and contracts", credit finance, shipping, logistics. Richard I took a prefabricated wooden castle to Palestine (p. 3). "The crusades, although not simple, were simply wars" (p. 8).
- **Thinking commanders.** Their leaders were educated men who valued *prudentia*, a quality the chroniclers began attaching to knights in the generation of the First Crusade (pp. 18–25).
- **A considered choice.** The clergy wanted the knight's conversion to the cross to be "a conscious transformation, a genuine, rational, considered choice" (p. 19).

His verdict on the causes is unsparing. "Middle Eastern Islamic rulers between the eleventh and fourteenth centuries posed little direct threat to western Europe. It took a very sophisticated—or sophistical—grasp of what might be termed theopolitics to find a danger" there. "As examples of confected causes for war, those for crusading rank among the most egregious in Eurasian history, considerable monuments to their planners' intellectual, political and promotional ingenuity" (pp. 33–34). The book ends on a paradox. "Viewed objectively, the ambition for western Europeans to occupy Palestine and rule Jerusalem defied political, military and financial sense, a dream from an imagined world. Except it worked." "Reason made religious war possible, a conclusion that might give anyone pause in the twenty-first century" (pp. 295–96).

### 7. Violence: The Rhineland and Jerusalem

The crusade began in blood among Christians' own neighbours. In 1096 bands heading east massacred the Jewish communities of the Rhineland. Madden notes the mixed motives: poverty, resentment of usury, and the logic of fighting Christ's enemies at home (pp. 18–19). Tyerman insists that "if awkwardly for medieval and modern apologists alike, the link between papal crusade rhetoric and populist attacks on the Jews in the Rhineland in 1096 or again in 1147 was direct" (p. 34).

On the fall of Jerusalem in 1099 Madden is at his most defensive. "By the standards of the time... the crusaders would have been justified in putting the entire population of Jerusalem to the sword. Despite later highly exaggerated reports, however, that is not what happened. It is true that many of the inhabitants, both Muslims and Jews, were killed in the initial fray. Yet many were also allowed to purchase their freedom or were simply expelled" (p. 34). The knee-deep rivers of blood were a biblical trope, "never meant to be taken seriously". Tyerman's phrase about modern judgments of crusader "insouciant sadism" (p. 295) shows that the violence remains at the centre of any honest assessment.

### 8. Colonialism: Bartlett and the Expanding Frontier

The colonial reading has a respectable form, which Robert Bartlett gives it. His subject is Latin Europe's expansion from 950 to 1350:

- English colonialism in the Celtic world;
- Germans moving into eastern Europe;
- the Spanish Reconquest;
- "the activities of crusaders and colonists in the eastern Mediterranean" (Bartlett, p. 3).

On this view the crusades are one theatre of a general movement of conquest, settlement and Christianization. Lisbon fell in 1147 "with the help of northern crusaders". In Livonia and Prussia "it was not possible to maintain an effective missionary bishopric without force; in the mental climate of the age of Pope Innocent III it was natural that force meant the crusade" (pp. 12, 18). After 1204 the cathedral chapter of Constantinople was overwhelmingly Venetian: "This was a colonial church" (p. 15). The crusades were "the best example of a papally orchestrated war of conquest", though "orchestration is not the same as playing the instruments" (p. 20).

Bartlett also marks the limits. The Levantine conquests "were less complete and less enduring" than those in Sicily and Spain (p. 13). In the transmarine colonies "the immigrants usually formed only a small minority", an aristocracy, clergy, burgesses and a sprinkling of farmers (p. 111). His closing sentence connects the medieval frontier to the modern empires: the Europeans who sailed to the Americas, Asia and Africa "came from a society that was already a colonizing society. Europe, the initiator of one of the world's major processes of conquest, colonization and cultural transformation, was also the product of one" (p. 314). Erdmann's translator noted the same shift in the field: crusader "colonialism" might be understood "as a cultural as well as an economic phenomenon", and colonies "need not necessarily be linked with a 'mother country' politically" (Baldwin, p. xvii).

Tyerman's conclusion agrees on the frontier and qualifies the causes. Crusading permanently reshaped Prussia, Livonia and al-Andalus, but "the crusades did not create the wars of Christian conquest in Spain or the Baltic. They complemented rather than caused the commercial penetration of western traders into the eastern Mediterranean" (pp. 294–95).

### 9. The Modern Myth

Madden's most important contribution is to show how recent the "colonial" crusade is as an idea:

- **European imperialism.** The French presented the conquest of Algeria (1830) as a successor to St Louis's crusade. After 1918 General Gouraud reportedly said at Damascus: "Behold, Saladin, we have returned." Punch drew Richard the Lionheart looking at Allenby's Jerusalem: "At last, my dream come true" (Madden, p. 215).
- **The historians who inverted it.** Marxist historians accepted the imperial reading but stripped out the idealism. Runciman's *History* (1951–54) fixed the popular picture: "the Holy War itself was nothing more than a long act of intolerance in the name of God, which is the sin against the Holy Ghost" (p. 216).
- **The Middle East.** The crusades "were virtually unknown in the Muslim world even a century ago". The Arabic term *harb al-salib* entered the language only in the mid-nineteenth century, and the first Arabic history of the crusades dates from 1899. Saladin had been largely forgotten until European romance and Kaiser Wilhelm II's restoration of his tomb returned him to Arab memory (pp. 217–19).

The "long memory" is "a constructed memory". "It is not the crusades... that led to the attacks of September 11, but the artificial memory of the crusades constructed by modern colonial powers and passed down by Arab nationalists and Islamists" (pp. 218, 222).

Madden goes further than his evidence when he concludes that "the crusades contributed nothing to the decline of the Muslim world" and "are evidence of the decline of the Christian West" (p. 222). His conclusion also insists that the crusade "was an act of piety, charity, and love". Modern condemnations "tell us more about the observer than the observed", for the medieval crusader "would be just as quick to condemn us" (p. 223).

### 10. Assessment

**Holy war: yes, and this is the most secure label.** Erdmann's demonstration stands. The crusade grew out of the reform papacy's sanctification of knighthood: the *militia Christi*, the banner of St Peter, the Peace of God turned outward. The chroniclers' Urban addresses knights as knights and offers them salvation in their own profession. Tyerman's account of Clermont as a *praelium sanctum* in deliberate contrast to the Church's earlier just wars (p. 29) confirms that something new was being offered: war as penance rather than war needing penance.

**Pilgrimage: yes, and Erdmann underrated it.** The vow, the cross, the indulgence, the protection of property, the self-designation as *peregrini*, and the overwhelming pull of Jerusalem all show that participants understood the expedition as an armed pilgrimage. Cowdrey's case that Jerusalem was central for Urban from the start, and Mayer's charter evidence, have mostly carried the day. The best formulation is Blake's: the crusade was the fusion of popular pilgrimage piety with papal holy war, sealed by the indulgence.

**Colonialism: partly, and only in Bartlett's sense.** As a theory of *motive*, the colonial reading has collapsed. The castoff younger son seeking land is not who went, the expedition ruined more families than it enriched, and most survivors came home. As a description of *effects* and of the *society* that crusaded, it has real force. Crusading was one instrument in Latin Europe's expansion. It produced settler aristocracies, colonial churches and Venetian trading colonies. Above all it shaped the Baltic and Iberian frontiers, where conquest proved permanent. The nineteenth-century use of the crusades to justify empire, which Madden documents, should not be read back into 1095. Nor should Madden's corrective be allowed to obscure Bartlett's point that medieval Europe was already a conquering and colonizing civilization.

**On Madden.** His book is valuable for the historiography and for demolishing the "long memory" myth. It is weakest where it becomes apologetic: the defence of 1099 "by the standards of the time", the claim that the crusades did nothing to harm the Muslim world, and the insistence on "love". Tyerman is the better counterweight. He is just as dismissive of the stereotype of medieval stupidity, yet blunt that the causes were "confected" and the link to the anti-Jewish massacres "direct".

**On "defensive" war.** Madden places the crusades in a long Christian response to Muslim conquest: Spain, Sicily, Manzikert. That context is real. Urban appealed to the plight of the Eastern Christians, and the Turkish advance into Anatolia was recent and dramatic. But Tyerman's objection holds for the Holy Land itself: Syria and Palestine posed no threat to France or Germany. The "defence" was of Christendom imagined as one body and of holy places held to be Christ's patrimony, a theological rather than a strategic claim.

### Conclusion

The First Crusade was all three things, but not equally. It was a holy war, the culmination of a century in which the reform papacy turned knighthood into a Christian vocation and promised salvation to those who fought for the Church. It was a pilgrimage, undertaken under vow to the Holy Sepulchre by men who called themselves pilgrims and expected the remission of their sins. And it was, in effect though not in motive, part of the conquest and colonization by which Latin Europe pushed out its frontiers from Lisbon to Livonia. The modern habit of calling it colonialism, whether to praise it as Gouraud did or to condemn it as Runciman did, says more about the nineteenth and twentieth centuries than about 1095. The medieval sources describe something stranger: an army of penitents, rationally organized and financed, marching two thousand miles to win salvation by killing. In Tyerman's words, reason made religious war possible.
