title: Why Christ's Two Natures Can Not Mix
tags: [theology, christology]
summary: Why a single mixed nature in Christ would produce a "Tertium Quid", neither truly God nor truly man.
---

If one were to claim that there is only a single nature in each hypostasis, it could lead to what might be described as a "Tertium Quid" in the Incarnation. The idea here is that such a view could produce a confusion in the person of Christ, resulting in a "third thing" that is neither truly human nor truly divine. Here's why:

1. **The Problem of Mixing Natures**: In the traditional Orthodox (Eastern Orthodox) understanding of the Incarnation, the divine Word (Logos) took on human nature without mixing or confusing the two natures. This means that in Christ, the two natures—divine and human—remain distinct and united in one Person (hypostasis). If one were to suggest that there is only one nature in the hypostasis of Christ, it might imply that the divine and human natures have been confused or blended in a way that results in something "other" than true humanity or true divinity—what could be called a "third thing" (Tertium Quid).
2. **The Dangers of Over-Simplification**: The "Tertium Quid" problem arises when someone tries to over-simplify the mystery of the Incarnation. For example, if one argues that the divine and human natures of Christ merge into one new nature in the hypostasis of the Logos, this would undermine the doctrine of Christ's full divinity and full humanity. It could imply that Christ is neither fully God nor fully man, but a "third" nature that is neither fully divine nor fully human, which would contradict the clear teachings of the Councils (especially Chalcedon) that Christ is fully God and fully man.
3. **The Role of the Councils**: The Council of Chalcedon (451) decisively rejected any notion that would lead to a "third nature" in Christ. It affirmed the doctrine of the "two natures" (dyophysitism) in Christ, maintaining that the divine and human natures are distinct but united in one hypostasis. This avoids the confusion of a "Tertium Quid," preserving both the full divinity and full humanity of Christ.
4. **Theological Implication**: If one were to argue that each hypostasis has only one nature, there could be a risk of falling into a position where the distinction between the divine and human natures in Christ is lost, thus creating a theological "third thing." This would undermine the very heart of the Christian understanding of the Incarnation: that the Son of God became fully human and fully divine without confusing the two natures, and thus He could truly be both Savior (by virtue of His divinity) and truly human (able to suffer and die).

In conclusion, if one were to insist that there is only one nature in each hypostasis, it would risk the creation of a "Tertium Quid"—a heretical idea that undermines the full divinity and full humanity of Christ by merging the two natures in a way that produces something neither fully divine nor fully human. The correct understanding, as affirmed in the Chalcedonian Definition, maintains the distinction of the two natures in the one Person of Christ.
