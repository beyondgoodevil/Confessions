Hume's starting point is empiricism taken all the way. If every idea comes from experience, what experience gives us the idea of **cause**, and what justifies expecting the **future** to be like the past? His answer to both: nothing that reason can supply. Mostly from the *Enquiry*, §§2-7.

## 1. The Framework

**Impressions and ideas** (§2)
- **Impressions**: lively perceptions (seeing red, feeling anger).
- **Ideas**: fainter copies of impressions (remembering or imagining red).
- **Copy principle**: every simple idea comes from a prior impression. Test for any philosophical term: "from what impression is that supposed idea derived?" If none, it's meaningless.

**Hume's fork** (§4)

| Relations of ideas | Matters of fact |
|---|---|
| maths, logic: "3 × 5 = 15" | "the sun will rise tomorrow" |
| known by thought alone | known only by experience |
| denial is a contradiction | denial is perfectly conceivable |
| certain | at best probable |

Everything we believe about the world beyond present perception and memory is a **matter of fact**, and rests on **cause and effect**.

The famous last line of the *Enquiry*: take any book of divinity or school metaphysics. Does it contain abstract reasoning about quantity or number? Experimental reasoning about matter of fact? No? "Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

## 2. Causation

**We never see necessity** (§4, §7)
- Watch one billiard ball hit another. You see: motion, contact, then motion of the second ball. You don't see any **power** or **necessary connection** passing between them.
- Adam, with perfect reason but no experience, couldn't have known from water's look that it would drown him, or from fire that it would burn.
- Nor do we perceive power in our own will: we don't know *how* the will moves the arm.
- Almost all of this is borrowed from Malebranche, who used the same arguments to conclude that God alone is a cause. Hume's one new move was to apply the test to God as well. See [[Malebranche: Occasionalism and Seeing All Things in God]].

**So where does the idea come from?**
- After seeing A followed by B many times (**constant conjunction**), the mind forms a **habit**: on seeing A, it expects B.
- That felt determination of the mind *is* the impression from which the idea of necessary connection is copied. We project it onto the objects.
- "Necessity is something that exists in the mind, not in objects."

**Two definitions of cause** (§7)
1. "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second" (regularity)
2. "an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" (psychological)

**Interpretations**
- **Traditional**: Hume is a regularity theorist: causation just *is* constant conjunction.
- **"New Hume"** (Strawson, Wright): Hume believes in real causal powers but says we can't *know* them.

## 3. The Problem of Induction

(Hume doesn't use the word "induction"; he talks about reasoning "concerning matters of fact".)

1. All reasoning about matters of fact rests on cause and effect.
2. All knowledge of cause and effect comes from experience.
3. All reasoning from experience assumes that **the future will resemble the past** (the uniformity of nature).
4. Can this principle be proved?
    - **Not by demonstration** (relations of ideas): it's conceivable that the course of nature changes. No contradiction in bread failing to nourish tomorrow.
    - **Not by probable reasoning** (experience): any argument from experience already assumes it. "It has worked in the past, so it will work in the future" is itself an inductive argument. **Circular**.
5. So our belief that the future will resemble the past has **no rational foundation**.

Hume's own answer is **sceptical but practical**: we do it from **custom or habit**, which nature has made unavoidable. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life." The sceptic's arguments are irrefutable but can't be acted on: a game of backgammon and they look "cold, and strained, and ridiculous".

## 4. Replies

| Reply | Who | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthetic a priori** | Kant | causation is a category the mind brings to experience; experience is impossible without it. Answers Hume by making causation necessary for *us*, but at the cost of the thing in itself. Kant: Hume "woke me from my dogmatic slumber" |
| **Common sense** | Thomas Reid | we have a first principle of trusting our faculties and the uniformity of nature. Hume trusts reason and the senses selectively; that's arbitrary |
| **Dissolution** | Strawson | asking for justification of induction as a whole is confused: being reasonable *means* reasoning inductively |
| **Falsification** | Popper | science doesn't use induction; it conjectures and refutes. Critics: then why rely on well-tested theories? |
| **Bayesian** | | updating probabilities. Still needs priors that favour uniformity |
| **Inference to the best explanation** | Armstrong, Foster | regularities are best explained by real laws or necessitation relations; once you have those, induction follows |
| **Powers / natures** | Aristotelians (Feser, Mumford) | things have **natures** and **causal powers**. Fire burns because it's *fire*. Hume's mistake was to look at events and not substances. The regularity comes from what things are |

## 5. Hume and God

- **The cosmological argument**: if causation is only constant conjunction, there's no basis for inferring a cause of the whole universe, which has only been observed once. And the "principle of causality" isn't self-evident: we can conceive of something beginning without a cause. See [[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]], [[Natural Theology: Is It Legitimate]].
- **The design argument**: *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*: the analogy between the world and a machine is weak; the designer might be many gods, or an infant god, or dead.
- **Miracles** (*Enquiry* §10): a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and the evidence for a law is uniform experience. So testimony to a miracle can never outweigh it. See [[Revelation and Miracles]]. Critics: if laws are only regularities, as Hume says, nothing *violates* them; and the argument begs the question by assuming "uniform" experience against the testimony.
- **Is/ought**: the same empiricism produces the gap between facts and values. See [[Is Ought]].

## 6. Notes

*Mine.*

- Hume shows where strict empiricism goes: no causes, no self (a "bundle of perceptions"), no induction, no morality beyond feeling. It's a *reductio* of empiricism more than a discovery about the world. See [[Critique of Empiricism]].
- The Aristotelian reply is strongest: Hume can't see power because he's looking for an **impression** of it. But natures aren't objects of sense; they're grasped by the intellect (*nous*). See [[Nous]], [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]].
- The theist has a ground for induction Hume lacks: the world is made by a rational and faithful God, who gives each thing its *logos*. Uniformity isn't a brute fact but God's faithfulness: "While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest... shall not cease" (Gen 8:22). Compare [[TAG]].
- Hume also undercuts himself: the copy principle isn't a relation of ideas or a matter of fact, so by his own fork it should be committed to the flames.
