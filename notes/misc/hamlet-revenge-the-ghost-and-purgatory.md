> "Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." (Rom 12:19)

The **standard assumption** goes like this: Hamlet has a sacred duty to avenge his father, and the play is about why he delays. Coleridge: he thinks too much. Freud: Oedipus. But Hamlet is a revenge play written for a Christian audience, every one of whom heard every Sunday that private revenge is a mortal sin. So the real question is whether Shakespeare's audience was meant to want Hamlet to kill Claudius at all.

## 1. Achilles or Christ?

Cantor's frame:
- "Achilles and Jesus have diametrically opposed attitudes towards vengeance, and it is hard to see how anyone could reconcile the two positions."
- The Renaissance was "an uneasy alliance of classical and Christian elements". Revenge was the issue "most perfectly calculated to expose the inner tensions of the age".
- **The Ghost itself is double.** In armour, crying for revenge, it calls up the world of Norse saga and epic heroism. Rising from what seems to be Purgatory, "it shatters the narrow bounds of the pagan imagination and opens a window on the eternal vistas of Christianity". Hamlet is "a modern Christian charged with the ancient pagan task of revenge".

## 2. What Elizabethans Were Taught About Revenge (Prosser ch. 1)

Prosser read the homilies, treatises and sermons:
- **Revenge is God's**: "Vengeance is mine". The private avenger usurps God's office and **endangers his own soul**. "Who art thou that judgest another's servant?" (Rom 14:4).
- **The proper channel is the magistrate**, "the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil" (Rom 13:4).
- **Even the gravest sinner must be left to God.** The Christian's real enemy is his own sin.
- **Anger and the desire for revenge** are "proper unto wilde beasts".
- **The duel** was condemned for crimes: "the punishment of wilfull acts contrarie to peace, is by civill authoritie to be performed".
- But people felt the pull. Prosser: "Few of us have been led by ambition to consider murder, but all of us have been prompted... to consider 'getting even'." Elizabethans sympathized with revengers without abandoning the teaching.

## 3. The Revenge Play Tradition (Prosser ch. 2; Cantor §6)

- **Kyd's *The Spanish Tragedy*** (c. 1587) founded the genre: a ghost, a play-within-the-play, madness and suicide.
  - Hieronimo begins "*Vindicta mihi!*", quoting Romans 12:19 in Latin, and almost decides to wait for heaven.
  - Then he picks up his Seneca: "the safe path for crime is always through crime".
  - Cantor: "Kyd goes out of his way to associate thoughts of active vengeance with classical literature." Kyd's ghost rises from a **classical** underworld, Charon and all.
- **Tourneur's *The Atheist's Tragedy*** (1611) is an inversion of Hamlet: the murdered father's ghost appears to forbid revenge. "Attend with patience the success of things, / But leave revenge unto the King of kings." Charlemont: "You torture me between the passion of my blood and the religion of my soul." He triumphs by patience: "patience is the honest man's revenge". Cantor calls this "a Christian revaluation of values in the exact Nietzschean sense".
- **Marston's *Antonio's Revenge***: a Stoic refuses revenge.
- **Prosser's survey** of every relevant play to 1610 found "not one example of a noble revenger who sought the damnation of his victim, not one example of a play in which revenge was clearly portrayed as a moral duty". Revengers who set out on revenge become villains or are destroyed. "Revenge was a sin against God, a defiance of the State, a cancer that could destroy mind, body, and soul — and that was that."
- **Shakespeare elsewhere** (ch. 3): Titus "has lost all claim to virtue". Edgar meets the dying Edmund with "Let's exchange charity". Henry V's traitors repent because of his compassion. Lear's "nobler reason" over his fury is the virtue that wins.

## 4. The Ghost: Two Readings

### Prosser: "Spirit of health or goblin damned?" A devil

- **The orthodoxy of ghosts.** Protestants (Lavater, King James) held that the dead don't return, so apparitions are angels or, usually, devils assuming the shape of the dead. Catholics allowed souls from Purgatory, but warned that most such appearances are devils. "The Protestant and Catholic positions were thus much closer than is usually thought."
- **The tests.** Good spirits "at first inspire a certain awe and reverential fear" but "never depart without reassuring and comforting... enflaming in men the love of things divine". Evil spirits do the opposite. A devil "may... for some dark design seem to applaud and exhort at first", "but he will very soon try to tempt men out of the right path".
- **The signs in the text**:
  - it appears at midnight, silent, "in that fair and warlike form";
  - Horatio "crosses" it, risking that it "blast" him;
  - **it flees at cockcrow**, "like a guilty thing upon a fearful summons", and Marcellus adds the lore that at Christmas, when the cock crows all night, "no spirit dare stir";
  - it calls for **murder**;
  - in the cellarage it moves underground, and Hamlet addresses it mockingly as "old mole", "truepenny", "this fellow in the cellarage", hardly how one speaks to a holy soul.
- Hamlet himself suspects it: "The spirit that I have seen / May be a devil: and the devil hath power / To assume a pleasing shape... / Abuses me to damn me" (2.2).
- **The play-test proves less than it seems.** The Mousetrap shows that Claudius is guilty, but a devil can tell the truth to tempt. Banquo: "oftentimes, to win us to our harm, / The instruments of darkness tell us truths" (*Macbeth* 1.3).
- **Its fruit**: what follows the Ghost's command is Ophelia's madness and death, Polonius killed, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern sent to death "not shriving-time allowed", Gertrude poisoned, Laertes, Hamlet himself. A tree is known by its fruit.

### Sister Miriam Joseph: a soul from Purgatory, rightly discerned

- **The discernment of spirits** (1 John 4:1, "try the spirits whether they are of God"; 1 Cor 12:10). The classical procedure tests three possibilities in turn:
  1. **natural**: hallucination or fantasy;
  2. **an evil spirit**;
  3. **a good spirit** (God, an angel, or a sanctified soul).
- **The play follows these steps exactly**:
  - Horatio: "'tis but our fantasy". Then "Before my God, I might not this believe / Without the sensible and true avouch / Of mine own eyes". Natural explanation ruled out.
  - "Be thou a spirit of health or goblin damn'd... / Thou com'st in such a questionable shape" (Hamlet, 1.4). Good or evil?
  - After the interview: "It is an honest ghost" and "**by Saint Patrick**", the keeper of Purgatory (St Patrick's Purgatory, Lough Derg).
  - Doubts return later, as St Teresa says they do after any vision. Hence the Mousetrap: "I'll have grounds more relative than this".
  - After the test: "I'll take the ghost's word for a thousand pound."
- **The Ghost's own words are purgatorial**: "Doom'd for a certain term to walk the night, / And for the day confin'd to fast in fires, / Till the foul crimes done in my days of nature / Are burnt and purg'd away" (1.5). It laments dying "Unhousel'd, disappointed, unanel'd", without communion, confession or anointing, "with all my imperfections on my head". It matches *The Golden Legend*, Thomas More and Aquinas.
- **Its charity toward Gertrude**: "Leave her to heaven, / And to those thorns that in her bosom lodge" (1.5). Later: "O, step between her and her fighting soul" (3.4). No devil prescribes Christian forbearance (West).
- **Is the command just?** Aquinas: a magistrate may put an evildoer to death for the common good. Here the magistrate *is* the murderer. "Something is rotten in the state of Denmark."
  - Claudius won the throne by murder and so is not God's deputy.
  - Hamlet is the named heir (1.2), and so morally the rightful authority.
  - A command from a good spirit can only come with God's permission. Compare the Levites commanded to slay at the golden calf (Exod 32:27), which Aquinas treats as God acting through them.
- **"Taint not thy mind"** (1.5) is the key qualification. "There is an essential difference between acting as a privately commissioned executioner and seeking private revenge." Aquinas: if the avenger's intention "rests" in the other's evil, "his vengeance is altogether unlawful: because to take pleasure in another's evil belongs to hatred".
- Her conclusion: Hamlet is morally free to act. Whether he taints his mind "is the question on which hinges the tragedy".

## 5. The Crucial Scenes

**"To be or not to be"** (3.1)
- Cantor: Hamlet "would have no difficulty in embracing suicide if he were a pagan". It's the **afterlife** that stops him: "in that sleep of death what dreams may come... / The undiscover'd country, from whose bourn / No traveller returns". His first soliloquy: "Or that the Everlasting had not fix'd / His canon 'gainst self-slaughter!"
- "Thus conscience does make cowards of us all... / And enterprises of great pitch and moment... / Lose the name of action."
- Prosser reads it as a **moral** choice. "Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer / The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune, / Or to take arms" is patience against revenge. "Conscience" is literally conscience, not just "thinking". When he resolves to set conscience aside, the Nunnery Scene follows, his cruelty to Ophelia: "the first ominous results of his decision to reject coward conscience".

**The prayer scene** (3.3), "literally the central issue" (Cantor)
- Claudius kneels: "O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven; / It hath the primal eldest curse upon't, / A brother's murder." That is Cain (Gen 4).
- He knows the theology of repentance exactly. He cannot repent because he will not give up the fruits: "My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen. / May one be pardon'd and retain the offence?" "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below: / Words without thoughts never to heaven go." Prosser calls it "the profound exposition of orthodox Christian concepts".
- **Hamlet finds him alone and doesn't strike, because he might go to heaven**: "Then trip him, that his heels may kick at heaven, / And that his soul may be as damn'd and black / As hell, whereto it goes."
  - Dr Johnson: "too horrible to be read or to be uttered".
  - Coleridge and Hazlitt: an excuse for delay, he can't mean it.
  - Prosser began her book here. Was it possible "that Shakespeare expected his audience to approve of Hamlet's pagan vindictiveness when he had explicitly thrown the scene into a Christian context?"
  - Cantor: Christianity forbids revenge, yet "offers a pattern of revenge more sinister than anything imagined in classical antiquity". "Achilles has no interest in what is going on in Hector's soul." Hamlet wants vengeance on an immortal soul. That is exactly what "taint not thy mind" forbids.
- **The irony**: Claudius' prayer failed. Hamlet couldn't know that. Revenge depends on reading another's soul, which only God can do.

**"Heaven's scourge or minister?"** (3.4). After killing Polonius blindly through the arras: "heaven hath pleas'd it so... / That I must be their scourge and minister." Prosser: the terms are "irreconcilable". A **scourge** is a wicked instrument God uses and then destroys (like Assyria, "the rod of mine anger", Isa 10:5). A **minister** is a righteous agent. Hamlet claims the second while acting like the first. "In all charity, we can only call his attitude self-righteous."

**The closet scene** (3.4). Gertrude sees "such black and grained spots" in her soul. Prosser: if Hamlet paused, she might repent. The Ghost appears again, this time "in his habit as he lived", not in armour, to protect her.

## 6. Act 5: "The Readiness Is All"

- **The change.** Back from the sea, Hamlet speaks of providence: "There's a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will." "There's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow" (Matt 10:29). "If it be now, 'tis not to come... the readiness is all."
- **The graveyard.** Yorick's skull: "Alexander died, Alexander was buried, Alexander returneth into dust". It's the *memento mori* of sermons and the vanity of glory: "Imperious Caesar, dead and turn'd to clay, / Might stop a hole to keep the wind away."
- **Prosser** (ch. 9): a real conversion. Hamlet "has come to see death, and thus life, in the context of eternity". "Since... the hour of death is hidden from him, man must keep his soul prepared." This is Christian readiness, not Stoic fatalism. He no longer plans revenge. When he kills Claudius, it is unplanned and immediate, as the King's treachery is exposed, more an act of judgement than a vendetta. "In the first part of the play, a very young man defiantly shouts 'No,' but he is transformed in the fifth act when he finds his being in 'Yes.'"
- **Cantor** dissents (§10):
  - Hamlet's providence is **fatalism and indifference**: "Thou wouldst not think how ill all's here about my heart — but it is no matter."
  - He disowns responsibility to Laertes: "Was't Hamlet wrong'd Laertes? Never Hamlet... His madness."
  - He sent Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to death "not shriving-time allow'd" with a clear conscience.
  - His walking into a rigged fencing match is "a suicidal non-suicide", what the gravediggers parody: "if the water come to him and drown him, he drowns not himself".
  - The **ending** is catastrophic: everything the Ghost forbade has happened. His mother is dead, his mind tainted, and the throne is handed to Norway, his father's old enemy.
  - "The conflict between the classical and Christian elements" has no resolution: "no resolution of his problem is possible."
- **Horatio**, the sceptical "antique Roman", wants to die with him. Hamlet stops him: "Absent thee from felicity awhile... / To tell my story." His blessing: "Good night, sweet prince, / And flights of angels sing thee to thy rest!"

## 8. With the *Oresteia* and Girard

- **Orestes and Hamlet** are both sons commanded by a god or ghost to avenge a murdered father on a usurper married to the mother. Goldhill makes the comparison himself. See [[Justice in the Oresteia: From Vendetta to the Court?]].
  - Orestes kills his mother. Hamlet is told not to touch her.
  - Orestes needs a court. Hamlet's Denmark has none: the judge is the murderer.
  - Orestes is pursued by the Furies. Hamlet is tormented by conscience and by the fear of "the undiscover'd country".
  - The *Oresteia* ends with a court founded. *Hamlet* ends with every court corrupt and the throne given to a foreigner.
- **Girard** wrote on Hamlet in *A Theater of Envy* (1991), which I haven't read here. His thesis there is that Hamlet's reluctance is the reluctance of a modern conscience faced with an obligation to repeat violence. Revenge is mimesis: Laertes and Fortinbras are Hamlet's doubles, the avengers he can't wholeheartedly be. Compare [[Girard's Scapegoat Mechanism: Sacrifice, Myth and the Gospels]].

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What Prosser gets right**
- The play really is soaked in Christian categories: Purgatory, sacraments, repentance, providence, the canon against self-slaughter, Cain. To read it as if revenge were simply a duty is to ignore everything Shakespeare put in.
- **The prayer scene** decides it. Wanting another's damnation is the opposite of the Gospel: "Love your enemies... pray for them which despitefully use you" (Matt 5:44); God "will have all men to be saved" (1 Tim 2:4). Whatever the Ghost is, Hamlet's wish there is diabolical, and he says so himself in the language of hell.
- **The fruits test** is the Fathers' own test of spirits: "by their fruits ye shall know them" (Matt 7:20). Good spirits bring peace, humility and love. Evil ones bring agitation, pride and despair (Athanasius' *Life of Antony*, the source of the whole tradition Sister Miriam Joseph cites).

**What Miriam Joseph adds**
- **Justice isn't vengeance.** Killing a usurping murderer for the common good, when there is no other authority, is not the same as private revenge. Rom 13 gives the sword to the ruler; Hamlet may be the rightful one. Orthodox tradition also distinguishes killing in war or to protect others (which still calls for penance; St Basil's canon 13) from murder.
- **"Taint not thy mind"** is the heart of it. The same act can be justice or sin, depending on the heart. "Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer" (1 John 3:15). This is the patristic emphasis on the *logismoi*: the inner motive, not just the outer deed. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].

**On Purgatory**
- The Ghost's "fires" that "burn and purge away" crimes are the Western doctrine of Purgatory, a place of satisfaction for temporal punishment. The Orthodox Church prays for the dead and believes the prayers of the Church help them, but rejects a Purgatory of satisfying punishment by fire (Florence, 1438–9: Mark of Ephesus). For Orthodoxy the Ghost's plea, "remember me", is exactly right: the dead ask for remembrance, the *mnēmosynon*, prayer and the Liturgy. Its demand for blood is exactly wrong. No soul being healed by God asks for murder. On that point the Orthodox answer sides with Prosser.

**On Act 5**
- Cantor sees fatalism. Prosser sees a real turn to providence. I lean to Prosser but keep Cantor's warning. "The readiness is all" is close to the Christian *memento mori* and abandonment to providence. But readiness for one's own death isn't the same as repentance for the blood already shed. Hamlet never repents Polonius, or Rosencrantz and Guildenstern. That is the remaining tragedy. The play shows a soul that came to the edge of the Gospel and didn't fully cross.
