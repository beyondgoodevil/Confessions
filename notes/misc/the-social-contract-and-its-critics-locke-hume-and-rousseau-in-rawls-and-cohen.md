The social contract says that political authority rests on agreement. Free and equal people agreed, or would agree, to be governed, and that agreement makes the government legitimate and binds them to obey it. The idea was put to work by Hobbes and Locke in the seventeenth century and by Rousseau in the eighteenth. Hume attacked it, and the attack was so effective that, as Rawls notes, Locke's doctrine had no real successors in England (Rawls, p. 170). Then John Rawls revived the contract in *A Theory of Justice* (1971).

This note follows the tradition through two sets of lectures. Rawls taught the history of political philosophy at Harvard from the 1960s to 1995. G. A. Cohen, the Marxist philosopher whose work Rawls credited with changing his own reading of Marx (Rawls, p. x), taught Hobbes, Locke and Hume at Oxford. Both sets of lectures were published after their authors' deaths. Reading them side by side is useful because they agree about much and read in very different ways.

Sections 1–2 cover the two lecturers and Rawls's map of contract doctrines. Section 3 is a short account of Hobbes. Sections 4–8 are Locke, sections 9–11 Hume's critique, and sections 12–14 Rousseau. Section 15 compares the two lecturers.

### 1. Two lecturers

The two editors describe the difference at the outset.

**Rawls**, as Samuel Freeman's foreword reports, set himself two rules. The first was to pose each writer's problem as the writer saw it, following Collingwood's remark that the history of political philosophy is "the history of a problem more or less constantly changing, whose solution was changing with it" (Rawls, p. xiii). The second was to present each doctrine at its strongest, following Mill: "A doctrine is not judged at all until it is judged in its best form" (p. xiii). He added a maxim of his own: "I always assumed ... that the writers we were studying were always much smarter than I was. ... If I saw a mistake in their arguments, I supposed they ... saw it too and must have dealt with it, but where? So I looked for their way out, not mine" (pp. xiii–xiv). He was "loath to raise objections to the exemplars—that's too easy and misses what is essential" (p. xiv).

**Cohen**, in Jonathan Wolff's account, worked the other way. Rawls "aims to give a fair and complete overview ... generally assuming that great philosophers do not make obvious mistakes, and so reinterpretation is called for. Cohen, in contrast, essentially discusses those things that interest him." For Cohen, "to respect a thinker is to engage with the arguments, following the logic wherever it leads" (Cohen, p. x). Cohen's lectures on Hobbes, Locke and Hume were "written up as a single set of connected lectures, all of which take political obligation as their central focus" (p. viii). Cohen himself said they were "stimulating and interesting," and that their weakness was that "they don't refer at all to recent scholarly literature" (p. vii).

These two methods produce different results throughout. Rawls usually finds a reading on which the writer is right about his own problem. Cohen usually finds the place where the argument breaks.

### 2. Rawls's map of the contract

Rawls's introduction sets out what he takes to be liberalism's central thesis: "A legitimate regime is such that its political and social institutions are justifiable to all citizens—to each and every one—by addressing their reason" (Rawls, p. 13). The social contract is one way of making this precise. It shows that each person has sufficient reason to agree to the political order, on the condition that others agree too.

He then separates questions that contract theories often run together (pp. 14–15):

1. **Actual or non-historical agreement.** Locke seems to mean an agreement that people actually make. Kant means "merely an idea of reason, which none the less has undoubted practical reality": a law is unjust if "a whole people could not possibly agree to it" (p. 14).
2. **How the content is fixed.** Either by the terms of an actual agreement, or by working out what people in a given situation would or could agree to. The second makes the contract hypothetical.
3. **Could or would.** It is much easier to show what people could not possibly agree to than what they would agree to. Locke needs only the first. To condemn Charles II he has to show that the people "could not possibly have agreed to royal absolutism" (p. 15).
4. **Legitimacy or obligation.** A contract can be used to say when a form of government is legitimate, or to say why particular citizens are obliged to obey it. These are different jobs. Rawls states his verdict on Hume in advance: "Hume's critique of the social contract view is effective for Locke's account of political obligation, but it doesn't touch Locke's account of legitimacy" (p. 15).

Every contract theory also needs an "initial situation": who the parties are, what they want and what they know (pp. 16–20). Rawls argues that less knowledge can produce a fairer agreement. His example is Elster's tennis match stopped by rain after three sets, where every party argues from what it already knows (p. 17). This is the root of his "veil of ignorance." He insists the idea is "not a strange idea, a philosopher's fancy, but a rather common and I think highly intuitive idea" (p. 20). All contract theories, he adds, "normalize" the interests of the parties. For Hobbes these are self-preservation, the good of one's family and "the means of commodious living"; for Locke, "lives, liberties, and estates"; for Rousseau, the interests of free and perfectible beings in equal standing (p. 226).

### 3. Hobbes in brief

Hobbes is treated at length in [[The Modern Break: Machiavelli and Hobbes]], so only the two lecturers' distinctive points are given here.

**Rawls** reads *Leviathan* as "the greatest single work of political thought in the English language" (Rawls, p. 23). His reason is not that it is true. It presents "a dreaded way of thinking about society which almost might be true." He sets aside Hobbes's theology and materialism and reads a "secular moral system" (pp. 26–30). On his reading, the state of nature is not a past condition. It is "an ever present possibility of degeneration into discord and civil war" (p. 32), so every rational person has reason to want an existing sovereign to continue. The argument does not need anyone to be wicked. Equal vulnerability, scarcity and uncertainty about others' intentions are enough: "we don't have to be monsters to be in deep trouble" (p. 51).

Rawls's key distinction is between the *rational*, which serves one's own good, and the *reasonable*, which is willingness to keep fair terms of cooperation (p. 54). Hobbes's laws of nature have a reasonable content: keep covenants, show gratitude, treat others as equals. But Hobbes justifies them only as rational, as long as others comply. "For Hobbes: the grounds of the Reasonable is the Rational" (p. 66). The sovereign does not change human nature. It changes the conditions under which people reason, so that compliance becomes rational. Rawls's example is a fair income tax that everyone would pay if they knew everyone else would (pp. 78–79). What Hobbes lacks is any room for "reasonable self-restraint" or "a sense of fairness"; he "comes close to saying: To each according to their (rational) threat advantage" (p. 87). That gap, Rawls says, is what leads on to Locke.

**Cohen** takes Hobbes apart more aggressively. A state of nature is simply a relation in which no one has authority over anyone else, so "a state of nature doesn't have to be like the Flintstones; it can be like the Simpsons, minus TV" (Cohen, p. 65). From Hobbes's "three principal causes of quarrel" (competition, diffidence and glory) he builds three distinct stories of how war arises (pp. 68–69):

- **Competition.** Egoists who do not care about others either way. The structure is a Prisoners' Dilemma.
- **Diffidence.** People who would cooperate if they could be sure others would. This is an "assurance game." "We don't all have to be rapacious for the diffidence story to work. We only have to be uncertain about others not being rapacious" (p. 76).
- **Glory.** Egoists who want to beat others for its own sake. This is the most colourful story but the least interesting, because it rests on the strongest assumptions.

Against Leo Strauss, who read Hobbes as a theorist of pride alone, Cohen points to the famous catalogue of what the state of nature lacks: "no place for Industry ... no Arts; no Letters; no Society" (pp. 81–82). These are not things wanted merely for prestige. Hobbes is "an implicit game theorist, but he did not realize that he was" (p. 85). Cohen also finds two Hobbeses side by side, one aristocratic (glory) and one bourgeois (competition and diffidence), from "an age of transition" (p. 87).

Cohen identifies four justifications of obedience in Hobbes: consequences, a hypothetical contract, actual (often tacit) consent, and authorization (pp. 87–90). His sharpest objection is that the state cannot be founded the way Hobbes says: "The very lack of trust among people which makes the state essential makes forming it impossible" (p. 95). He quotes Einstein's secretary, Otto Nathan, on world government: "World government will only be possible when it is no longer necessary" (p. 95). He also finds a straight contradiction. The subject must obey the law because he made it, through his representative. The sovereign is not bound by the law, also because he made it. "It cannot follow from 'X makes the law' both that X is subject to it and that X is not subject to it" (p. 102).

### 4. Locke's problem and his natural law

Rawls's first point about Locke is historical. The *Two Treatises* were written during the Exclusion Crisis of 1679–81, not after 1688 (Rawls, p. 106). They are a Whig tract defending resistance to Charles II under a mixed constitution, in which Crown and Parliament share the legislative power and neither is supreme (pp. 105, 122–123). Locke's argument is that by abusing his prerogative Charles had acted as an absolute king and so dissolved the government. Its powers then returned to the people, since government is "a fiduciary power" held on trust (p. 122). Rawls notes the danger Locke was in. He was involved with Shaftesbury in what may have been treason, and he fled into exile after the Rye House Plot. "It is quite remarkable that anyone could write such a reasonable work, one of such imperturbable good sense, while actively engaged, at great personal risk, in what may have been treason" (p. 108).

Locke's foundation is the law of nature: the law of God as known by natural reason (pp. 109–110). We are God's "Workmanship ... made to last during his, not one another's pleasure" (¶6, quoted p. 113). The basic law is that "Man [is] to be preserved, as much as possible." Rawls stresses two features. First, this law is "distributive, not aggregative": it protects each person, not the greatest number (p. 120). Second, our rights rest on prior duties to God. In Locke's own theological terms we are not "self-authenticating sources of valid claims" (p. 120). Rawls warns against modern "Lockean" views, such as Nozick's, that assert property rights without Locke's derivation of them: "for Locke and his contemporaries this religious background is fundamental" (p. 121).

The state of nature is a state of "equal jurisdiction," with everyone, in Locke's phrase, "Kings" over themselves (p. 115). Against Filmer, who held that men are born subject, Locke argues from God's silence (God has named no ruler) and from our equality as beings of one kind (p. 116). Rawls suggests that Locke is not really arguing here but "elaborating a certain conception of human society under God" (p. 116).

### 5. Legitimacy by "ideal history"

Rawls's reconstruction of Locke's test of legitimacy is his most original contribution:

> A political regime is legitimate if and only if it is such that it could have been contracted into during a rightly-conducted process of historical change, a process that began with the state of nature as a state of perfect freedom and equality. (Rawls, pp. 128–129)

He calls this process "ideal history." In it everyone acts rationally (advancing their legitimate interests) and reasonably (keeping their duties under natural law). No one uses force or fraud, and no one sells himself into slavery (p. 129). The test uses "could," not "would." It does not pick out the best regime. It excludes the ones that could never have been agreed to (pp. 130–131). Royal absolutism is excluded, because "no rational Creature can be supposed to change his condition with an intention to be worse" (¶131), and absolutism is worse than the state of nature (p. 130). A mixed constitution passes. The test is hypothetical but not, like Rawls's own original position, non-historical: a regime such as the Norman one, founded by conquest, can become legitimate as its form changes (p. 131). Rawls's verdict is that Locke "argues for what he needs and not more" (p. 131).

### 6. Consent and obligation

Legitimacy is a feature of a regime. Obligation binds a particular person. For Locke, fathers cannot bind their sons; each person must consent on coming of age, and does so singly, which is why the consent goes unnoticed (Rawls, pp. 132–133). Locke distinguishes two kinds:

- **Express consent**, given by "positive Engagement, and express Promise and Compact," which makes a person a perpetual member of the society.
- **Tacit consent**, given by owning or enjoying land under a government, which binds only while one stays (pp. 133–134).

Legitimacy comes first. One cannot be obliged to a regime that is "clearly unjust and violent," because then "War is made upon the Sufferers" (p. 134). Rawls notes that Locke never says how "the people" are to act when the government is dissolved. Their constituent power had no institutional form until constitutional conventions were invented, the first in Massachusetts in 1780, "an American invention" (pp. 136–137).

**Cohen** presses on tacit consent. Tacit consent should not bind less merely because it is tacit: if I say "unless you shake your head, I'll take it you agree," your stillness binds as much as words (Cohen, p. 117). The limit comes from what one is said to consent to, not from the manner. One basis, accepting inherited land, is sound: the land came under the state's protection on terms its heir cannot refuse. The other basis, merely travelling on the public highway, is "truly bizarre." Why can the traveller not say he renounces the state's protection (p. 118)? Cohen uses John Bennett's test. An action can count as a sign of consent only if it is of no interest for its own sake, or if the person setting the condition has a right to forbid it. Inheritance meets the test. Using the highway does not, unless we already assume the state has the right to govern us (pp. 118–119). Cohen also notes a curiosity: Hobbes relies on tacit consent more heavily than Locke, since for Hobbes it binds as fully as express consent (pp. 93–94).

### 7. Self-ownership and property

**Cohen** frames Locke through self-ownership: "every man has a property in his own person" (¶27). He calls this "a cardinal tenet of liberalism" in one sense of the word, and Locke "the grandfather of libertarianism" (Cohen, p. 103). Self-ownership raises two opposite problems. How can a self-owner come to own things outside himself, when God gave the earth in common? And how can a self-owner come under enforceable political obligations (pp. 104–105)? He rejects the attempt by James Tully and others to deny that Locke held self-ownership. The duty of charity in the *First Treatise* is not said to be enforceable. The duty not to attack others follows from their self-ownership, and does not limit one's own: "my right to use my fist as I please stops at the tip of your nose" (pp. 105–106).

He finds four arguments for private property in chapter 5 of the *Second Treatise* (pp. 107–109):

1. **Survival**: we must appropriate to live. This is a bad argument, since a right to eat does not require full ownership: "Monasteries are not (necessarily) death camps."
2. **Labour-mixing**: I own my labour, so I own what I mix it with.
3. **No harm**: appropriation that leaves "enough and as good" wrongs no one. This is inconsistent with the labour argument, because if labour itself confers ownership the proviso is irrelevant. But it is the most powerful argument, as in ¶33: no one is injured by another's drink "who had a whole River of the same Water left him." The major premise is strong; the minor premise is false "in all the cases that matter."
4. **Value**: labour creates nearly all value. This is distinct from labour-mixing. If you could add value by wishing, this argument would still give you title.

**Rawls** reads the same texts to show that Locke's property right is conditional. In the *First Treatise* God gives a "Liberty to use" his creatures, and "Charity gives every Man a Title to so much out of another's Plenty, as will keep him from extreme want" (¶42, quoted Rawls, p. 145). From this Rawls extracts three limits on ideal history: a principle of justice (each is owed the product of honest labour), a principle of charity (no one falls into extreme want), and a principle of reasonable opportunity (access to "the great common of the world") (p. 146). In political society property becomes conventional, so even "a liberal socialist regime" would not violate Locke's property rules (p. 150).

### 8. The class state

Locke accepts a franchise limited by property, the forty-shilling freehold, which excluded somewhere between three-fifths and four-fifths of adult men (Rawls, pp. 140–141). C. B. MacPherson concluded that Locke thought the propertyless incapable of reason, and so excluded them from the contract. Rawls replies that there is little textual support for this. MacPherson "overlooks a central point about all agreements": their terms depend on the parties' bargaining positions outside the agreement (p. 139).

Rawls then tells a "just-so story" drawn from Joshua Cohen (pp. 152–155). There are two groups, those with enough property to vote and those without, and four options: a class state, a democracy, a split into two states, or the state of nature. The propertied prefer the class state, then a split. The propertyless prefer democracy, then the class state. Since the propertied can force a split, the propertyless do best by joining the class state. All the conditions of ideal history are kept: the rule of law for all, opportunity to qualify for the vote, the product of one's labour, and relief from want. Locke's theory permits the class state; it does not require it.

The story clears Locke of MacPherson's charge but exposes "a disturbing feature." Because the parties know their positions, basic rights come to depend on historical contingencies. It even raises the question of whether the constitution "should not be reconsidered after each important shift in the distribution of political and economic power" (p. 155). This is why the contract must be revised, as Rousseau and Kant began to do and as Rawls's veil of ignorance does. Rawls ends without blaming the man: "It would be indecent to take a lofty critical tone towards him because his view is not as democratic as we now would like" (p. 140).

### 9. Hume's attack on the original contract

Hume's essay "Of the Original Contract" (1748) is the classic critique. Both lecturers summarise it in much the same way.

Hume grants each party something, in a way neither would want. The Tory may say kings rule by divine right, but no more than "a thief who takes my purse," since all power comes from God (Rawls, p. 165). The Whig may say government began in consent "in the woods and deserts," when men were nearly equal, but "this consent was long very imperfect, and could not be the basis of a regular administration" (Cohen, pp. 121–122). Cohen stresses that Hume is quite sure there was a historical contract at the start. What he denies is that legitimacy rests on consent now (p. 122).

His factual objections follow (Rawls, pp. 166–168):

- Almost no one believes the doctrine. Princes claim their subjects as property, and anyone who preached consent would be locked up by magistrates, if his friends had not already "shut you up as delirious."
- Nearly all governments began in conquest or usurpation.
- In 1688 a majority of some seven hundred men in Parliament decided for a nation of ten million.
- The free choice to leave is a fiction. A poor peasant who knows no foreign language is like a man carried aboard a ship asleep, who must jump overboard and drown if he wants to leave.
- Generations do not arrive all at once, like silkworms; "the new brood should conform themselves to the established constitution."

He ends with the claim that "new discoveries are not to be expected in these matters" (Rawls, p. 170).

**Cohen** answers these objections one by one. That people believe princes rightfully own them no more shows it is true than slaves' belief in slavery shows slavery legitimate: "Only a historian could think so" (Cohen, p. 123). Henry VII had good reason not to admit that his title rested on Parliament, since admitting it would concede a right of rebellion (p. 123). The argument from popular opinion also cuts against Hume's own theory, since people do not think utility is what binds them (p. 123). Hume seems to confuse the factual question of how governments are held together with the normative question of what makes them legitimate (p. 124). Cohen grants that one sound argument survives. Governments rarely rest on consent and could not; government is necessary; what benefits everyone is legitimate. So consent cannot be the only basis of legitimacy (p. 125).

On "new discoveries," Cohen is scathing. The premise is false, since Hume himself cites the *Crito*. It is also "repugnantly conservative ... just plain stupid" (p. 126). Hume had himself praised, as a new and "noble principle," the idea that every Englishman "had entire power to dispose over his own actions" (p. 126). His own moral philosophy was new too (p. 127). Cohen also catches Hume misreading Locke. Hume says a prince may forbid emigration, and that this disproves consent; but Locke held that only express consenters lose the right to leave (p. 128).

### 10. The "unnecessary shuffle": artificial duties and the circuit

Hume's philosophically serious argument rests on his distinction between natural and artificial duties. Natural duties, such as love of children or gratitude, run with our instincts. Artificial duties (justice, fidelity to promises, allegiance to government) are "artificial" in the old sense of being devised by reason for "the general interests and necessities of society" (Rawls, p. 169). Rawls gives an example of the old sense: Charles II praised Wren's new St Paul's as "awful and artificial," meaning awe-inspiring and rational (p. 169).

All three artificial duties rest on the same ground: "Society cannot possibly be maintained without the authority of magistrates." So there is no point in grounding allegiance on a promise. If you ask why promises bind, the only answer is utility; and utility can ground allegiance directly. In Rawls's phrase, the contract is an "unnecessary shuffle" that hides the real ground (Rawls, pp. 169–170). Cohen's version: "if you think political obligation is founded on promising, then you don't know why promises themselves bind" (Cohen, p. 133).

Cohen explains Hume's account of artificial virtue with Hume's own images. Benevolence is like "a wall, built by many hands, which still rises by each stone that is heaped upon it." Justice is like "a vault, where each individual stone would, of itself, fall to the ground" (Cohen, p. 131). A single just act can do no good, or even harm, as when a bad man inherits a fortune. But the practice cannot be tailored to cut out such cases (p. 130). Both lecturers note that Hume misdescribes the problem justice solves. Cohen says Hume treats it as a matter of short-term against long-term interest, when it is really a collective action problem in which a minority could free-ride (pp. 130–131, 137). He makes the same complaint against Hobbes (p. 86).

### 11. The replies to Hume

The two lecturers reply on different grounds.

**Rawls** says Hume misses the structure of Locke's theory. Locke never says that ancestral consent binds us. ¶116 says no one "can by any Compact whatsoever, bind his children or posterity" (Rawls, p. 170). Hume also overlooks two of Locke's distinctions: joining against originating consent, and express against tacit consent. Above all, he misses that Locke's theory has two parts, a test of legitimacy and an account of obligation (pp. 170–171). Locke can also say we have a natural duty, not based on consent, to support a legitimate regime (pp. 171–172). The real question between them is therefore whether Locke's hypothetical test and Hume's test of utility pick out the same regimes. Would utility, strictly defined as the greatest net sum of advantages, ever permit royal absolutism? "It would depend on circumstances" (p. 175). "Hume never really discusses that issue" (p. 172). Hume simply denies Locke, as Locke had simply denied Filmer, and the two views never meet "at the most basic level" (pp. 175–176).

**Cohen** has four replies of his own:

1. Hume "never once addresses the argument on which Locke bases his insistence that consent is a necessary and sufficient condition" of legitimacy. That argument is self-ownership: if we own ourselves, government can have no rights over us that we do not give it (Cohen, pp. 121, 133).
2. Hume contradicts himself when he calls consent, where it exists, "surely the best and the most sacred" foundation. On the circuit argument, consent should add nothing (pp. 133–134).
3. The obligation to keep a promise does not come only from the good effects of the practice. A lie is wrong even when its effects are good, because "I mistreat you, because I break faith with you." "The error in pure consequentialism in general is to suppose that the consequences of an act are its only dimension of assessment" (p. 134). Without a social convention, I can still deliberately lead you to rely on my doing something, and that is enough to bind me: "Tertium datur" (pp. 135–136).
4. Consenting is not promising. I consent to your act and promise my own, and consent can be withdrawn (p. 136).

If promises are not grounded in utility, Hume can still argue that utility suffices for government, but his challenge "loses its interesting ironical turn": there is no "pointless circuit" left to expose (p. 135).

### 12. Hume's justice and the judicious spectator

Rawls adds two points about Hume's own position. Hume's "justice" is narrow. It covers three rules of property: stable possession, transfer by consent, and keeping promises. In effect these are "the basic norms of economic relations," for "an association of owners," a market and contract (Rawls, pp. 178–179). "Public utility is the sole origin of justice" (p. 180). Rawls notes that Hume does not allow for the possibility that the rules of property serve the powerful rather than the general interest. He reads this as a deliberate idealization in what is meant as a psychological account, not a normative one (pp. 183–184).

Second, Hume asks how we come to agree in moral judgments at all. His answer is that we take up a "common point of view," that of the "judicious spectator," from which only the "principle of humanity" (fellow-feeling where our own interests are not engaged) is at work. Approval then tracks how far an institution serves the general good (pp. 184–186). Rawls calls this "one of the most important and interesting ideas in moral philosophy. It appears in Hume for the first time" (pp. 186–187). The contrast with Locke is complete. Locke argues "like a constitutional lawyer" within a constitution laid down by God. Hume, who "hates religion," is a naturalist explaining why property exists: "For Hume, let bygones be bygones" (pp. 164, 187).

### 13. Rousseau's problem

Only Rawls lectures on Rousseau, and the treatment is his most sympathetic. He calls *On the Social Contract*, taken with the *Second Discourse* and *Emile*, perhaps the greatest work of political philosophy in French (Rawls, p. 191). He also warns that Rousseau's style can distract from "the intricacies of reasoning" (p. 192). De Maistre answered the book's famous first line with "Sheep were born carnivorous and everywhere eat grass" (p. 192n).

Rousseau's concern is wider than Hobbes's or Locke's: he is "a critic of culture and civilization" (p. 192). The *Second Discourse* tells how humans moved through stages from a solitary animal to the corrupt society of his day. Political authority was "in part a trick of the rich," a fraudulent compact that fixed inequality in place (pp. 202–203). The work ends where it began, with all equal again, but now "because they are nothing" under a master (p. 203). Rousseau's central thesis, that "man is naturally good and that it is through these institutions alone that men become bad" (p. 195), is aimed at two targets. One is the Augustinian doctrine that slavery and property are remedies for original sin. The other is Hobbes, whose human nature, Rousseau thinks, belongs only to the last and most corrupt stage (pp. 205–206). The thesis comes down to two claims: that institutions largely decide which human tendencies develop, and that at least one just, stable and happy scheme of institutions is possible (pp. 206–207).

The key to the whole system, Rawls argues, is *amour-propre*: the concern for one's standing in others' eyes. Most readers take it to be simply vanity. Rawls, following N. J. H. Dent and Frederick Neuhouser, holds that it has a natural form, a need for equal standing which is ready to grant the same standing to others. Only in unequal societies does it become a desire to dominate (pp. 198–199). His authority is Kant, who says self-love that compares "is originally a desire merely for equality," from which the "diabolical vices" grow (p. 199). "Kant is the best interpreter of Rousseau" (p. 200). Without this reading, *On the Social Contract* has no basis in human nature and becomes "utterly utopian." With it, the charge that Rousseau is "a dazzling though confused and inconsistent writer" falls: "Don't believe it" (p. 200).

### 14. The social compact and the general will

Rousseau's problem is to "find a form of association that defends and protects the person and goods of each associate with all the common force," in which "each one, uniting with all, nevertheless obeys only himself and remains as free as before" (SC 1.6.4, quoted Rawls, p. 219). The answer is "the total alienation of each associate, with all his rights, to the whole community" (p. 220). Since the conditions are the same for all, "no one has an interest in making them burdensome for the others." And "as each gives himself to all, he gives himself to no one" (pp. 220–221). We become wholly dependent on society as a whole, and so independent of every particular person (pp. 218, 221).

Rawls reads the general will through five questions (pp. 224–228). It is the will each citizen has as a member, not the will of some entity above the citizens. It wills the common good: the social conditions under which citizens' common interests can be met. Common interests make it possible; shared fundamental interests make those possible; and a normative conception of human nature fixes those. When voting on fundamental laws, citizens are to give their opinion on what serves the common good, not vote their private interests. Rousseau's remarks about the "pluses and minuses" that cancel out describe private biases cancelling one another (pp. 226–227). This "point of view" is, Rawls says, the origin of the idea of public reason (p. 231).

The general will is not utilitarian. Rousseau calls the maxim that one may sacrifice an innocent for the multitude "one of the most execrable that tyranny ever invented" (p. 230). It produces justice because each voter "appl[ies] that word 'each' to himself, and ... think[s] of himself as he votes for all"; it must "come from all to apply to all" (SC 2.4.5, quoted p. 232). Rawls calls this "a marvelous paragraph" (p. 232).

### 15. Forced to be free, the legislator, and equality

Three passages often cited against Rousseau get a defence.

**"Forced to be free."** Whoever refuses to obey the general will "shall be forced to be free" (SC 1.7.8). Rawls reads this as a free-rider case. His example is a citizen who voted for mandatory pollution-control devices but would rather not fit one. Fining him holds him to a law he endorsed from the point of view of his own general will (pp. 241–244). Rawls admits Rousseau "misspeaks in saying we remain as free as before. ... We are free in a better and far different way" (p. 244). Moral freedom is "obedience to the law one has prescribed to oneself" (p. 221), and it is possible only in society.

**The legislator.** Rousseau's founder, who persuades a people to accept laws by attributing his wisdom to the gods, seems to sit awkwardly in a democratic theory. Rawls treats him as "a fictional figure—a deus ex machina" for two questions the compact does not answer: how a just society could arise, and how it keeps itself stable (p. 241). The difficulty is circular: "the effect would have to become the cause ... men would have to be prior to laws what they ought to become by means of laws" (SC 2.7.9, quoted p. 239). Once running, the institutions produce the citizens they need.

**Equality.** Freedom needs equality, "because freedom cannot last without it" (SC 2.11.1, quoted p. 233). Equality does not mean equal wealth. It means that "no citizen should be so opulent that he can buy another, and none so poor that he is constrained to sell himself" (SC 2.11.2). Since "the force of things always tends to destroy equality ... the force of legislation should always tend to maintain it" (p. 233). Rawls calls this an ancestor of his own view that the basic structure of society is the primary subject of justice (p. 234). He lists the usual reasons for limiting inequality: relieving suffering, preventing domination, the harms of status, and keeping procedures fair. Rousseau's distinctive contribution is equality "at the highest level": citizens related as equals in how society itself is conceived (pp. 244–248).

### 16. The two lecturers compared

| | Hobbes | Locke | Hume | Rousseau |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem addressed (Rawls) | Civil war among religious sects | Resistance to the Crown under a mixed constitution | Explaining the origin and support of moral and political institutions | Diagnosing civilization's vices; designing a just, stable society |
| Use of the contract | Reasons to support any effective sovereign | Test of legitimacy ("ideal history") plus consent for obligation | Rejected: an "unnecessary shuffle" | Terms of association expressing the general will |
| Ground of obligation | Rational self-interest; authorization | Consent within a legitimate regime; natural duty | Utility ("necessities of society") | Laws we give ourselves as citizens |
| Interests normalized | Life, family, commodious living | Lives, liberties, estates | Not applicable | Free will, perfectibility, equal standing |
| Rawls's main criticism | No reasonableness or fairness | Rights hostage to bargaining power (class state) | Misses the legitimacy/obligation distinction | Largely defended |
| Cohen's main criticism | The state cannot be formed as described; authorization contradiction | Tacit consent incoherent (highway); proviso inconsistent with labour argument | Ignores self-ownership; is/ought confusion; promises not utility-based | Not treated |

The two readers agree more than their methods suggest. Both see Hume's essay as effective against consent as the source of obligation and ineffective against something deeper in Locke. They differ on what that deeper thing is. For Rawls it is the test of legitimacy by what free and equal people could have agreed to. That test survives Hume, and Rawls revises it, through Rousseau and Kant, into his own veil of ignorance. For Cohen it is self-ownership. Hume never confronts it, and it is the premise Cohen had long criticized in Nozick (Cohen, p. viii). Both lecturers notice that Hobbes and Hume describe as short-sightedness what is really a collective action problem. Rawls uses this to explain Hobbes's sovereign charitably; Cohen uses it to say that Hobbes and Hume did not understand their own insight.

The difference in tone is also instructive. Rawls's Locke is a brave and sensible man whose theory, rightly reconstructed, fits his purpose; the fault is only that "our problems are not his problems" (Rawls, p. 155). Cohen's Hume makes arguments that are "just plain stupid" (Cohen, p. 126). Yet Cohen's criticisms are usually precise, and several (the highway, the authorization contradiction, the inconsistency between the labour argument and the proviso) are points Rawls does not make.

### Conclusion

The contract tradition, as these lectures present it, is less a single doctrine than a family of devices put to different uses. Hobbes uses it to show why everyone should want a sovereign. Locke uses it to show which governments could be legitimate and so may be resisted when they turn absolute. Rousseau uses it to describe a society in which obeying the law is a form of freedom. Hume's attack lands squarely on the weakest use: the claim that each of us is bound because we consented. It does not touch the strongest use, a hypothetical test of what free and equal people could agree to. That is why Rawls could revive the tradition two centuries later.

Both lecturers leave a question open. If legitimacy turns on what people could have agreed to, much depends on how the agreement is set up: who the parties are, what they know, what they want. Locke's parties know their property, and the result can be a class state. Rawls's answer is to hide that knowledge behind a veil. Cohen's lectures, by insisting on self-ownership as Locke's real premise, suggest the deeper disagreement is about what people own before any agreement begins.
