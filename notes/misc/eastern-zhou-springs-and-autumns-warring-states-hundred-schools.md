The five centuries after 771 BCE were the most violent in early Chinese history and also the most creative. The **Zhou kings** lost their power, the **aristocratic order** of the Springs and Autumns broke down, and the **Warring States** turned into centralized war machines until Qin swallowed the rest in 221 BCE. In the middle of this crisis the **Hundred Schools** of thought argued about how to restore order, and their answers shaped China for two thousand years. Here's how it works:

### 1. Kings Without Power

When the Zhou court fled east to Luoyang after 771, the kings kept their title of **Son of Heaven** and their role as the only ones who could sacrifice to Heaven, but they lost their armies. Pines notes that a newly found bamboo text, the Tsinghua *Xinian*, says that for nine years (749–741) "Zhou was without a king", and that in 707 King Huan was defeated and wounded by the lord of Zheng, which ended royal military action for good (Oxford Handbook ch. 22).

Confucius later described the decline in a famous passage, Analects 16.2: when the Way prevails under Heaven, rites, music and punitive expeditions come from the Son of Heaven; when it is lost, they come from the lords, then from their ministers, and the power slips lower each time.

**The hegemons.** In place of the king, the strongest lord acted as **hegemon** (*ba*), leading the other states "in the name of" the king against outsiders. The first was **Duke Huan of Qi** (r. 685–643) with his minister **Guan Zhong**. Ebrey says Guan Zhong was credited with coinage and with state control of salt and iron (ch. 2), though Pines argues that his famous reforms are a Warring States invention (ch. 22). After Qi the system became two-sided: **Jin** in the north led one alliance, **Chu** in the south led the other, and the small states in between were trampled.

### 2. An Aristocratic Age in Crisis

The Springs and Autumns (770–453 in Pines's dating; Ebrey uses 722–481, the years of the Annals) were the heyday of the hereditary nobility. Pines calls it also one of China's "deepest systemic crises" (ch. 22).

- **Chivalry.** Early battles were fought by nobles in chariots by appointment, with rules of honour. Ebrey lists some: you did not attack a state in mourning, you waited for the enemy to line up, and you left a defeated ruler a successor to carry on his sacrifices (ch. 2). But she also notes the violence: severed ears were presented at temples and captives' blood smeared on drums.
- **Power slipped downward.** Just as the king lost power to the lords, the lords lost it to their ministerial lineages. In Lu, the "Three Huan" families drove out Lord Zhao in 517, and the state went seven years without a ruler. In Jin, six great lineages fought until three of them, Wei, Han and Zhao, split the state in 453. In Qi, the Chen (Tian) family ruled from 481 and took the throne in 386. Lord Xian of Wei complained that all the administration would be in the hands of the Ning lineage while he would control only the sacrifices (Pines, ch. 22).
- **Concubines and succession.** Ebrey points out that rulers' many wives and concubines caused endless succession disputes (ch. 2).
- **A shared culture.** Despite the wars, the nobles of the different states intermarried, spoke an "elegant language", quoted the *Odes* to each other, and swore **covenants** (*meng*) by smearing blood on their mouths and burying the text for the spirits (Sanft, ch. 28).

### 3. Confucius, the Annals and the Right to Expel a Ruler

Lu kept a court chronicle, the **Springs and Autumns** (*Chunqiu*), which gave the period its name. Pines calls it "the most boring and least inspiring" of the classics, about 17,000 characters of bare entries (ch. 23). But Mencius 6.9 says Confucius composed it when the world was in decline, so that "regicidal ministers and patricidal sons were overawed". Later commentators read every word as "subtle words" of praise and blame. The fuller narrative of the **Zuozhuan** became, in Pines's words, the "fountainhead of traditional Chinese historiography".

The Zuozhuan has a striking speech by **Master Kuang**, the blind music master of Jin, when the people of Wei expelled their lord in 559 (Xiang 14.6):

> "The ruler is the master of the deities, the hope of the people. But if he fatigues the people's lives, neglects the deities, and ignores the sacrifices, then 'the hundred clans' will lose their hope, and the altars of soil and grain will have no master. What use is [such a ruler]? What can one do but expel him?"

- **Significance:** Pines traces this to the Mandate of Heaven. A ruler exists for the people and the altars of soil and grain (*sheji*), and loyalty belongs to those altars more than to his person. This is a long way from Han Feizi's ruler, who is above every law (section 14).

### 4. The Warring States (453–221)

After Jin split, the period becomes a struggle among seven great states: Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan, Han, Zhao and Wei (Pines, ch. 25).

1. **Wei rose first.** Lord Wen of Wei (445–396) hired talented men of low rank, including Wu Qi and Li Kui. In 403 the Zhou king recognized the three heirs of Jin as lords.
2. **The lords became kings.** King Hui of Wei took the title of king in 344, and Wei and Qi recognized each other as kings in 334. Ebrey dates the general change to 335 (ch. 2). The Zhou king was now one king among many.
3. **Alliances.** Diplomats built "vertical" alliances against Qin and "horizontal" alliances with it. Su Qin is said to have been prime minister of six states at once.
4. **Qin's rise.** The Shiji says the other states had shunned Qin as "small and remote", treating it as Rong and Di. After Shang Yang's reforms (section 6) it took Sichuan in 316, Chu's capital in 278, and in 262–260 won the battle of **Changping** against Zhao, after which its general Bai Qi massacred more than 400,000 prisoners.
5. **The end of Zhou.** Qin annexed the last royal domain in 256. The *Lüshi chunqiu*, compiled in Qin, says "There is no turmoil greater than the absence of the Son of Heaven". Between 230 and 221 Qin conquered Han, Zhao, Wei, Yan, Chu and Qi, and its king took a new title, *huangdi*, "August Thearch".

Pines argues that because the multistate system failed so badly, **unification** of All-under-Heaven became the central goal of Chinese political culture afterwards (ch. 22).

### 5. The New Warfare

War changed from an aristocratic contest to the mobilization of whole populations (Galvany, ch. 29; Ebrey ch. 2):
- **Iron.** China cast iron almost from the start, unlike Europe, which made mass production possible. But Lam shows that iron went mostly into farm tools; bronze weapons stayed common, and there is no good evidence that iron explains Qin's victory (ch. 26).
- **Infantry, crossbows and cavalry.** Mass infantry came from the south; the crossbow was invented in Chu; cavalry was learned from the northern nomads. King Wuling of Zhao made his soldiers wear "northern tribesmen's clothes" in 307 so they could ride.
- **Scale.** Ebrey quotes a third-century general saying that "200,000 troops were needed" for a campaign. A Zhanguo ce passage says one campaign cost "the equivalent of ten annual harvests" (Galvany).
- **Total war.** Defeat now meant the end of the state. Sunzi opens: "War is the most important affair of the State, the terrain of life and death, the way that leads to survival or extermination."

Galvany argues that "instrumental rationality" replaced ritual. Planning in the ancestral temple turned from divination into arithmetic, and the noble warrior gave way to the professional **commander** who did not fight himself.

### 6. Shang Yang and the Centralized State

The most thorough reforms were those of **Shang Yang** (Gongsun Yang, d. 338) in Qin under Lord Xiao (Pines, ch. 27; Ebrey ch. 2):

1. **Ranks for heads.** The hereditary aristocracy was replaced by twenty ranks given for merit, measured by the number of enemy heads cut off in battle. Ranks could be inherited only once and at a lower grade, so the system created social mobility.
2. **Counties and registers.** New land was divided into counties run by appointed officials, who kept household registers. A Qin household strip from Liye even lists the householder's rank and his "group of five" leader (Sanft, ch. 28).
3. **Groups of five.** Families were grouped in fives and made responsible for reporting each other's crimes. People needed permits to travel.
4. **Farming and war.** Peasants could buy and sell land and were freed from the local nobles, but in exchange the Qin state controlled them directly through taxes, labour service and conscription.

The Qin archives found at Shuihudi and Liye show how deep this went. Officials measured the girth of oxen each season and were punished if they got thinner; they counted rat holes in granaries. The *Book of Lord Shang* explains that soldiers are only brave when "there is nowhere to flee from the army ranks, and migrants can find no refuge" (Pines, ch. 27).

- **Significance:** Pines, following Mark Lewis, calls the result a "state organized for war". It won, but the same system could not adjust to peace, which helps explain why the Qin empire collapsed so soon.

### 7. The Shi and the Wandering Persuaders

The **shi** were the lowest rank of the aristocracy, "knights". As states were destroyed, their nobles became shi and looked for work elsewhere. Ebrey says men had no compunction about leaving their home state to serve a rival, and the saying was that they served "Qin in the morning and Chu in the evening" (ch. 2; Pines ch. 25).

Meyer warns against taking the "rise of the shi" at face value. Most of the Masters' texts were written by shi, so their praise of merit over birth is partly self-interested (ch. 30). The seven ruling houses themselves were never seriously challenged.

The cities grew too. Su Qin's description of the Qi capital, **Linzi**, with its 70,000 households, says that on its roads "carts rub hubcaps and men rub shoulders", and everyone plays the flute or zither, fights cocks or kicks a ball (Meyer, ch. 30). Sanft estimates Linzi's population at about half a million, the largest city of its time.

### 8. The Hundred Schools and the Axial Age

Ebrey explains why this crisis produced so much thought. Rulers fighting to survive welcomed men with ideas. Those men argued with each other, and once their teachings were written down, a text "tended both to freeze a viewpoint among followers and to elicit refutations from other masters". The traditional phrase, the time when "a hundred schools of thought contended", captures what she calls the most important legacy of the late Zhou (ch. 2).

Benjamin Schwartz places this in Karl Jaspers's **Axial Age**: in the same centuries came the Hebrew prophets, the Upanishads and the Buddha, and the Greek philosophers. He defines the common feature as transcendence, "the standing back, the looking beyond, the negation of the actual", seen in "Confucius turning inward to the source of jen" and the Laozi's "yearning for the reality which is nameless" ("The Age of Transcendence").

Schwartz adds two points:
- In China, unlike Israel or Greece, the breakthrough happened **inside** an established culture, not among outsiders.
- The great material progress of the Zhou did "not dissuade Confucius or Mencius from the view that they live in an age of profound spiritual and moral decay". Transcendence and material progress pulled in different directions.

### 9. Confucius (551–479)

Confucius came from the lower aristocracy of **Lu**, the state founded for the Duke of Zhou. He belonged to the *ru*, the learned men who advised on rituals, the calendar and precedents. He wandered from state to state looking for a ruler who would take his advice, failed, and became instead a great teacher (Ebrey ch. 2). His sayings were collected by his disciples in the **Analects**.

His key ideas:
1. **Ritual (*li*).** Confucius believed that in the early Zhou everyone had accepted their place through ritual, so a better society was possible. When a disciple wanted to stop sacrificing a sheep at the new moon, he said: "Si, you love the lamb. I love the rite" (3.17).
2. **Filial piety.** Respect for parents was both ritual and feeling. Society was the family writ large (Ebrey). When the governor of She boasted of a man who turned in his father for stealing a sheep, Confucius replied: "The father will shelter the son and the son will shelter the father. Righteousness lies precisely in this" (13.18).
3. **Humaneness (*ren*).** His highest virtue. Asked about it, he said, "Do not do unto others what you would not have them do unto you" (12.2). Ebrey notes that *ren*, unlike filial piety, extends to everyone without hierarchy.
4. **The gentleman (*junzi*).** The word had meant a man of noble birth. Confucius redefined it as a man of moral character: "The gentleman understands integrity; the petty person knows about profit." This opened the way for the shi.
5. **Virtue over punishment.** "Lead the people by means of government policies and regulate them through punishments, and they will be evasive and have no sense of shame. Lead them by means of virtue and regulate them through rituals and they will have a sense of shame and moreover have standards" (2.3).

Schwartz notes that Confucian rules are about roles (father, son, ruler, minister), not abstract categories like stealing or killing "(as in the Ten Commandments)". But a role does not guarantee its norm: "The same role may be enacted well or badly", and that depends on the person ("Social Role and Sociologism").

### 10. Mozi: Care for Everyone

**Mozi** (c. 470–390), only slightly later than Confucius, agreed that morality mattered but attacked Confucian priorities (Ebrey ch. 2; Leung ch. 32; Defoort ch. 33):
- **Three tests.** A doctrine should be judged by the deeds of the sage kings, by what people actually see and hear, and by its effects when put into practice.
- **Inclusive care (*jian ai*).** We should care for others as for our own. "If one loves another as much as he loves himself, then how can anyone in the world be unfilial?"
- **Heaven as the standard.** Mozi said he held Heaven's will like a wheelwright holding a compass or a carpenter holding a square. He wanted everyone to agree with their superiors up to the ruler, who follows Heaven: "What the superior considers wrong, all shall consider wrong."
- **Against waste.** He opposed aggressive war (mass murder for land), court music, and the Confucian three-year mourning, which he said impoverished the people and weakened defence. He wanted the able promoted even if they were peasants or craftsmen.

Mencius answered him fiercely (3B9): Yang Zhu is "for oneself", which means having no lord; Mo "cares inclusively", which means having no father, and "To be without lord or father is something for birds and beasts." Mohism vanished around the turn of the era. Defoort shows how it came back in the nineteenth century, when Sun Yat-sen and Liang Qichao compared Mozi's *jian ai* to Jesus's love.

### 11. Mencius: Human Nature Is Good

**Mencius** (c. 370–300), who had studied with Confucius's grandson, travelled to courts like Confucius did (Ebrey ch. 2; Roberts ch. 31):
- **Against profit.** When King Hui of Liang asked how to profit his state, Mencius replied: "Why must Your Majesty use the word 'profit'? All I am concerned with are the good and the right." If the king seeks profit, his officials and people will too, and the state will be in danger.
- **The people and Heaven.** He told rulers to their faces that if they ruled badly, Heaven would make the people overthrow them. Roberts quotes him: "Heaven sees according as my people see; Heaven hears according as my people hear."
- **Human nature.** "Anyone today who suddenly sees a baby about to fall into a well feels alarmed and concerned", without thinking of reward. Everyone has the four **sprouts** of goodness. Against Gaozi, who said nature is like whirling water that flows wherever a channel is cut, Mencius said: "Goodness is to human nature like flowing downward is to water."
- **Ox Mountain.** A mountain stripped by grazing and woodcutting looks barren by nature, but it isn't. Neither are people who act badly.

### 12. Xunzi: Human Nature Must Be Shaped

**Xunzi** (c. 310–220) had more practical experience in government and wrote his own essays (Ebrey ch. 2):
- **Heaven does not intervene.** "Why does it rain after a prayer for rain? In my opinion, for no reason. It is the same as raining when you had not prayed." Yet he kept the rituals, because they give feelings an orderly outlet and keep distinctions of rank.
- **Human nature is bad**, meaning wayward. We naturally want to eat when hungry and rest when tired. When a son yields to his father, his action goes "against their natures and run[s] counter to their feelings", and that is exactly what ritual and teaching are for.
- **Music.** "Music is joy, an emotion which human beings cannot help but feel at times", so the ruler should use calm and dignified music to shape people.

His student Han Feizi became the greatest Legalist.

### 13. Laozi and Zhuangzi: The Way of Nature

The **Daoists** rejected the activism of both Confucians and Mohists. They wanted rulers to leave people alone, and they put human beings inside a larger natural order, the **Dao**, rather than at its centre (Ebrey ch. 2).

**Laozi.** A copy of the *Laozi* (*Daodejing*) found at Guodian in 1993 shows it was in circulation by 320 BCE. It prefers the yielding to the assertive: "Water benefits all creatures but does not compete." The sage ruler "empties people's minds and fills their bellies", and order comes from non-action. Roberts reads it as demoting both paternal authority and Heaven below the Dao (ch. 31).

**Zhuangzi** (369–286) cared less about government and more about freedom. When the king of Chu offered him office, he asked whether a sacred tortoise would rather be dead and venerated or alive dragging its tail in the mud, and chose the mud. When his wife died, he first wept, then sang, because her life and death were like the four seasons. His wheelwright tells a duke that books contain only the dregs of the ancients, since real skill cannot be put into words. For more, see [[Zhuangzi: Perspectives, the Butterfly Dream and the Useless Tree]].

Ebrey sums up the contrast: for Confucians the Dao was the right way for humankind; for the Daoists it was the way of nature, beyond full human understanding.

### 14. The Legalists: Lord Shang and Han Feizi

Ebrey says the **Legalists**' "starting point was not what society should be but what it is" (ch. 2). Strong government depends on institutions, not on the virtue of the ruler.

- **Lord Shang** scorned tradition: "wise people create laws while ignorant ones are controlled by them; the worthy alter the rites while the unworthy are held fast by them." Law is the ruler's will, written down and applied to everyone except the ruler himself.
- **Han Feizi** (d. 233), Xunzi's student, wrote from the ruler's point of view. "When the ruler trusts someone, he falls under that person's control", and that includes wives and concubines scheming for their sons. The ruler should hide his intentions and control officials with **rewards and punishments**.
- **Against the family model.** Confucians compared the ruler to a parent. Han Feizi replied that even parents calculate: they celebrate a son and complain at a daughter. "A mother loves her son twice as much as a father does, but a father's orders are ten times more effective than a mother's." To ask rulers to govern by love "is expecting rulers to go further than parents."
- **The people as infants.** A baby howls when his head is shaved or his boil lanced, not seeing the benefit. In the same way the people call the ruler cruel and greedy when he makes them farm, fight and pay taxes for their own good.

Ebrey's key observation is that in Legalism there was no law above the ruler that could limit him, unlike the will of Heaven in Mohism or natural law in Greek thought (ch. 2). Schwartz adds a qualification: the harsh penal side of imperial law was not only Legalist, because "To Confucius no less than to the Legalist, the realm of litigation is the realm of brute force" ("Review of Law in Imperial China"). Compare [[The Modern Break: Machiavelli and Hobbes]].

### 15. Sunzi and the Art of Not Fighting

The *Art of War* attributed to **Sunzi** (Sun Wu) treats war as calculation and deception. Galvany quotes: "War is the art of deception. Hence, when able to attack, you must seem unable" (ch. 29). Because war costs so much, the best victory is one won without battle: "The good commander vanquishes the enemy forces without going into combat." Sunzi also warns: "The ruler should not deploy his troops out of anger", because "the state that has been destroyed cannot be restored and the dead cannot be brought back to life."

Galvany links Sunzi to the Laozi. Both praise water, formlessness and the "feminine" virtues of yielding, and Sunzi says the commander should be "as shy as a virgin" before striking.

### 16. Who Counted as Chinese

The breakdown of the Zhou made people think harder about who belonged together (Ebrey ch. 2; Pines ch. 22):
- **All-under-Heaven** (*tianxia*) and the **Central States** (*zhongguo*) meant the civilized world centred on the Son of Heaven, even when he had no power.
- The **Hua** or **Xia** were contrasted with the "barbarians": Yi, Man, Di and Rong. A Rong lord in 599 said: "Our Rong drink, our food, our clothes are all different from those of the Hua … Our languages are mutually incomprehensible."
- But the boundary was open. Asked how he could live among barbarians, Confucius answered: "If a gentleman lived among them, what uncouthness would there be?" Pines shows that Rong and Di were often allies, and that the image of Qin and Chu as "barbarian" is largely a Warring States product.

**Chu** is the best example. It absorbed fifty or more small states and pioneered counties and infantry armies (Ebrey). Its religion was rich in spirits and soul journeys: the *Songs of Chu* (*Chuci*) include poems summoning the soul (*hun*) home, and Chu developed the new supreme deity **Taiyi**, "Great Unity", later worshipped by Han Wudi (Major and Childs-Johnson, ch. 34).

### Conclusion

The Eastern Zhou began with a Son of Heaven who had lost his army and ended with a king of Qin who called himself August Thearch. In between, the aristocratic order collapsed from the top down, warfare became total, and Qin built a centralized state that registered every household and ranked men by the heads they took. The Hundred Schools were answers to that crisis. Confucius looked back to ritual and forward to moral character; Mozi to impartial care and Heaven's will; Mencius to the goodness of human nature and the people's verdict; Xunzi to education and ritual against a wayward nature; the Daoists to the Way of nature and non-action; and the Legalists to law, rewards and punishments with the ruler above them all. Qin won with Legalism, but the Han would rule with Legalist institutions and Confucian ideals together.
