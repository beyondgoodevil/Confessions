---
title: The debate between Maximus the Confessor and Pyrrhus
tags: [theology, christology, church-history]
summary: Maximus's defence of two distinct natures and wills in Christ against Pyrrhus.
---

Maximus the Confessor opposed Pyrrhus, who was a proponent of Monophysitism, by defending the distinction between the two natures of Christ—divine and human—while maintaining their union in the one Person of Christ. Pyrrhus had argued for a "composite" nature, where the two natures of Christ were so thoroughly united that they lost their distinction, effectively blurring the line between the divine and human.

Maximus, however, insisted that the two natures of Christ, while inseparably united in the one Person of the Word, retain their distinct properties. He emphasized that Christ's human and divine natures were not mixed, absorbed, or confused in any way, but existed in a perfect union without loss of identity. He argued that the humanity of Christ must be fully human, possessing its own will, emotions, and experiences, while the divinity remained fully divine, and the union of the two was without confusion.

Maximus also rejected Pyrrhus' idea of the human nature of Christ being absorbed or "deified" by the divine nature, which would undermine the authenticity of Christ's human nature. For Maximus, the integrity of both natures—human and divine—must be preserved in their full reality. He maintained that both the divine and human wills in Christ operated without contradiction, fully cooperating in the Person of Christ without separation or confusion.

Maximus' view upheld the Chalcedonian definition, which affirmed that Christ is fully God and fully man, with both natures remaining distinct yet united in one Person. This understanding was crucial to preserve the true humanity of Christ and the salvific role of His incarnation.
