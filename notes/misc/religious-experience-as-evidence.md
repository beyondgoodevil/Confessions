The argument from religious experience: people experience God, and experience is normally good grounds for belief. The question is whether experiences of God count the way experiences of tables do.

## 1. What Counts

- Experiences that **seem to the person to be of** a religiously significant reality: God, Brahman, emptiness, the holy.
- Not the same as:
    - **religious feelings**: elation you later put down to God. It has to seem to be *of* something.
    - **mysticism**: a practice deliberately undertaken. Many experiences come unsought (Paul on the road, Moses at the bush).

Four kinds:

1. **Visions and voices**: internal but claimed to be real.
2. **"Seeing as" realisation**: the yogi seeing identity with Brahman, the Buddhist seeing things as they are.
3. **Ordinary perception with more in it**: God in nature, the burning bush, a miracle seen by many.
4. **Ineffable direct awareness**: can't be put into sense terms at all.

## 2. Can Religious Language Even Say Anything?

- **Logical positivism** (Ayer): unverifiable statements are meaningless, so "I experienced God" says nothing. Reply: the verification principle isn't verifiable either. It refutes itself. See [[Critique of Empiricism]].
- **Non-cognitivism** (Braithwaite): religious claims express commitment, not facts.
- **Wittgensteinian** (D. Z. Phillips): religious talk is its own language game with its own rules.
- Objection to the last two: believers *do* make factual claims. "Christ died for my sins" entails "Christ died."

## 3. The Argument

1. Religious experiences are relevantly like sense experiences.
2. Sense experiences are good grounds for beliefs about the world.
3. So religious experiences are good grounds for religious beliefs.

Three defences:

**Swinburne's principle of credulity**

- If it seems to you that something is present, you're justified in believing it is, unless there's a specific defeater.
- That's how all perception works. So the burden is on the sceptic to show defeaters (almost) always apply to religious experience.

**Alston, *Perceiving God* (1991)**

- A **doxastic practice** = a socially established way of forming beliefs, with its own checks and its own defeaters (sense perception, memory, reasoning...).
- None of them can be shown reliable without circularity: you can only check perception with more perception. See [[The Fallacy of Circular Reasoning]], [[Criterion Problem]].
- So it's rational to engage in an established practice unless it produces massive, unresolvable contradictions.
- **Christian mystical practice** is one: it has a tradition, checks (Scripture, the Church, spiritual directors), and fruits.

**Plantinga**

- Belief in God can be **properly basic**: rational without argument, like belief in other minds.
- Produced by a *sensus divinitatis* (Calvin's phrase). If God exists and made us this way, those beliefs are warranted.
- See [[Natural Theology: Is It Legitimate]] sec. 4.

**Testimony**

- William James: authoritative for the person who has it, not for others.
- But we rely on testimony for most of what we know. Why not here? Left open.

## 4. Objections

| Objection | Point | Reply |
|---|---|---|
| **Diversity** | traditions report contradictory things: a personal God, an impersonal Absolute, nothingness | conflicting eyewitnesses don't show nothing happened; and a practice can be reliable on its core even with errors at the edges (Alston). Hick: one Reality experienced differently. (Then at least some are wrong about it) |
| **No public checks** | you can't verify it by others looking | rational intuition (logic, maths) can't be checked that way either, and we trust it. And there *are* checks inside a tradition |
| **Naturalistic explanation** | Freud (wish), Marx (economics), neuroscience (seizures, drugs, brain states) | every experience, including seeing a tree, has a neural correlate. Finding the brain activity doesn't show nothing was perceived. And these explanations usually assume what they set out to show |
| **Hiddenness** | if God shows Himself, why to so few? | see below |
| **Counter-induction** (Axtell) | holding your own tradition true when the same sources produce rivals is irrational | Alston/Plantinga: being inside a tradition gives you resources outsiders lack, like someone who has travelled |

## 5. Non-Perceptual Models

- **Schleiermacher**: religion is the "feeling of absolute dependence".
- **Rudolf Otto**: the numinous: *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*.
- Criticism: experiences claim to be *of* something, not just feelings. Reducing them to feeling gives the game away.

## 6. The Orthodox Side

*Mine.*

- **The saints as data.** The Orthodox case isn't "some people have had feelings". It's a long, consistent, *tested* tradition: the Desert Fathers, Symeon the New Theologian, Palamas, Seraphim of Sarov, Paisios. Same light, same stages, same warnings, across fifteen centuries and many languages. That's closer to Alston's "established practice" than to scattered reports.
- **Built-in checks.** The tradition is *more* suspicious of experience than its critics:
    - don't seek visions; distrust them if they come
    - test everything with a spiritual father
    - *prelest* (spiritual delusion) is a named danger
    - the fruits matter more than the experience: humility, love, repentance
    - See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]] sec. 7.
- **Purification as the condition.** Here's an answer to the diversity and hiddenness objections that usually isn't raised. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matt 5:8). On the Orthodox view, the faculty of seeing God (the *nous*) is darkened in most people and has to be healed. Then it's not surprising that most don't see, or see confusedly. Like asking why only trained eyes can read an X-ray. See [[Nous]], [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- **Not private.** "Mystical" for Dionysius meant *hidden*, not *subjective*. Experience is checked against the Church's common faith. See [[St. Dionysius the Areopagite]].
- **Experience and argument.** [[Mysticism Does Not Contradict Evidentialism or Evidence]] already argues that mystical knowledge doesn't conflict with evidence. Alston's point is the philosophical form of it: experiential belief is innocent until shown guilty.
- **Limits.** Experience can't by itself settle *which* God. It needs the tradition's teaching to interpret it. That cuts both ways: it's why the Orthodox claim rests on the experience *of the Church*, not of individuals.
