> "Hear then the parable of the sower." (Matthew 13:18)

Is a parable of Jesus an **allegory**, in which each detail stands for something else, or a **metaphor**, a single picture that makes one point or opens one vision? The church read the parables allegorically for eighteen centuries. Augustine's Good Samaritan is the best-known case: the man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho leaves the heavenly city for mortality, because "Jericho means the moon and signifies our mortality" (*Quaestiones Evangeliorum* 2.19, cited in Hultgren, p. 95). In 1886–99 Adolf **Jülicher** declared that the parables are not allegories at all. Most twentieth-century scholarship followed him and treated every allegorical feature as a later addition. This note reads two large commentaries that both reject Jülicher's verdict, for opposite reasons. **Bernard Brandon Scott**'s *Hear Then the Parable* (1989) is a literary reading from the circle of the SBL Parables Seminar and the Jesus Seminar. **Arland Hultgren**'s *The Parables of Jesus* (2000) is an exegetical and theological commentary written "for the sake of interpreting the parables of Jesus within the Christian church" (p. 17). Both books go through the parables one by one, and both are given equal attention here.

### 1. Two Commentaries and One Question

The two books are built very differently, and the structure is already an argument.
- **Scott** retitles the parables by their opening words ("A Man Had Two Sons" instead of "The Prodigal Son"), because traditional titles "embed summaries of inherited meanings" (p. 4). He groups them by the social world they draw on rather than by theme: family, village, city and beyond (chs. 1–7); masters and servants (chs. 8–13); home and farm (chs. 14–21) (pp. 72–74). His preface warns that "to undertake an exposition of parables runs counter to the very nature of parable", because a parable's form "subverts the effort at control" (p. xi).
- **Hultgren** groups thirty-eight parables under theological headings: the revelation of God, exemplary behaviour, wisdom, life before God, final judgment, allegorical parables and the kingdom (p. 5). He adds that "the categories should not be used in a rigid way" (p. 5). Each entry gives a translation, textual notes, verse-by-verse commentary, an exposition for preachers and a bibliography.

Their definitions show the difference at once.
- Scott: "a parable is a mashal that employs a short narrative fiction to reference a symbol" (p. 8). He chooses the neutral verb *reference* "deliberately", because it leaves open "whether a parable is metaphor or allegory. Given the corpus of Jesus and rabbinic parables, this issue is impossible to predetermine. Nor is the answer univocal" (p. 8).
- Hultgren: "A parable is a figure of speech in which a comparison is made between God's kingdom, actions, or expectations and something in this world, real or imagined" (p. 3). There are narrative parables and similitudes ("is like"), and the four Lukan "example stories" are only a subset of the narrative parables (pp. 3–4).

Scott defines the parable by what it *does* (it references a symbol). Hultgren defines it by what it *compares* (God and the world). The allegory question looks different from each starting point.

### 2. Where the Question Comes From: Jülicher's Single Point

Both authors begin with Jülicher, and both say he won the argument against the old allegorizing.
- Scott calls Jülicher's arguments "the rock that breaks those who would counterattack in favor of allegory" (p. 43). Jülicher's strategy was the **single point**: a parable has a picture (*Bild*), a subject (*Sache*) and one point of comparison (*tertium comparationis*). An allegory, by contrast, is a set of metaphors, each detail a separate one (p. 43).
- Hultgren quotes Jülicher directly: "the parable is there only to illuminate ... one point" (1:317), and the interpreter "must draw from the parable only one thought" (1:74) (p. 13). His verdict: "Since the work of Jülicher, one cannot go back to the allegorical method of interpretation" (p. 13). He gives a 1928 Lutheran parish paper as an example of what was given up. In it the man who goes abroad in the Parable of the Talents "is Jesus Christ", and "the talents are the means of grace, the word and sacraments" (p. 13).

Both then refuse Jülicher's next step, the claim that Jesus could not have spoken allegorically.
- Scott separates Jülicher's *strategy* (one point) from his *reason* (the Synoptic allegories are manifestly later). He keeps the conclusion and drops the strategy: "even if we reject Julicher's strategy of the 'single point,' his conclusion of the secondary nature of the Synoptic allegories stands" (p. 45). The one-point method "destroys the parable", whereas "allegory's openness to the multiple points of contact is more in line with polyvalency" (p. 45).
- Hultgren keeps Jülicher's rejection of *allegorizing* and drops his rejection of *allegory*. Following Raymond Brown, he says that "parable and allegory were not sharply differentiated in the world of Jesus and his contemporaries; the term mashal covers both" (p. 13). The view that Jesus told no allegorical parables "needs to be challenged" (p. 14).

So the two books agree that Jülicher was wrong about the genre, and they part company over what should replace his rule.

### 3. Scott: Mashal, Symbol and the Direction of Transference

Scott's answer rests on four steps.
- **The mashal is connotative.** The Hebrew *mashal* covers the proverb, the riddle, the taunt, the oracle and Ezekiel's allegory of the eagle. It is "inherently connotative language ... In connotative language something hidden always needs interpretation" (p. 11). For this reason even Mark's harsh theory that parables hide their meaning from outsiders "is not as outrageously outside the bounds of mashal" as critics supposed: "That parables are simple, clearly understandable stories is not supported by the evidence of mashal" (p. 24).
- **Allegory and metaphor lie on a continuum.** "The parable as genre is neither by necessity allegorical nor by necessity metaphorical ... The genre parable can be allegorical, metaphorical, or mixed" (p. 44). The Romantic tradition made metaphor and symbol noble and allegory base; Paul de Man called that preference "an act of ontological bad faith" (p. 46). "Allegory and metaphor are not juxtaposed but are part of the same continuum" (p. 47).
- **The real issue is direction.** "The real issue, however, is the direction of transference. Does the transference go from parable to referent or the other way? ... is the parable a true illustration or is it dictated by what it illustrates? ... is parable an ornament or does it have cognitive value?" (p. 47). Following Ricoeur, Scott holds that "parables demand that the primary direction of transference be from parable to referent" (p. 48). The traditional readings of the Good Samaritan (Luke's neighbourliness, Augustine's salvation of the soul, Jeremias's boundless love) all "bypass the literal level", so that the meaning flows from the doctrine to the story (p. 48).
- **The fault is a later ideology.** "The problem with allegory in the parables is not allegory per se, but the ideological reading of the parables with an ideology that is manifestly later" (p. 44). The Wicked Tenants, read as salvation history, requires that the son be Jesus: "If the son is Jesus, no one in the contemporary audience would have recognized it" (p. 44).

The test case is the **Leaven**. In the ancient world leaven stood for corruption ("A little leaven leavens the whole lump", 1 Cor 5:6; Gal 5:9), and unleavened bread stood for the holy (pp. 324–25). Interpreters have made the leaven a good thing because the kingdom must be good. In doing so the parable "is stripped of its cognitive value and becomes a mere ornament ... It is altogether illegitimate to deny the literal level because it conflicts with the supposed second level of reference" (p. 49). Scott does allow conventional metaphors. "Fathers, sons, slaves, vineyards, fig trees, leaven" play roles already fixed in Israel's repertoire, and "dogmatic assertions about the allegorical status of characters, images, or themes in parables should be avoided" (p. 49).

### 4. Scott: Handles on the Symbol of the Kingdom

The second half of Scott's prolegomena explains what a parable references. The rabbis' parables reference the Torah. Song of Songs Rabbah 1.1.8 says Solomon put "handles" on the Torah, like a basket with handles or a deep well with a rope (pp. 52–53). These rabbinic parables "are not simple allegories, as Julicher would have it. They are complex metaphors, hermeneutical efforts to affix handles" (p. 54). Jesus' parables reference the **kingdom of God**, and the kingdom is a **symbol**, not a concept.
- Drawing on Umberto Eco, Scott calls a symbol a "content nebula": "Any symbol is an enigma" (p. 58). The opposite of a symbol is a code, and so "allegory is overcoding of a symbolic text to establish authority over the text so that its content will not be nebula but known" (p. 58).
- "Precisely the kingdom's symbolic character demands that the transference be from parable (discourse) to kingdom (symbol)" (p. 61). In Jesus' parables the relation is often **diaphoric** rather than epiphoric. It does not extend the usual associations of the kingdom but "changes or challenges" them. "To situate 'kingdom' in a discourse that begins with a woman taking leaven and hiding it violates the primary associations of male power and moral goodness" (p. 61).
- "Solomon put handles on the Torah. In the Jesus tradition, parables are handles on the symbol of the kingdom of God ... By means of parable one penetrates to the mystery of the kingdom - but only in parable" (pp. 61–62).

Scott also borrows a warning from Ricoeur about the Gospels themselves. Putting the parable into a Gospel "is both a part of its meaning for us who have received the text from the church, and the beginning of its misunderstanding", so the parables must be read "both with the help of and against the distortions provided by the ultimate context" (p. 55).

### 5. Scott: Antimyth and the Minor Key

Scott's readings put this theory into practice, and they explain why he distrusts allegory. Parables "are examples of concrete thought, not abstract thought". Turning them into "parables of grace, advent, and so forth" is a translation "into our way of thinking" (p. 37). They take up the mythical elements of their culture and block them: "The Jesus parables are not myth; they are antimyth ... Yet ... myth frequently manages to reclaim the parable" (p. 39). The voice behind them plays "in minor keys" (p. 66), and "the common wisdom of interpretation has often removed the parable's fangs" (p. 67). In his reconstructions allegory is usually where myth reclaims the parable.
- **Mustard Seed.** A mustard plant is a shrub, and the cedar of Ezekiel 17 is the expected image of a kingdom. The tradition grew the shrub into a tree "because the shrub is an inappropriate metaphor for the final state of the kingdom" (p. 377). "Many have preferred the mustard tree, this unnatural malformity of mythical botany, to the recognition that God's mighty works are among the unclean and insignificant" (p. 387).
- **Great Banquet.** Isaiah's messianic banquet is the mytheme. In Jesus' version "the dinner never escalates to the expected messianic banquet, because the master is powerless to attack those who have snubbed him ... the householder cannot raise the poor up but must himself join them". Matthew's king, his burned city and his wedding garment restore the mytheme (pp. 162–63, 172–74).
- **Wicked Tenants.** Thomas 65, found in 1945, matches almost word for word the original form Dodd had reconstructed without it (pp. 30, 238). The psalm about the rejected stone "turns a story of defeat into one of triumph!", and so allows "the allegorization within the story to take place" (p. 248). Without it, the story ends "with a foolish master, devoid of vineyard, honor, and son" (p. 251). Every attempt to match the figures to God, Israel or Rome fails: "The parable frustrates not only allegory but also any effort to make sense of it!" (p. 252).
- **Good Samaritan.** Only in Luke is it an example story. Following Robert Funk, Scott holds that "the literalness of the Samaritan turns the story into a metaphor" (p. 29). A Jewish hearer cannot imitate the Samaritan; the only options are "to identify with the half-dead man and suffer the compassion of a Samaritan, or to reply that the story is false" (p. 199).

Scott sums up the pattern: the parables take up the structures of Mediterranean life "and most often play against them so that the patron is a fool, the Samaritan is savior, and the kingdom is like leaven" (p. 74). His epilogue states the cost of allegory plainly. Telling the kingdom in a narrative places the parable "at risk", because "expectations about the kingdom will overwhelm the parable, even silence it, in the twice-told tale of allegory ... The overcoded symbol of the kingdom of God is laid upon the parable" (p. 420).

### 6. Hultgren: Recognize Allegory, Do Not Allegorize

Hultgren's rule is set out in one paragraph of his introduction and applied throughout. "The interpreter should recognize allegorical elements wherever they exist and respect them. Moreover, more than one symbol can appear in a given parable. But in the final analysis the interpreter should not work under the assumption that there are hidden meanings within the text that can be brought out by allegorizing" (p. 14). He takes the distinction between **allegory** (a feature of the text) and **allegoresis** (a reader's method) from Hans-Josef Klauck (p. 14). He then sorts the material into three kinds.
- **Allegorical through and through:** the Wicked Tenants, the Wedding Feast and the Great Banquet (p. 14).
- **Allegorical elements within a parable:** the destroyed city of the Wedding Feast, the bridegroom of the Ten Maidens, the shepherd of the Final Judgment (p. 14).
- **Appended allegorical interpretations:** the Sower, the Weeds, the Dragnet. These "illustrate how fluid the dynamics were between parable and allegory in the first century" (p. 14).

The line between recognizing and allegorizing shows clearly in three verdicts.
- **Recognizing.** The landowner who hires the labourers "is surely a metaphor for God ... Jesus' parables typically speak of kings, fathers, and masters as the major figures, and in each case the hearer or reader makes the metaphorical connection. To do so is not to allegorize. To fail to do so, or to refuse to do so, is to tear the parables from their symbolic universe" (p. 36). In a footnote he observes that Scott "seems to dismiss the view that the landowner is a metaphor for God, but then ... seems to take it for granted" (p. 36 n. 13).
- **Allegorizing.** One reading makes the tower-builder Jesus the Builder of the Kingdom and the king going to war Jesus the Warrior against evil: "Such allegorizing is not convincing" (p. 144). For the Barren Fig Tree he declines both the three years as the three years of Jesus' ministry (a Johannine chronology) and the vinedresser as Jesus interceding with God. "The two figures within the story can easily represent the two sides of God - judgment and mercy" (p. 245).
- **Over-reading by demanding parallels.** Some object that the Prodigal Son's elder brother, faithful and heir to all, cannot stand for the Pharisees. Hultgren replies that the point of comparison is the refusal to rejoice: "To demand more parallels, and then to find fault with the correlations imagined, borders on allegorizing" (p. 85).

His method follows from his aim. The parables are read in their Gospel contexts, because "the church regards the canonical texts of Scripture as authoritative, not the Jesus of one's own reconstruction" (p. 17). Taken out of those frames they risk an "inexhaustible hermeneutic potential" (Kermode's phrase) of the kind the patristic allegorists enjoyed (p. 17). "While parables cannot mean just anything, they continue to provoke interpretations that, while similar, are certainly not all identical, nor should they be" (p. 19).

### 7. Hultgren: Metaphor, Hyperbole and the Portrait of God

For Hultgren the metaphors that count most are metaphors for God. Of his six distinctive features of Jesus' parables, the fourth is that they are "thoroughly theological" without being abstract. God appears as a father, a king, a shepherd, the owner of a vineyard, "or a woman who sweeps her house", and "the verbal images and the behavior of the metaphorical figures described are more powerful than propositional language about God could convey" (p. 10). The fifth is **surprise**: the main figures behave as no real person would, "because they represent God" (p. 42).
- **Unforgiving Slave.** Ten thousand talents is about 200,000 years' wages. There is "no reason to reduce the size of the debt owed. It is precisely its fantastic size that makes the parable so memorable" (p. 24). "No actual king would act in that way ... But God's actions surpass all human ways of acting by far" (p. 30).
- **Lost Coin.** "The parable is quite remarkable, for it portrays a woman as a metaphor for God. No other parable does so" (p. 64). God "cannot be portrayed simply in terms of the shepherd, a man of daring and energy, but must also be thought of in terms of a woman ... who seeks the lost coin with a fastidious, meticulous care that the tradition, androcentric by habit, is apt to forego" (p. 68).
- **Prodigal Son.** The father "extends unconditional love prior to repentance - indeed, even apart from repentance" (p. 86). Whether the son repents at all "is one of the intriguing and significant ambiguities of the parable" (p. 83 n. 59).

Where a figure cannot stand for God, Hultgren uses the rabbinic argument "from the lesser to the greater" (*qal wahomer*). The Unjust Judge is no portrait of God: "the parable rests not upon a portrait of God at that point but rather on the argument of qal wahomer" (p. 258). If even such a judge gives in to a persistent widow, God will surely answer.

### 8. Hultgren: The Allegorical Parables

Hultgren's seventh chapter is what most sets his book apart from Scott's. It opens with a thesis: "it is not possible to make a sharp distinction between parable and allegory in the world of Jesus, nor is it possible to assert that Jesus taught parables but not allegories and that any of the latter must be early Christian (post-Easter) constructions" (p. 331). Three parables "are so carefully embroidered with allegorical detail that they can be recognized instantly as allegories or, more precisely, allegorical parables". They "sketch out the history of salvation and the place of the ministry and significance of Jesus" (pp. 331–32).
- **Great Banquet (Luke).** "The dynamics of the parable to this point must carry allegorical meanings". The earlier invitation is the prophets; the slave's summons is the ministry of Jesus; the refusal is the refusal of his preaching; the host is God (p. 337). Hultgren is also cautious. The two final commissions may be Israel and then the Gentiles, but they more likely "simply point to the all-encompassing and thorough impulses of the divine mission" (p. 338).
- **Wedding Feast (Matthew).** This version "has been developed allegorically more than the other two" (p. 343). The burned city "allude[s] rather obviously and allegorically to the destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans in A.D. 70", and so cannot come from Jesus (p. 345). Hultgren still sets limits. The king's son need not be Jesus "if one does not look for allegorical features in every facet" (p. 343). The meaning of the two sendings of slaves "is not obvious", which "causes one to pause" (p. 345).
- **Wicked Tenants.** "In all three Synoptic versions the parable is rich in allegorical detail": vineyard, owner, slaves, tenants, son, punishment, others (pp. 357–60). Hultgren notes a "subtle shift" from Isaiah's song of the vineyard. In Isaiah, God's quarrel is with the vineyard; in the parable it is with the tenants. "God as owner does not plant a new vineyard (signifying Christian supersessionism). It is the tenants, the leadership of the people of God, that are the problem" (p. 359).

Then comes the claim that sets Hultgren against the whole tradition from Jülicher onward. The authenticity of the Tenants "in a reduced, less allegorized form has been accepted by a wide range of interpreters ... Here one should notice that the most allegorical of all the parables of Jesus is being considered, and various interpreters across the spectrum have concluded that it is authentic at its core" (p. 361). Reduced to its essentials and compared with Thomas, it has four parts: an owner (God), a vineyard (Israel), tenants (the Jerusalem leadership), slaves (the prophets), a son and new tenants. "Allegorical elements remain, but they fit as understandable referents within the ministry of Jesus prior to his crucifixion" (p. 361). The son "need be only an implicit Christology" (p. 362). As Dodd said, "it is the logic of the story, and not any theological motive, that has introduced this figure" (p. 362). Hultgren's closing chapter states the consequence: this observation "undercuts the old view that Jesus told parables; that allegories are from the early church; and that the two should not be confused" (p. 425).

### 9. Interpretations Inside the Gospels: The Sower and the Weeds

The two explicit allegorical interpretations in the Synoptics are the test case for both authors. Here they agree more than anywhere else.
- **Scott** calls the Sower the "test case for allegory" (p. 343). Its interpretation reads the story through Mark's own narrative: Peter "on the way" is the seed eaten by Satan, the rich man is the thorns, the rocky ground is the persecution of chapter 13 (pp. 345–47). The yields of thirty, sixty and a hundredfold are "an average-to-good harvest". The form of the story leads the hearer to expect a miracle the content never gives: "Jeremias, Dodd, Wilder, and others have been tricked by the form" (pp. 357–58). He excludes the Weeds among the Wheat altogether. Matthew and Thomas give the same structure, but it fits the church's need to decide whether to expel evil members: "Setting up boundaries is part of community building; attacking them appears to represent Jesus' position" (pp. 68–70).
- **Hultgren** agrees that the Sower's interpretation "is allegorical, providing a meaning for each of the items in the parable", and that it is "an early Christian composition" (pp. 190–91). In it "the comparisons are not equivalencies. They are exceedingly loose ... the use of metaphors is inconsistent" (p. 192). The parable looks at the sowing; the interpretation looks "from the receiving end". He adds that Matthew "may have been as frustrated as any modern interpreter in trying to align the seeds in Mark's interpretation with those in the parable", and so made each seed a type of person (pp. 193–94). On the Weeds, the interpretation is "an allegorical exposition, beginning with a word list" of seven equivalences, from sower = Son of man to reapers = angels (p. 297). It "knows nothing of the major point of the parable, that is, that there should be no premature judgment", and so comes from Matthew (p. 301). Unlike Scott, Hultgren traces a shorter form of the parable itself to Jesus, in a following that mixed good and bad, and set against the hope of Psalms of Solomon 17 that the Messiah would "drive out the sinners" (p. 300).

Hultgren then draws a moral for preachers that Scott would not: "Both patience and warning are canonical themes", and the interpreter must choose with discernment (p. 302).

### 10. Thomas as a Test Case

The Gospel of Thomas matters to the argument because it gives several parables without their allegorical interpretations. The two authors read that fact in opposite ways.
- **Scott** treats Thomas as independent of the Synoptics, judged by its order and by the absence of Synoptic redactional features. He holds that it is not necessarily gnostic, and that it lacks the allegories of the Sower, the Tares and the Vineyard (pp. 30–33). The Sower, the Tares and the Vineyard occur in Thomas without allegory, so allegory came into them independently, which supports his view that the Synoptic allegories are later (p. 44).
- **Hultgren** argues at length that Thomas in its present form dates from the middle of the second century at the earliest and depends on Matthew and Luke. Logion 5 in its Greek fragment agrees word for word with Luke 8:17 against Mark; another fragment shows features of both Matthew and Luke; logion 13 demotes Peter and Matthew below Thomas (pp. 433–37). Its parables "are totally free of appended allegorical interpretations ... They are not, however, devoid of allegorical elements. The Parable of the Wicked Tenants ... contains allegorical elements within itself comparable to its Synoptic parallels" (p. 448). Against the claim that Thomas lacks allegory, he notes that "there is certainly allegorizing in that version, too, in regard to the sheep that is the largest and most valued", which probably stands for the gnostic Christian (pp. 50–51 and n. 17). Gnostic reading turned the kingdom into "a state of being which is achieved when the individual is enlightened with true knowledge" (Montefiore, quoted p. 447).

On this point Hultgren turns the argument around. Thomas does not show parables before allegory. It shows a different allegory.

### 12. Where They Argue Directly

Hultgren wrote eleven years after Scott, and he names him often. The disagreements are about method, not just about details.
- **The ideological charge.** Hultgren quotes Scott's epilogue, that the parables "subvert ... the myths that sustain our world" (Scott, p. 424). He sets it beside William Herzog's reading of the parables as codes for oppression. He then applies Hayden White's complaint about criticism that consists in "the unmasking of the ideological understructure of the text" (p. 18). Scott had accused the allegorists of imposing a later ideology. Hultgren implies that the literary critics impose one too.
- **Overreading the literal level.** Scott asks how the finder can dig up his treasure without facing "the rather embarrassing question of whence it came" (Scott, p. 402). Hultgren grants that such questions "illustrate how cleverly the parables of Jesus have been crafted", but adds: "Not all questions that a modern interpreter might raise are relevant to the actual interpretation of a parable" (p. 415). Of other details he says more bluntly that "some questions are out of order in listening to a parable; by asking them, one ruins a good story" (p. 25).
- **Shamelessness in the Friend at Midnight.** Scott reads the *anaideia* of Luke 11:8 as the sleeper's wish to avoid shame. Hultgren notes that Scott translates the word "shame" on one page and "shamelessness" on another. "How could he possibly act 'shamelessly' ... if being a shameful person is precisely what he seeks to avoid in the eyes of his neighbors!" (p. 231 and n. 24).
- **The elder son.** Scott denies that the original parable identified the elder brother with the Pharisees, since nobody in the story is rejected. Hultgren answers: "Both the Pharisees and the son are implicitly criticized, not rejected" (p. 82 n. 55).
- **The seed growing secretly.** Scott makes the farmer who "knows not how" ignorant, even a sluggard. Hultgren: "The man's not knowing how the plants grow is a sign of wonder at a mystery, not of his ignorance" (p. 388 n. 14).

Some points of agreement matter just as much. Both reject Jülicher's ban on allegory. Both treat the Synoptic interpretations of the Sower and the Weeds as early Christian. Both read the explanation of Mark 4:11–12, that parables are told so that outsiders may not understand, against the background of the mashal as a riddle. Hultgren concludes, with Jeremias, that the passage "affords no criterion for the interpretation of the parables, nor any warrant for seeking to find in them by means of an allegorical interpretation some secret meaning hidden from the outsiders" (p. 461).

### 13. Assessment

| question | Scott | Hultgren |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Is a parable allegory or metaphor?** | Neither by necessity; both lie on one continuum, and the answer is not "univocal" (pp. 8, 44, 47) | Parable and allegory were not sharply distinguished in Jesus' world; metaphors for God are normal, and three parables are allegorical throughout (pp. 13–14, 331) |
| **What is wrong with the old allegorizing?** | It runs the transference backwards, from a later ideology to the story, and turns the parable into ornament (pp. 44, 47–49) | It hunts for hidden meanings the text does not contain (p. 14) |
| **Could Jesus speak allegorically?** | In principle yes, but the Synoptic allegories are secondary (p. 45) | Yes; the Tenants, "the most allegorical of all", may be authentic at its core (p. 361) |
| **What do parables reference?** | The kingdom as an open symbol, a "content nebula" (p. 58) | God, God's kingdom and God's expectations (p. 3) |
| **Strength** | A rigorous theory of how metaphor works; close attention to first-century social and religious codes; it lets the parables surprise | Balanced, comprehensive, careful with texts and history; honest about allegory where it is plainly present; useful to preachers |
| **Weakness** | The pattern of subversion can become its own overcoding, so that every parable must shock; some details are pressed further than the text bears (the leaven, the treasure); the exclusion of the Weeds and the Ten Virgins rests partly on an assumed profile of Jesus as a breaker of boundaries | The canonical frame and the theological headings can tame a parable before it is heard; the line between recognizing allegory and allegorizing is drawn case by case, not by a stated rule; the expositions sometimes slip into moralizing |

Taken together, the two books dissolve the old either-or. Scott shows that the issue was never allegory as such but the direction of meaning: whether the story shapes the idea or the idea dictates the story. Hultgren shows that parables in Jesus' world used conventional metaphors as a matter of course, and that the presence of allegory, even a great deal of it, does not by itself prove a parable late. Each is weakest where the other is strongest. Scott's reading of the Leaven depends on a literal level that Hultgren thinks he presses too hard. Hultgren's reading of the Banquet depends on a theological frame that Scott thinks closes the story off. The safest conclusion is the one both share: to recognize the conventional metaphors, to treat appended keys as early interpretations, and to let the story speak before deciding what it stands for.

### Conclusion

The question "allegory or metaphor?" turns out to be badly put. In the Hebrew *mashal* and in the rabbinic parables, the two were never separate kinds. Jesus' kings and fathers and vineyards carried conventional meanings his hearers knew. Some of his stories may well have been allegories from the start. What both Scott and Hultgren reject is a method, not a genre: the habit of deciding first what a parable must mean and then decoding it to fit. For Scott the test is whether the meaning flows from the story to the kingdom and not the other way. For Hultgren it is whether the reader recognizes the comparisons the text actually makes, without inventing secret ones. Scott ends with the words he took for his title: "Solomon made handles for the Torah and Jesus made handles for the kingdom. The challenge remains the same: 'Hear then the parable'" (pp. 424–25). Hultgren ends his last chapter with the claim that the evangelists, and Matthew and Luke above all, "could hardly have produced their Gospels without" the parables (p. 429).
