In 2005 Jung Chang, author of the twelve-million-selling *Wild Swans*, published *Mao: The Unknown Story* with her husband Jon Halliday. Its first sentence sets the tone: Mao "was responsible for well over 70 million deaths in peacetime, more than any other twentieth-century leader." The editors of the academic response summarise its portrait. Mao is "a liar, ignoramus, fool, philistine, vandal, lecher, glutton, hedonist, drug-peddler, ghoul, bully, thug, coward, posturer, manipulator, psychopath, sadist, torturer, despot, megalomaniac and the greatest mass murderer of the twentieth century—in short, a monster, equal to or worse than Hitler and Stalin" (Benton and Lin, p. 1).

Reviewers in the press called it "a triumph" and "an atom bomb". Nicholas Kristof wrote that it "methodically demolishes every pillar of Mao's claim to sympathy or legitimacy". Simon Sebag Montefiore called Mao "the greatest monster of them all" (p. 1). Most China specialists were scathing. This note sets out the case against the book, the case for it, and the harder question beneath both: how Mao should be judged.

### 1. The Book and Its Claims

The book's scoops, as Andrew Nathan lists them, include:

- Mao amassed a private fortune in the Jiangxi Soviet.
- His troops fought only one real battle on the Long March.
- Chiang Kai-shek deliberately let them escape.
- The famous battle at the Dadu (Luding) Bridge never took place.
- Mao poisoned his rival Wang Ming.
- He enjoyed the slow death of Liu Shaoqi.
- He launched the Great Leap in order to buy weapons.

Behind these lie a theory of character, based on the young Mao's notes on a German ethics textbook: "Absolute selfishness and irresponsibility lay at the heart of Mao's outlook". There is also a theory of power: Chiang was undone by three Communist moles among his generals (Nathan, pp. 21–23; Goodman, p. 92). The apparatus looks formidable: 363 named interviewees in 38 countries and about a thousand written sources. Chang said: "Nobody has put them together into this coherent picture of Mao. People looked but they didn't see" (Benton and Lin, p. 5).

### 2. The Charge Sheet: Method

The specialists' objections fall under a few heads.

- **Unverifiable sources.** Nathan: "many of their discoveries come from sources that cannot be checked, others are openly speculative or are based on circumstantial evidence, and some are untrue." The sources include anonymous interviewees ("a laundry worker", "Mao's girlfriends", "a Russian insider"), unpublished documents with no location given, and a garbled archival citation that cannot be traced (pp. 22–25). The anonymity in itself is common in China studies. What troubles Nathan is "the authors' failure to give readers any information to help them to evaluate their sources' reliability" (p. 24).
- **Selective and distorted use.** Davin: "Their methodology is always to choose the account least favourable to Mao" (p. 19). Willmott: sources supporting the thesis "are reproduced as fact, while those opposing it are ignored" (p. 177).
- **Ignoring the field.** Goodman notes that Lucian Pye's 1976 psychobiography, which already portrayed Mao as a damaged personality, is missing from the bibliography, as are the recent lives by Davin, Breslin and Short (p. 93). Halliday's own co-authored history of the Korean War is missing too, because it contradicts the new thesis (Willmott, p. 184).
- **Omniscient narration.** The authors know what Mao felt on the Long March and on his deathbed ("in [his mind] stirred just one thought: himself and his power"). Even the book's admirer Waldron asks how (Chan, p. 100; Waldron, p. 171).
- **Contradicting themselves.** *Wild Swans* described warlord China as chaotic and cruel and credited the Communists with real resistance to Japan and real popular support. *The Unknown Story* reverses all three. The authors' earlier biography of Song Qingling was close to hagiography; now she is "beyond doubt" a Soviet agent (Davin, pp. 18–19; Chan, p. 102).

Timothy Cheek's verdict is the sharpest. The book is "a Maoist denunciation of Mao himself, done in the florid style of the Cultural Revolution denunciations". It uses the three devices of Maoist mass criticism: "a black-and-white dichotomy", "plausible but erroneous lines of argument", and "cheap shots, slurs, sexual innuendo" (pp. 55, 58). Frederick Teiwes, who met the authors early in the project, recalled: "It quickly became clear that the party line was fixed, and would not change... any attempt at balance was simply not in the game plan" (Cheek, p. 57).

### 3. Test Cases

The reviews test specific claims against the sources:

- **The Dadu Bridge.** The authors rest the "complete invention" claim mainly on a 93-year-old local woman interviewed in 1997. Benton and Tsang list a mass of contrary evidence. The Red Army papers reported the fighting the next day. Nationalist dispatches record Chiang ordering the warlord Liu Wenhui to hold the river "on pain of a court martial". A Nationalist regiment at the bridge lost fifty men. The 2003 official history of the First Front Army records three of the 22-man vanguard "died as martyrs" (pp. 46–47). A journalist found another surviving eyewitness, Li Guixiu, then fifteen: "The fighting started in the evening. There were many killed on the Red Army side" (Goodman, pp. 94–95). Benton and Tsang concede that "Mao certainly exaggerated the battle". But "Chiang did not leave the passage open" (p. 47).
- **The Paulsen notes.** The authors quote Mao at 24: "Morality does not have to be defined in relation to others". Alfred Chan sets the full passage, in Stuart Schram's translation, beside it. "Self-interests are indeed primary for human beings, but it does not stop here. It is also of our nature to extend this to helping others... so working for the interests of others is in my own self-interest... if the situation is desperate I would rather die myself than let her die." The duty passage reads: "Helping those in need... sacrificing oneself to save others, are no more than duty, since I desire to do them, and only then will my mind be at rest" (Chan, pp. 103–5). Chen Yung-fa draws a subtler lesson from the same notes. "The danger in Mao's personal ethics lies not in the lack of morality but in its excessive morality", in a conviction that he must pursue his truth "even if thousands opposed him" (p. 116).
- **The litters.** The claim that Mao was carried through the Long March leaves out that he had malaria. The other leaders carried were also sick, wounded or pregnant: Wang Jiaxiang had an open abdominal wound, Deng Yingchao had tuberculosis, and He Zizhen was pregnant and hit by shrapnel (Chan, pp. 106–7).
- **The "Secret Superpower Programme".** The claim that 61 per cent of the First Five-Year Plan was military comes from adding defence (23.4 per cent) to *all* capital construction (37.6 per cent). Chan calls this "a gross distortion" (p. 100).
- **Changchun.** The authors blame the deaths in the 1948 siege on Mao's pitilessness. Their own source shows Chiang forbidding the civilians to leave, failing to supply the city by air, and requisitioning civilian grain: "both sides contributing to the tragedy" (Chan, p. 101).
- **The moles.** Benton and Tsang observe that by the same standard of proof the authors should have unmasked "the fourth and biggest mole—Chiang Kai-shek" (p. 54). Chen Yung-fa calls the mole thesis "basically conjectural" (p. 115).

Spence adds a case of sourcing: a supposedly bugged, sexually explicit phone call between Lin Biao's wife and the army chief of staff. It comes from an unsourced 1993 compilation and reads to him as "much closer to the bawdy Chinese popular storytelling tradition than to an actual bugged conversation" (pp. 37–38). Barmé locates the book in the Chinese tradition of *yeshi*, "wild histories" of court intrigue. He recalls Edmund Backhouse's forged diary of the Boxer year, which was hailed by the press in 1910 and exposed only in 1936 (pp. 77–78).

### 4. What the Critics Concede

Almost none of the critics defend Mao. Nathan's review begins: "Mao Zedong's long, wicked life". He says: "We already knew that Mao was selfish and ruthless" (pp. 21–22). Davin: "No honest person who has studied the Maoist record would wish to be cast as an apologist for him. His utopian dreams, his periodic refusal to engage with reality, his ruthlessness and his determination to win resulted in terrible human suffering" (p. 20). Benton and Tsang: "We do not disagree that Mao authored atrocities and suffering and that he bullied and murdered party members and others" (p. 54).

Some of the book's material is accepted as real, though already known: the Yan'an purges, the opium trade that financed the base area, Mao's private life, Soviet money. Chen Yung-fa thinks most of its details on the campaigns of the 1950s and 1960s "are believable. However, most are also generally known". The Chinese edition was retitled *Mao: The Little-Known Story* (p. 114).

Two reviewers see genuine advances.

**Lowell Dittmer** reviews the 1949–65 chapters. He finds the book "not a work of objective scholarship but a passionate polemic, consisting of the vacuum-cleaner assemblage of every bit of information conceivably damaging". Yet he thinks "the cumulative picture is convincing and in my view quite devastating". He finds two new contributions, both from the authors' interviews: Mao laid out his plan to "lure the snakes out of their holes" *before* the Hundred Flowers opened in 1957, and Liu Shaoqi "ambushed" Mao at the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference of 1962 by substituting his own speech. Dittmer calls it "the biography the Chairman deserves, having in his career as leader presided over the systematic character assassination of so many of his political opponents" (pp. 64–71).

**Arthur Waldron** dissents openly. He compares the book to Solzhenitsyn's *Gulag Archipelago*: "resting on a mass of evidence, overwhelmingly accurate and well-supported, it conveys its story in the voice not of the bloodless scholar but of the novelist and the moralist". He says it will "wreck Mao's reputation beyond salvage" (pp. 167–71). His target is Western complacency. "No American textbook of Chinese history classes Mao with Stalin, or with Hitler." John K. Fairbank could write in 1972 that "the Maoist revolution is on the whole the best thing that has happened to the Chinese people in centuries", and many specialists "actively protected themselves from this evidence" (pp. 167, 172). Yet even Waldron calls the "stress on conspiracy and personal politics to the exclusion of nearly everything else... perhaps the greatest weakness" (p. 171).

### 5. The Deeper Objection: Despot-Centred History

The most important criticism is not about footnotes. It is that one man's wickedness cannot explain a revolution.

- **Nathan:** "Of course, Mao deserves harsh moral judgement... But this portrayal impedes serious moral judgement. A caricature Mao is too easy a solution to the puzzle of modern China's history." It would have been "closer to the truth, had we been shown that there are some very bad institutions and some very bad situations, both of which can make bad people even worse" (p. 29).
- **Spence:** by making Mao vile from birth to death, the authors "deny any room for change", and "the Chinese people as a whole are denied all agency. And Mao himself ceases to be absorbing. How far can Mao have to fall, when he is at the bottom already?" Historians try to be fair even to monsters because otherwise "there is nothing we can learn from such a man" (p. 39).
- **Goodman:** "There is a major paradox at the heart of this book. Mao was power crazed, venal, incompetent... Yet nonetheless he was able to exercise absolute power and authority" (p. 96).
- **Barmé:** the authors "evacuate entirely ideas, ideology and non-personal motives from modern Chinese history" and so "cut that country off from the twentieth century". They treat the reader "to a snuff-fest of outrages" that "leaves us none the wiser as to what the hell it was all about" (pp. 80–81).
- **Chen Yung-fa:** to understand Mao one must understand how he made himself sole interpreter of "scientific truth (Marxism-Leninism) and patriotism (nationalism)". "We should criticize and deconstruct the Marxism-Leninism and the nationalism he expounded", which was "a double-edged sword. It can bring liberation but can also bring slaughter" (pp. 116–17).

Waldron, from the opposite camp, makes the same demand. Following Hillel Halkin on Arendt, he asks: "Where in late-Qing or Republican Chinese society would one find the roots of democide?" (p. 172).

### 6. The Defenders of the "Known Story"

At the other pole stands Mobo Gao, a Chinese "new left" scholar. For him the book is "an intellectual scandal" and "a politically motivated piece of show business". Against it he sets "the known story". The revolution raised average life expectancy "from 35 in 1949 to 63 by 1975", unified the country, carried out land reform, and improved women's status and literacy (Gao, p. 81).

Gao also brings documents on the famine. His most interesting evidence is Mao's letter of 29 April 1959, addressed to officials at six levels down to the production team. It warns against boasting about output and insists that "food is the number one priority". He adds Mao's corrections to the October 1960 emergency directive on the communes, which made it more lenient to peasants (pp. 82–84). Gao concedes: "Mao should certainly be held primarily responsible for the consequences of the Great Leap Forward." But "to identify Mao as the person responsible for a policy disaster is not the same as to say Mao was the murderer of so many people" (p. 86).

Gao is plainly an advocate. He calls the Great Leap "theoretically guided rationality" and argues for the "constructive" side of the Cultural Revolution (pp. 87, 96). He also claims that the village studies he knows report no famine deaths, which is not a serious test of national mortality. The editors of the critical volume share part of his outlook. They defend the revolution's "noble aims" and compare British India's famines (pp. 6–10). Jin Xiaoding ends by blaming Western journalists' "profound anti-Chinese prejudice" (p. 160). Reading the volume fairly means noticing that some critics have their own political commitments, as the authors do.

### 7. The Famine: Numbers and Intent

The Great Leap famine is the centre of any judgement on Mao, and here the more neutral historians matter most.

**Numbers.** Fairbank and Goldman: "In 1958–1960 some 20 to 30 million people lost their lives through malnutrition and famine because of the policies imposed upon them by the Chinese Communist Party... one of the greatest of human disasters". Later in the book the figure is "more than 30 million" (pp. 368, 410). Meisner reports that official mortality statistics released in the 1980s "indicat[e] 15,000,000 famine-related deaths", and that some scholars put the figure at "as many as 30,000,000". Davin gives "up to 30 million excess deaths" (p. 16). Chang and Halliday's 38 million is at the top of the range, and Waldron's "on the order of 50 million" is above it (p. 170). Gao rightly notes the method problems: there is no reliable census, deaths from hunger are hard to separate from deaths from hardship, and some estimates count children who were never born (pp. 85–86). He uses them to push the total down without offering a better figure.

**Mechanism.** Fairbank: inflated statistics "added up to an enormous increase in production, more than a doubling of output. The result was that government requisitions continued high even while production was actually dropping. This led to a first-class manmade famine" (p. 372). Meisner: "In 1959, as agricultural output was falling, state procurements of food grains were rising. Not until well into 1960 was the gravity of the situation fully recognized in Beijing."

**Responsibility.** Meisner's judgement is the most careful. "Mao Zedong, the main author of the Great Leap, obviously bears the greatest moral and historical responsibility for the human disaster... But this does not make Mao a mass murderer on the order of Hitler and Stalin... There is a vast moral difference between unintended and unforeseen consequences of political actions, however horrible those consequences might be, and deliberate and willful genocide." But Meisner does not let Mao off. Mao knew something was wrong. In February 1959 he said: "With the peasants, he [Stalin] drained the pond to catch the fish. Right now, we have the same illness." At Lushan he admitted that "the main responsibility was mine." Then he purged Peng Dehuai for saying so and revived the radical policies. "For Mao... personal political consideration ultimately proved more compelling than the welfare of the peasantry... Mao must bear the main responsibility for the enormous human tragedy that in large measure resulted from the renewal of the 'communist wind'" (Meisner).

That is the crux. Gao's evidence of Mao's caution in early 1959 is real. But it makes the decision at Lushan in July 1959 worse, not better. Mao knew enough to warn against exaggeration, and he still chose to destroy the man who said aloud what he himself had said in private.

### 8. The Hitler Comparison

The critics unanimously reject the comparison. The editors: "the Great Leap deaths were unintended: any equation of them with colonial and racist genocides would be preposterous", and the Leap "was designed to accelerate China's industrialization", not to exterminate a people (pp. 10–11). Barmé speaks of "competitive body counting", and of the "utterly distasteful" line in the book: "It was, it seems, a good day if the boss waived a few million deaths" (p. 81). Meisner draws the line between unintended consequences and genocide.

Waldron answers that only the Nazi horror is "regularly acknowledged and truly well known", while Lenin's, Stalin's and Mao's crimes remain "the province of specialists". He cites Mao in 1954: "We must kill... And we say it's good to kill" (pp. 165, 170). Both points can be true. The Leap was not Auschwitz. Yet the land reform, the campaigns against "counter-revolutionaries", the labour camps and the Cultural Revolution were deliberate killing and persecution. Fairbank and Goldman put the toll at one to two million landlords in land reform, and half a million killed and about 100 million persecuted in the Cultural Revolution (p. 410). They belong in the same moral register as other twentieth-century tyrannies, even if their logic was different.

### 9. The Other Ledger

Several reviewers insist that a judgement must weigh what the regime built. The figures are not in dispute:

- The crude death rate halved in eight years. Life expectancy roughly doubled, from about 35 to the mid-60s (Davin, p. 20; Meisner; Gao, p. 81).
- Industrial output grew at 11.3 per cent a year from 1952 to 1977, "as rapid a pace of industrialization as has ever been achieved by any country during a comparable period" (Meisner).
- Literacy and basic health care spread across the countryside.

Meisner applies E. H. Carr's warning about the Russian Revolution: "The danger is that we shall be tempted to forget altogether, and to pass over in silence, its immense achievements."

Meisner is just as clear about the other column:

- Agriculture grew only 2.3 per cent a year.
- Peasant incomes "increased little, if at all, after 1957".
- The state "exploited the people it ruled, especially the peasantry".
- Socialism's political conditions were never created: "The cult of Mao Zedong was one of history's most extreme examples of the alienation of social power into fetishized political authority."

His summary is a "deep incongruity between the Maoist regime's progressive socioeconomic accomplishments and its retrogressive political characteristics" (Meisner, "Legacies"). Dittmer adds that much of the progress came despite Mao rather than because of him. The real corruption of absolute power was "the power to realize fantastic charismatic visions and ignore negative feedback, with often tragically unanticipated consequences" (p. 71).

### 10. Assessment

**As history, *Mao: The Unknown Story* fails.** The case is made source by source and by specialists of every political colour: a Taiwanese anti-Communist historian (Chen Yung-fa), an Oxford specialist on the Nationalists (Tsang), liberal Americanists (Nathan, Spence), a New Zealand sinologist (Willmott). Its most sensational claims fail when checked against the evidence: Dadu, the moles, Chiang letting the Reds go, the superpower programme, the Paulsen reading. Many of its true claims were already known. Its method is the one Cheek identifies. It is a denunciation, which first decides that its subject has always been bad and then finds the evidence.

**But the critics do not exonerate Mao, and should not be read as doing so.** Every reviewer except Gao and Jin accepts that Mao bore primary responsibility for a famine of perhaps 15 to 30 million or more deaths. They accept that he chose to intensify the policy after being warned at Lushan, ran a system of terror, and in the Cultural Revolution persecuted his own colleagues to death. Meisner's distinction between unintended catastrophe and genocide is morally important, but it is a distinction between degrees of guilt, not between guilt and innocence.

**"Monster" is the wrong category, but not because Mao was benign.** It is wrong because it explains nothing. Nathan's point stands: bad institutions and bad situations make bad people worse. Chen Yung-fa's point too: Mao's power rested on a claim to embody scientific truth and national redemption, and that claim made millions willing to act on his visions. A purely personal monster cannot account for the enthusiasm of 1958, the loyalty of Zhou Enlai and Peng Dehuai, or the persistence of Mao's portrait over Tiananmen. A purely structural account, which Gao and the editors sometimes approach, cannot account for Lushan, where one man's choice cost millions of lives.

**The press's enthusiasm is itself a historical fact.** The book was received as authoritative, ahead of decades of specialist work that had long recorded the purges and the famine. Waldron has a point: Western opinion was slow to see Mao's crimes, as Fairbank's 1972 remark shows. But the remedy for a sentimental myth is not a demonological one. Spence's and Cheek's call to write better popular history is the right answer.

**Most persuasive.** On the man, Meisner and Chen Yung-fa: a revolutionary of real conviction and real achievement, whose certainty that he possessed the truth justified any means. On the famine, Meisner: primary responsibility, aggravated by the political choice at Lushan, without genocidal intent. On the book, Nathan: "Jade and plastic together, the pieces are arranged in a stark mosaic, which portrays a possible but not a plausible Mao" (p. 28).

### Conclusion

Was Mao a monster? On the evidence collected here, he was something more instructive and more troubling than that. He was a man of ideas and immense political skill. He led a revolution that unified China, roughly doubled life expectancy and began its industrial revolution. Through utopian haste, a cult of his own infallibility and a willingness to sacrifice others for his truth, he also presided over the worst famine of the century and two decades of persecution. Chang and Halliday's monster is a caricature, and the specialists showed how badly it was built. But the caricature exaggerates something real. The critics' own verdicts, from Nathan's "long, wicked life" to Meisner's "main responsibility for the enormous human tragedy", leave Mao among the century's great perpetrators. As an old man in Chengdu told Bill Willmott's brother: *Ren buhao. Sixiang hao.* "Man not good. Thoughts good" (p. 185). Whether the thoughts were good is exactly the question the book never asks.
