St. Gregory of Nyssa's writings against Eunomius, especially in his work *"Against Eunomius"*, focus on defending the orthodox Christian understanding of the nature of God, particularly the doctrine of the Trinity, against Eunomius' radical theological views. Eunomius, a leader of the Anomoean faction, taught that the essence (ousia) of the Father and the Son is completely different (heteroousios), thus denying the Son's full divinity and asserting that He was created. This view was in sharp contrast to the Nicene doctrine of *homoousios*, which affirms that the Father and the Son share the same essence.

St. Gregory of Nyssa countered Eunomius' views on nature (ousia) by emphasizing the following points:

1. **God's Nature is Incomprehensible**: Gregory argued that human reason cannot fully comprehend God's nature, especially when it comes to the eternal generation of the Son. He criticized Eunomius for attempting to define the divine essence in a way that limits God's transcendence and infinite nature. Gregory emphasized that the divine essence cannot be reduced to simple human categories or concepts, as human understanding is finite and cannot grasp the infinite mystery of God.
2. **The Son's Eternal Relationship with the Father**: Gregory affirmed that the Son is eternally begotten from the Father, and this relationship does not imply any subordination or difference in nature. He used the analogy of light and the sun—just as light is not separate from the sun, the Son is not separate from the Father. Both share the same divine essence.
3. **The Unity of the Trinity**: Gregory stressed that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are of one essence (homoousios) and share the same divine nature, though they are distinct in their personal properties. The nature (ousia) of God is not divided but is fully shared among the three persons. Gregory rejected Eunomius' understanding of the Father and Son as having different natures, affirming that the unity of the Godhead remains intact in the relationship between the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
4. **Theological Language and Analogies**: Gregory used theological language to show that we must be careful not to speak about God in ways that reduce His infinite majesty. He argued that while we speak of "Father" and "Son," these are relational terms that do not diminish the divine unity or nature. The Son is not created or inferior to the Father, as Eunomius claimed, but is fully divine and of the same essence as the Father.

In summary, St. Gregory of Nyssa's response to Eunomius was centered on defending the doctrine of the Trinity and the eternal, consubstantial nature of the Father and the Son. He argued that God's essence is beyond human understanding, and that attempts to reduce the Trinity to simple human logic or definitions were misguided. The Father and Son are of the same divine nature, and any attempt to separate them was a heretical misunderstanding of the Christian faith.
