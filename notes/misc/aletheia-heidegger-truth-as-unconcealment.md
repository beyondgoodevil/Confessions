The Greek word for truth is *alētheia*. Heidegger spent forty years arguing that if you hear it properly it says ***un-concealment***: *a-* ("not") plus *lēth-*, the root of *lanthanō* ("I am hidden") and of *Lēthē*, oblivion. Truth, for the early Greeks, was not first of all a correct sentence. It was things coming out of hiding, showing themselves, and that coming-out always fought against a hiddenness that never went away. Heidegger thought the story of how this experience faded (from Plato's cave, through the Latin *veritas*, to Descartes's certainty and Nietzsche's "justice") was the hidden history of the West. Late in life he admitted that part of the story was philologically wrong, but he didn't give up the thought. Here's how it works:

### 1. The Word and Its Directives

Everyone, Heidegger says, already "knows" what truth is: a statement corresponds with the facts. *The Essence of Truth* (1931–32) opens by showing how quickly this falls apart. If the statement matches what we know, and what we know is itself what matches the facts, then "truth is correspondence with a correspondence" (p. 3). And we call things true, not just sentences: "true gold," "a true friend." What do these correspond to?

So he goes back to the Greek word.

1. **It is privative.** *A-lētheia* is built like the German *Unschuld* ("innocence," literally "un-guilt"), where the negative word names the positive state. "For the Greeks, truth too is privative": the true is what has been "torn away from hiddenness" and "robbed" of it (pp. 7–8).
2. **It has nothing to do with sentences.** "Truth as unhiddenness and truth as correctness are quite different things; they arise from quite different fundamental experiences" (p. 8). What the Greeks called *alēthes* was "the beings themselves," not assertions (p. 9).
3. **But don't trust etymology.** Heidegger warns himself on the same page: "To what fruitless discussions and fateful errors have the daring artifices of etymology led!" The word matters only if it comes from a real experience of things hiding. His witness is Heraclitus, fragment 123, *phusis kruptesthai philei*: "beings in their being love to conceal" themselves; "it is their proper innermost drive to remain hidden" (pp. 9–10).

In the *Parmenides* lectures (1942–43) he turns this into four "directives" that the translation "unconcealedness" gives us (pp. 13–14, 17, 131ff.):

1. **Un-*concealedness*** points to concealment, which has many forms: veiling, masking and covering, but also "conserving, preserving, holding back, entrusting," like "a source that wells up only as long as it is already preserving" (p. 16).
2. ***Un*-concealedness** says truth involves a struggle: "'Truth' is never 'in itself,' available by itself, but instead must be gained by struggle. Unconcealedness is wrested from concealment" (p. 17).
3. **Opposition.** Truth stands in a whole field of opposites, of which falsity is only one.
4. **The open.** Unconcealedness presupposes an open space in which anything can appear (§9 below).

### 2. Lanthanō: Being Hidden in Homer

The strongest evidence Heidegger gives is not *alētheia* itself but the way Greek uses its root verb. *Lanthanō* doesn't mean "I hide something." It means "**I am hidden**," and Greek puts it in the main verb where we would put "unnoticed" in an adverb.

- **Odysseus weeping.** At the Phaeacian court the bard Demodocus sings of Troy, and Odysseus covers his head with his cloak. McCrorie translates: "He kept his crying away from all of the others / but not the king. Only Alkinoos marked him" (*Od.* 8.93–94, p. 104). Literally, Homer says Odysseus *elanthane*, "was concealed" as one shedding tears. For the Greeks, Heidegger says, the point isn't that the other guests failed to notice: "round about this man and his existence there lies a concealment" that cuts the others off from him (*Parmenides*, pp. 23, 27–28).
- **Athena and the spear.** When Achilles' first spear misses, "Pallas Athene / snatched it, and gave it back to Achilleus, / unseen by Hektor shepherd of the people" (*Il.* 22.276–77, Lattimore). In Greek, Athena "was concealed to Hector in her giving back" (p. 24).
- **Epicurus.** *Lathe biōsas*, "live unnoticed," is really "be concealed in the way you conduct your life" (p. 24).
- **The plague.** In Thucydides (2.49) some survivors of the Athenian plague were seized by *lēthē* of everything, "so that they did not know either themselves or their friends." Heidegger translates *lēthē tōn pantōn*: "the remaining hidden of all beings befell them." It is not a mental lapse but an event that "pertains to all beings: they fall into hiddenness" (*Essence of Truth*, p. 101).
- **Forgetting.** So *epilanthanomai*, "I forget," means: I am hidden from myself in relation to something, which then sinks away, and the sinking-away is hidden too. "A more uncanny way to think the essence of forgetting in one single word can hardly be imagined" (*Parmenides*, pp. 24–25).

The conclusion: "The 'concealed' and the 'unconcealed' are characters of the very being itself and not characteristics of the noticing or apprehending" (p. 24). Heidegger adds a modern warning: there may be "an invisible cloud of forgetting itself, the oblivion of Being," hanging over the earth, "a cloud [no] airplane could ever breach" (p. 28).

### 3. Pseudos: The Strange Counter-Word

If truth is *a-lētheia*, you would expect falsity to be called *lēthes*. It isn't. The Greek word is ***pseudos***, from a different root. Heidegger takes this oddity seriously.

- **The pseudonym.** A pseudonym is not a false name in the sense of a wrong label (calling chalk "a sponge"), nor an impostor's alias, nor a military cover name like "Operation Michael." When Kierkegaard signs *Fear and Trembling* "Johannes de Silentio," the name hides the author and at the same time reveals something about him: "a covering that at the same time unveils" (*Parmenides*, p. 30; *Essence of Truth*, p. 98). So *pseudos* is **dissembling**: hiding by showing.
- **The cupboard.** A cupboard placed in front of a door doesn't just cover the doorway; it can "pretend there is no door at all in the wall" (*Parmenides*, p. 32). This is an "objective" event, like a neighbour's house blocking the view of the mountains, not a lie in someone's head. False gold "looks like gold" and so "hides what it is in truth: non-gold" (p. 36).
- **Zeus's lightning.** Nestor recalls that when the Greeks sailed for Troy, Zeus flashed lightning on the right, a good omen that turned out to hide disaster (*Il.* 2.348ff.). A sign always shows and holds back at once: "a concealing that shows." When it hides what is coming, it is *pseudos* (pp. 31–32, 37).
- **Nereus.** Hesiod gives the decisive pairing. "Pontos sired truthful Nereus, his oldest son, / who tells no lies," and who is "never forgetful / of right" (*Theogony* 233–36, Athanassakis). The Greek is *apseudea kai alēthea*. Heidegger reads the *kai* as "because": Nereus does not dissemble *because* he does not conceal. Non-falsity is grounded in unconcealment (*Parmenides*, pp. 33, 38).
- **The Muses.** The same poem shows how close the two are. The Muses tell Hesiod: "we know how to tell many lies that pass for truth, / and we know, when we wish, to tell the truth itself" (*Theogony* 27–28). Their *pseudea* look like the real thing; their *alēthea* are what they may choose to let out.

There is also a twist. The Greeks themselves often defined the true as the "not-false," *a-pseudes* (a Nereid in *Iliad* 18.46 is even called Apseudes). Once that happens, truth starts to be thought from falsity: the undistorted, the straight, and finally the **correct** (*Essence of Truth*, pp. 97–100; *Parmenides*, p. 22).

### 4. Being and Time §44

The first public version is in *Being and Time* (1927). Heidegger takes the three traditional theses (truth lives in judgment; truth is agreement; Aristotle said so) and asks what "agreement" could mean between a mental act and a thing.

- **The picture on the wall.** Someone with his back to the wall says "the picture on the wall is hanging askew," then turns round and sees it. What gets confirmed? Not that a "representation" in his head matches the picture. "Asserting is a way of Being towards the Thing itself that is." What is demonstrated is that the entity "shows itself just as it is in itself." So: "To say that an assertion 'is true' signifies that it uncovers the entity as it is in itself" (H. 217–218).
- **Dasein is "in the truth."** Uncovering is something we do in being-in-the-world, and it rests on Dasein's **disclosedness**, its being the "there" where anything can show up. That is "the most primordial phenomenon of truth" (H. 220–221).
- **And in untruth.** Because we are "falling" into the chatter of the "they," things "show themselves, but in the mode of semblance." So "'Dasein is in the truth' states equiprimordially that 'Dasein is in untruth'" (H. 222).
- **Robbery.** "Truth (uncoveredness) is something that must always first be wrested from entities. Entities get snatched out of their hiddenness. The factical uncoveredness of anything is always, as it were, a kind of robbery. Is it accidental that when the Greeks express themselves as to the essence of truth, they use a privative expression—*a-lētheia*?" Parmenides' goddess shows him two paths, "one of uncovering, one of hiding" (H. 222).
- **Newton.** "'There is' truth only in so far as Dasein is." "Before Newton's laws were discovered, they were not 'true'; it does not follow that they were false." Once uncovered, things "show themselves precisely as entities which beforehand already were" (H. 226–227). This isn't subjectivism: uncovering is exactly what takes truth "out of the province of Dasein's discretion" (H. 227).

He also guards himself: "we must avoid uninhibited word-mysticism," though "the ultimate business of philosophy is to preserve the force of the most elemental words" (H. 220).

### 5. The Cave: Truth Begins to Slide

In *The Essence of Truth* Heidegger reads Plato's cave (*Republic* 514a–517a) as a story about *alētheia*, since the word comes up at every stage.

1. **The prisoners** see shadows and call them *to alēthes*. Even chained, "being human means... to comport oneself to the unhidden" (p. 20). But they have no idea of the fire, of shadows as shadows, or of hidden and unhidden.
2. **The first release** fails. The freed prisoner, dazzled, thinks the shadows were *alēthestera*, "more unhidden." So unhiddenness has degrees, and Plato adds that whoever turns to "more beingful" things "sees more correctly" (*orthoteron*, 515d). Here Heidegger finds the hinge: "**Truth as correctness is grounded in truth as unhiddenness**" (pp. 25–26).
3. **The ascent** is violent and slow: shadows, reflections, things, the night sky, then the sun. The sun is the Good; the light is the *idea*, the "look" of a thing through which we see what it is. "Being, the idea, is what lets-through: the light" (p. 42). Light also means freedom: a forest **clearing** is where there are no trees, so "lighting up therefore means making-free" (pp. 43–44). And "Poetry makes beings more beingful" (p. 47).
4. **The return** ends in death: the liberator who goes back down is mocked and killed, a clear reminder of Socrates. Today, Heidegger adds, the philosopher would be poisoned more quietly, by being made "interesting" (pp. 61–62). This stage teaches the most: the freed man can only show the shadows *as* shadows because he knows that the unhidden is always won from a concealment. "Concealment belongs essentially to unhiddenness, like the valley belongs to the mountain" and so "**untruth belongs to the essence of truth**" (pp. 65–66).

But Plato, Heidegger argues, is already losing this. Unhiddenness in the cave is "a theme, and at the same time not a theme" (p. 91): Plato asks about the unhidden things and the idea, never about hiddenness. In the Theaetetus the decisive question becomes what makes a *false view* possible. Plato's answer (someone in the distance who looks like both Theaetetus and Socrates is "mis-taken" for Socrates) is brilliant, and Heidegger works it out with the image of a "fork": present perception on one prong, what we hold in mind on the other. But Plato then calls the mistake an archer's miss (194a), a wrong predicate. So falsity becomes "the un-correctness of the *logos*, of the proposition," and truth "also must have its seat in the *logos*" (p. 226). "So much being, so much seeming" (p. 229): the deeper connection was there and went unthought.

### 6. Phusis, Seeming, and the Collapse

*Introduction to Metaphysics* (1935) ties *alētheia* to the Greek word for being, ***phusis***, "the emergent self-upraising" (p. 64).

- **Being means appearing.** The roots *phu-* and *pha-* (*phainesthai*, "to appear") "name the same thing." "Being essentially unfolds as appearing." Hence: "Truth, as un-concealment, is not an addendum to Being. Truth belongs to the essence of Being" (pp. 106–107).
- **Three kinds of *Schein*.** German *Schein* means (1) shine, as in sunshine or a saint's halo; (2) appearing ("the long-awaited book has now appeared"); (3) mere semblance, as with false saints (*Scheinheilige*) or the moon looking "two feet wide." The second is the ground of the other two (pp. 104–105). Because things appear, they can also merely *seem*: "Wherever there is unconcealment of beings, there is the possibility of seeming" (p. 110). And seeming "is not nothing": the sunrise is not refuted by astronomy (p. 110).
- **Oedipus.** *Oedipus the King* is "a unique struggle between seeming (concealment and distortion) and unconcealment (Being)." He begins as the savior of the city "in the brilliance of glory" and ends unconcealed as parricide, then puts out his eyes, "placing himself outside all light." Heidegger quotes Hölderlin: "King Oedipus has perhaps one eye too many" (p. 112).
- **Three paths.** Parmenides' poem lays out the way to Being, "the pathway of grounded trust, for it follows unconcealment"; the way to Nothing; and the way of mere opinion. "The way to Being is unavoidable. The way to Nothing is inaccessible. The way to seeming is always accessible and traveled, but it can be avoided" (pp. 117–120).
- **The oracle.** Heraclitus fragment 93: "The lord whose soothsaying happens at Delphi neither gathers [*legei*] nor conceals [*kruptei*], but gives indications." *Legein* stands opposite concealing, so *logos* first meant "de-concealing" (p. 181).

Then the **collapse**. In Plato, the *idea*, the look a thing offers, is a genuine consequence of *phusis*, but it gets "raised to the level of essence itself." From then on: "The truth of *phusis*—*alētheia*...—now becomes *homoiōsis* and *mimēsis*: resemblance... the correctness of seeing" (pp. 194, 197). Logos as gathering becomes logos as statement, and the statement becomes "the locus of truth in the sense of correctness" (p. 199). "Unconcealment, the space founded for the appearing of beings, collapsed. 'Idea' and 'assertion'... were rescued as remnants" (p. 203). He insists this wasn't simple failure: "The inception, as incipient, must, in a certain way, leave itself behind" (p. 204).

### 7. The Roman Bastion

The *Parmenides* lectures add a second, Latin stage. Heidegger thinks translating Greek into Latin was "the genuine event of history" (p. 42), because the words carried a different experience of being.

1. **Falsum.** The Grimm dictionary calls *falsch* "an un-German word"; it comes from *fallo*, related to "fall" and Greek *sphallō*, "make totter." Rome's world is the *imperium*, command, and its typical act is not open war but *fallere*: bringing the other down from behind and keeping him standing beneath you. *Pseudos*, which hid by showing, becomes *falsum*, what trips you up (pp. 39–45).
2. **Verum.** *Ver-* (compare German *Wehr*, a dam or gate) first meant closing off and protecting. It came to mean staying upright, not falling, being on top: *verum* is *rectum*, the right, the just. "Veritas is then rectitudo, 'correctness'" (pp. 47–48). Church doctrine (orthodox and heretical) carried this on.
3. **Certitude and justice.** *Adaequatio intellectus ad rem*; *res aletheias ad rationem venit* ("aletheia has come to reason"); Luther's question of how one can be certain of salvation; Descartes's "correct use" of reason; Kant's critique of its correct and incorrect use. "The verum becomes the certum." At the end, Nietzsche: truth as "justice," the will to power fixing a power relation (pp. 50–53).

"The circle of the history of the essence of truth... is now closed. Yet *alētheia* remains outside this circle." It isn't just buried under rubble: "there has been built on it an enormous bastion" of *veritas*, and *alētheia* is walled into it "as one of the building stones" (p. 53). Even the polis, he says, we still see "with Roman eyes" (p. 43).

### 8. The Kinds of Concealment

If falsity isn't the only opposite of truth, what are the others? Heidegger lists several (pp. 58–64):

| Kind of concealment | Greek or example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Dissembling | *pseudos*: the cupboard, the pseudonym | hides by showing something else |
| Deception | *apatē*, "off the path" | leads you down a by-way |
| Sheltering | *kruptō*, *kalyptō*: Troy hides treasure, the earth covers the dead (*Od.* 3.16) | keeps safe |
| Night, earth, death | night "veils"; Hades | the dark from which things come and go back |
| The rare | "what is truly rare is available precisely always and for everyone" | preserves by not being grabbed |
| The open mystery | "not touching the concealedness of the simple" | is respected, not solved |
| The clandestine | a conspiracy | lies in ambush |
| The not-yet-known | the "miracles of technology" | waits to be discovered |
| Oblivion | *lēthē* | withdraws and hides its own withdrawing |

For the Greeks, birth and death "take their essence from the realm of disclosiveness and concealment," and the earth is the in-between of the underworld's darkness and heaven's light (p. 60). Myth itself, *muthos*, is the word that lets this disclosing and concealing appear. "There is only one myth, namely the *muthos* of the Greeks" (p. 60).

### 9. Lēthē, Strife and the Field of Oblivion

The deepest opposite of *alētheia* is *lēthē*. The Greeks, Heidegger says, mostly kept silent about it, and he thinks that silence fits.

- **Hesiod.** "Then loathsome Strife bore Ponos, the bringer of pains, / Oblivion and Famine and the tearful Sorrows" (*Theogony* 226–27). Lēthē is a granddaughter of Night. *Limos* isn't hunger as a bodily feeling but "absence of nourishment," a falling-away. Oblivion is "a concealment that withdraws what is essential and alienates man from himself" (*Parmenides*, pp. 72–73).
- **Pindar.** In the seventh Olympian (lines 43ff.), Awe (*aidōs*) "thrusts up the flourishing of the essence," "but sometimes there comes over it the signless cloud of concealment, which withholds from actions the straightforward way." A cloud across the sun darkens things *and* people; and it is "signless" because it doesn't show itself as a cloud. "Forgetting itself occurs already in an oblivion" (pp. 74, 79–82). His summary definition: *lēthē* "conceals while it withdraws" (p. 83).
- **The myth of Er.** The *Republic*, Heidegger notes, both begins (the cave) and ends (Er) with a myth about concealment. Er, killed in battle, revives on his pyre and reports what he saw. The souls about to be reborn cross "the field of Lēthē," "bare of trees and of everything the earth lets spring forth." Since *phusis* is emergence, this field is the very opposite of *phusis* (621a; pp. 118–119). There flows the river **Amelēs**, "Carefree," "whose water no vessel can contain." Each soul must drink a measure. "Those not saved by insight [*phronēsis*] drank more than the measure," and whoever drank only that water "could not be on earth as a man" (pp. 119–123). So "a measure of withdrawing concealment belongs to the possibility of unconcealedness" (p. 123). Heidegger adds that the "river Lethe" of later tradition is a misreading: Lēthē is the field, not the river (p. 126).
- **Phoenix at the turning-post.** Unconcealment is kept by memory. At Patroclus' funeral games Achilles stations by the turning-post "a judge, Phoinix the godlike... / to mark and remember the running and bring back a true story" (*Il.* 23.360–61, Lattimore). The Greek is *hōs memnōito dromous kai alētheiēn apoeipoi*. *Alētheia* here rests on *memnēmai*, keeping in mind (pp. 128–131). That is why the mother of the Muses is **Mnemosyne**, and why "Sing, goddess" opens the *Iliad*: "the poet does not invoke the goddess... the poet is already invoked himself" (p. 127).

The point is not that *lēthē* is simply the enemy. It is "that withdrawal by means of which alone the essence of *alētheia* can be preserved and thus be and remain unforgotten" (p. 127).

### 10. Disclosure as Sheltering, the Open, the Goddess

Two last ideas from the *Parmenides* complete the picture.

1. **Dis-closure.** Heidegger's word *Ent-bergung* plays on *bergen*, to shelter. Think of it like "dis-semination, which is not opposed to the seed," or "in-flaming," which doesn't remove the flame but brings it out. Disclosure takes away concealment and at the same time **shelters** what it reveals in the open: "*Alētheia* is against concealing closure, and through this 'against' it is for sheltering enclosure" (p. 133).
2. **The open.** Behind unconcealment is the open, "the ground and the essential beginning of unconcealedness," which he also calls freedom (p. 143). He rejects the easy explanation that the Greeks were just "visual" people in a sunny land: the Egyptians had light too. "We do not see because we have eyes, but we have eyes because we can 'see'" (pp. 145–146). Seeing being needs nothing special: "simple wakefulness in the proximity of any random unobtrusive being, an awakening that all of a sudden sees that the being 'is'" (p. 149). He even reads Sophocles' *Ajax* (646–47) this way: "The broad, incalculable sweep of time lets emerge everything that is not open as well as concealing (again) in itself what has appeared" (p. 140). Rilke's "open" in the Eighth Duino Elegy, the animal's free gaze into "open water," shares only the sound of the word (pp. 152ff.).
3. **The goddess.** Parmenides' poem is spoken by a goddess who takes the thinker by the right hand. Heidegger's view changed. In 1932 he still asked, "Why then speak here of a goddess at all?" and answered that it shows "the unusualness and sublimity of this way" (*Beginning of Western Philosophy*, p. 109). By 1942 he calls the "personification" reading "a singular error" (p. 6): "The goddess is the goddess 'truth.' 'The truth'—itself—is the goddess" (p. 5). And at the end: "Should we then be offended if in Parmenides' primordial thinking *alētheia* appears as *thea*, as goddess? We would now be more surprised if that were not the case" (p. 123). Still, the last word is modest: "We do not know the essence of truth" (p. 162).

### 11. The Critics and the Retraction

The thesis drew serious fire.

- **Friedländer.** The classicist Paul Friedländer first welcomed the etymology, then objected that in Homer *alēthes* and *alētheia* occur mainly with verbs of saying ("tell the truth"), and that Plato himself (*Cratylus*) etymologised the word differently.
- **Tugendhat.** Ernst Tugendhat (1967) argued that if every uncovering is called "truth," the word loses its job, which is to separate true claims from false ones (Wrathall, appendix to ch. 1).
- **Detienne and Nagy.** Marcel Detienne's *The Masters of Truth in Archaic Greece* (1967) and Gregory Nagy tie early *alētheia* to memory rather than to unconcealment. In traditional poetry you are told to be mindful (*mnē-*) and not unmindful (*lēth-*) of what is right, so truth is what must not be forgotten: "not only memorable but also absolutely unforgettable" (Nagy, 2018). Interestingly, this sits close to Heidegger's own reading of Phoenix and of Nereus, who is "never forgetful of right."

In "The End of Philosophy and the Task of Thinking" (1964) Heidegger gave ground. It is "misleading to call *Alētheia*, in the sense of clearing, 'truth'." "The natural concept of truth does not mean unconcealment, not in the philosophy of the Greeks either." Homer already uses *alēthes* "only in the *verba dicendi*, in statements." So "the assertion about the essential transformation of truth, that is, from unconcealment to correctness, is also untenable" (*Basic Writings*, p. 447). He had written to Tugendhat that it was "advisable to renounce the philosophical use" of "truth" for the clearing, and in a 1960 note to the *Origin of the Work of Art* he called his own use of the word there "inappropriate" (*Off the Beaten Track*, p. 1).

Wrathall argues the retraction gives up the label, not the idea. Heidegger had never confused unconcealment with correct sentences. He only needed the claim that correctness *presupposes* something already showing itself, and that is true whatever the word once meant (Wrathall, p. 16 and the appendix to ch. 1). Dahlstrom adds that calling unconcealment "incorrect" would be "a category mistake since correctness presupposes unhiddenness and not vice versa" (*Dictionary*, p. 12).

### Conclusion

On the narrow philological question, Heidegger lost, and he admitted it. Early Greek mostly used *alēthes* of true speech, and memory (not some primal experience of unconcealment) seems to stand behind the word. The big story of a fall at a datable moment, with Plato and then with Rome, is also too neat, as he conceded about Plato himself.

What survives is harder to dismiss.

1. **Correctness presupposes showing.** Before a sentence can match anything, the thing has to have shown itself. The picture on the wall has to be there to be seen askew.
2. **Concealment is not just error.** Things hide behind other things, in the past, in the future, in night and death and in the very familiarity that makes us stop looking. Truth is always wrested, never simply owned.
3. **Falsity hides by showing.** The pseudonym, the cupboard and false gold catch something the true/false table misses.
4. **Forgetting is not only a mental lapse.** It is something that happens to a whole age, and even forgets itself.

His own examples sometimes say it better than his theses: Odysseus weeping under his cloak, unseen by everyone but the king; the souls drinking a measured draught from a river no cup can hold; old Phoenix by the turning-post, set there to keep the race in mind and bring back a true story.
