On the fourth page of *Paideia* Werner Jaeger wrote a sentence that sums up a whole view of Greece: "All later culture, however high an intellectual level it may reach, and however greatly its content may change, still bears the imprint of its aristocratic origin. Culture is simply the aristocratic ideal of a nation, increasingly intellectualized." Walter Donlan's *The Aristocratic Ideal in Ancient Greece* (1980) opens by quoting it and asking, "But is this true?" This note sets the two books against each other across four centuries, from Homer to the end of the fifth century, with Hermann Fränkel as a third reader of the two poets at the centre of the dispute, **Theognis** and **Pindar**. At issue is whether the **aristocratic ideal** shaped Greek culture, or whether the class was itself shaped by a culture it never owned.

### 1. Jaeger's Thesis

Jaeger distinguishes *education*, the practical and moral training every society gives, from *culture*, the shaping of man towards an ideal whose "vital factor is *kalon*, the Beautiful". Culture in this sense, he argues, began with a class:

> "At first this process is confined to one small class within the state—the nobility. The aristocratic origin of the *kalos kagathos* in classical Greece is as clear as that of the gentleman of England... as the two types were taken over by the bourgeoisie in its rise to power, the ideals inspiring them became universal and at last affected the whole nation. It is a fundamental fact in the history of culture that all higher civilisation springs from the differentiation of social classes—a differentiation which is created by natural variations in physical and mental capacity between man and man... The nobility is the prime mover in forming a nation's culture." (p. 4)

- **Arete as the clue.** The word *paideia* appears only in the fifth century and first meant child-rearing, so Jaeger traces the history of *arete* instead. Its "oldest meaning is a combination of proud and courtly morality with warlike valour", and it is "the quintessence of early Greek aristocratic education". In Homer "ordinary men have no areté"; "Areté is the real attribute of the nobleman" (p. 5).
- **The twofold inheritance.** The city-state took from the nobility "the obligation to be brave", and its higher social standards, "liberality and a certain magnificence in the conduct of life", came from aristocratic practice (p. 6).
- **The motto.** Glaucus' father told him "to strive always for the highest areté, and to excel all others" (*Iliad* 6.208). Jaeger remarks that teachers of every age had quoted it until "modern educational 'levellers'" dropped it (p. 7). Phoenix's charge to Achilles, "to be both a speaker of words and a doer of deeds", is "the earliest formulation of the Greek educational ideal" (p. 8).
- **Philosophy as sublimation.** Plato and Aristotle built their ethics on the old aristocratic morality. Aristotle's great-souled man is modelled on Achilles and Ajax, and "the class limitations of the old ideals were removed when they were sublimated and universalised by philosophy" (p. 11).
- **The setting.** The introduction frames Greece as the beginning of "civilization—the deliberate pursuit of an ideal" for the "Hellenocentric" nations: "Other nations made gods, kings, spirits: the Greeks alone made men." The book appeared in Germany in 1933–34, and its contrast with peoples "racially and intellectually different from us" belongs to that moment.

### 2. Homer: A Warrior Code, Not Yet a Class

Donlan agrees with Jaeger that the Homeric warrior code was the pattern that later aristocrats "assumptively accepted as right and proper". He disagrees about what kind of society produced it.

- **Chiefs, not nobles.** Following Finley, Donlan places Homer's world in the tenth and ninth centuries. It was a society of chiefdoms, "ranked" but not stratified into classes, and it kept "a profoundly rooted spirit of egalitarianism". There is "no birth or class nomenclature in Homer", and the rank and file too are called *aristoi* and heroes.
- **What the words meant.** *Agathos* and *esthlos* mostly mean "brave", *kakos* "cowardly". Worth is public: "how one 'seemed' not what one 'was'". Jaeger makes the same point in his own terms: the Homeric man "estimated his own worth exclusively by the standards of the society to which he belonged", and the Greek conscience was "entirely public" (p. 9).
- **Birth as a spur, not a title.** Glaucus recites his lineage to show what he must live up to. Agamemnon tells Diomedes not to pick the worse man "looking to birth—not even if he is more chiefly" (10.237–39). For Donlan this is the key contrast with the later aristocratic appeal to ancestry "as automatic evidence of class superiority".
- **The view from below.** Sarpedon tells Glaucus their honours are earned by fighting in the front rank (12.310–21), and Thersites demands that the leader answer for his failures (2.225–42). On the poet's insistence that the army cheered Thersites' beating, Donlan writes that "Homer appears to be protesting too much here". The "empty belly" that Odysseus defends against Achilles (19.225ff.) becomes a lasting motif of non-aristocratic thought.
- **Hesiod.** Hesiod has no trace of the warrior ideal. His *basileis* are "gift-devouring", Justice is Zeus' daughter, and he pairs *agathos* with *dikaios*, which Homer never does. Donlan stresses that Hesiod "does not record a radical change in values nor is he consciously rebutting" anyone. He simply speaks for his part of society.

### 3. Hoplites and Challengers

The seventh and sixth centuries brought population growth, colonies, the polis and, between 700 and 650, the **phalanx**. Donlan argues that from then on the aristocratic ideal was "adaptive".

- **Tyrtaeus.** *Arete* now means standing firm in the line: "A common good this, for the whole polis and all the demos" (fr. 9). Tyrtaeus will not praise a man for running, wrestling, beauty, wealth or eloquence without "savage valor". Jaeger calls this recasting "the Homeric ideal of the single champion's areté into the areté of the patriot". Donlan accepts the description but calls it "not so much a rejection... as a transvaluation". The phalanx put the poor man beside the noble: "The poor man could be the hero of the day, the 'nobleman' the coward."
- **Archilochus.** The son of a Parian noble and a slave mother, he threw his shield away and wrote about it ("I'll get another one just as good"). He preferred a general "little, bandy-legged... full of heart". His fable of the fox and the deer contains the **first Greek word for noble birth**, *gennaios*. Donlan argues that the appearance of such words marks the beginning of an aristocratic "reaction".
- **Phocylides.** The ordinary man's maxims: "Seek a livelihood; and when you have a living, arete"; "I want to be middle in the polis". This is the first open attack on birth: "What good is noble birth for those who lack grace in words and counsel?" And then: "In dikaiosyne is summed up all arete."
- **Xenophanes.** An Olympic victory does not put the city in better order or "fatten the treasury of the polis". Usefulness to the community is the test.
- **Solon.** An aristocrat with an aristocrat's pleasures ("dear children, single-hooved horses, hunting dogs and a guest-friend"), Solon still reverses the class vocabulary: "For many kakoi are rich, and agathoi poor; but we will not exchange with them arete for wealth."

### 4. Birth, Style and Softness

Donlan's account of the aristocrats' response has two strands: a new stress on **birth**, and a cultivated **style of life**.

- **Alcaeus** longs in exile for the Assembly and Council. Yet the worst name he can find for Pittacus, a man of good Mytilenean society, is *kakopatrides*, "base-born", which in effect "drummed him out of the nobility". Class labels multiply: *eupatridai*, *hippobotai* and *geomoroi* against "the naked" and "dusty feet".
- **Style.** Long hair with golden grasshoppers (Thucydides 1.6), purple robes at Colophon, chariots kept for prestige long after they were useless in war, and pederasty, which is absent from Homer, Hesiod and Archilochus and becomes an aristocratic marker.
- **Softness.** Mimnermus' "what joy is there without golden Aphrodite?" and Anacreon's love poems written at tyrants' courts turn the old words of merit into words about appearance: in Mimnermus, old age makes a man *aischros* and *kakos*. Donlan's summary: as the polis "co-opted many of the values of the warrior-aristocrat", the nobles were "forced to turn increasingly inward", towards "intellectual, moral and aesthetic excellence".

### 5. Theognis: Three Readings

The *Theognidea* is about 1,400 lines of elegiac verse from sixth-century Megara, mixed with other poets' work. All three scholars agree it voices aristocratic anger at a social upheaval. They read that anger very differently.

The central poem (53–60, in Fränkel's translation):

> "Still is our city a city, but far changed, Cyrnus, the people. Those who formerly knew nothing of justice and law, they who used to clothe their naked bodies in goatskins, scattered outside the walls, living like beasts of the field—they are the 'good men' now, and those who were formerly reckoned good are become the mob. Who can endure such a sight?"

1. **Jaeger: the codification of a class tradition.** Theognis teaches Cyrnus "the things which I myself learnt from the nobles when I was still a boy". His book is a counterpart to Hesiod's codification of peasant wisdom, and the bond of Eros between man and boy is its educational frame. Jaeger admits the code has narrowed: "hard times make narrow minds". Friendship becomes a party loyalty, and the octopus that changes colour amounts to "protective mimicry in the struggle for life against the demos". Its core still holds, however: "that the agathos, the nobleman, shall be noble in conduct as well as in birth. Theognis believed that the identification of nobility with noble character was the strength of his class, and its last defence" (p. 200). The line "In justice all virtue is summed up, and every just man is a nobleman" may come from Phocylides, but Theognis "could not help adopting it as the motto of his own party; for the masses in their struggle for power had borne it on their banner" (p. 203).
2. **Donlan: a crisis of identity.** Donlan takes the same lines as evidence that the nobles' monopoly of the words of merit had failed. The newcomers were called *agathoi* because they voted, grew rich, held office and fought in the phalanx. The nobles therefore relocated excellence where it could not be imitated: in loyalty, endurance, *sophrosyne*, the mean, judgment (*gnome*), justice and beauty. "The morally good man, in other words, was the aristocrat, and only the aristocrat could be a morally good man." In Theognis, Donlan notes, *agathos* never means "brave", and the doctrine that goodness is inborn is "for the first time in Greek literature, plainly spelled out": "by teaching never will you make the kakos man an agathos" (429–38).
3. **Fränkel: an opportunist, and bourgeois.** Fränkel is harsher than either. The new citizens "must have been peasants and small farmers, fishermen and artisans", whom Theognis turns into "skin-clad shepherds, subhumans". His advice is to feign friendship and "howl with the wolves until normal times return. The author of this advice was not an aristocrat by conviction, but an undisguised opportunist." Read as a whole, the collection has "an abundance of angry resentment and grumbling complaints; but of lofty ideals there is very little". There is no duty to law or ancestral custom, no desire for lasting fame, no generosity and no athletic contest. Its friendships are "not ties of the spirit but partnerships for the pursuit of advantages". "If by an aristocratic attitude we mean that spirit which speaks so clearly in the poems of Pindar, the tone of this collection can more fitly be called bourgeois."

All three cite the couplet on breeding (183–92): men breed rams, asses and horses from good stock, yet a noble will marry a base man's daughter for her dowry, so that "money confuses the breed". Jaeger notes that Theognis' ideas returned "during the struggle of the bourgeoisie against the proletariat". Donlan sees in it the most damaging change of all, the separation of wealth from birth.

### 6. Pindar: Phya, Blood and Victory

Pindar celebrates the same values that Theognis defends, but with confidence.

- **Inborn excellence.** "What is given by nature (*phya*) is best" (*Olympian* 9.100). In Jaeger's translation of *Nemean* 3.40–42: "Through inborn glory a man is very mighty; but he who learns from teaching is a twilight man, wavering in spirit." Against those who have merely "learnt", the man wise by nature is the eagle among chattering crows and jackdaws (*Olympian* 2.86–88; *Nemean* 3 end).
- **Jaeger's Pindar.** Pindar is "the voice of aristocracy", showing it at its "noblest transfiguration". The class was "more than the jealous preservation of inherited class-privilege and class-prejudice... It was the true creator of the lofty ideal of humanity" that we still see in Greek sculpture (p. 205). "Pindar is a sculptor on a higher level: he carves his victors into the true models of areta" (p. 213). His educational message is "Become what you are" (p. 217), and Plato's philosophy is "rooted in the aristocratic system of education". Jaeger openly endorses the eagle-and-crows contrast: the gulf "between natural nobility born in its possessor and the knowledge and powers which have been merely acquired by learning... is actual and right" (p. 220).
- **Donlan's Pindar.** The odes link god, hero, family, victor and city in "an unbreakable circularity of cause and effect": the victory proves the inborn excellence that made the victory possible. Donlan calls this "a triumph of propaganda" and "an exclusivism of the purest sort". Pindar never uses *deilos*, never uses *kakos* of persons, and dismisses everyone else as "invisible". In *Pythian* 2 the rule of "the wise" is the good constitution, and democracy is "the noisy host".
- **Fränkel's Pindar.** In Pindar "the archaic ideal was expressed in impeccable purity", untouched by the revolution in ideas around him: "As one born out of due time, he lived on into an age that became more and more alien to him." For Pindar, Fränkel writes, *arete* unites "striving towards lofty ends and... the ability to encompass them". He is "no advocate of... the golden mean of bourgeois contentment." Fränkel also pricks a modern assumption: the claim that Pindar descended from the noble Aegeidae rests on one misread passage. "We have no notion from what sort of a family Pindar came", though the ideas he expresses are those of the leading families.
- **The Persian Wars.** Jaeger and Donlan agree that Pindar's Thebes stood aside in "ignominious neutrality" (Jaeger) while Simonides became the poet of the war dead. For Donlan, Pindar's pan-Hellenism was a solidarity of aristocrats across cities, which later, in civil strife, "tended to outweigh" loyalty to the polis.

### 7. Simonides and Bacchylides

The contemporaries of Theognis and Pindar show that the aristocratic monopoly was not the only voice of the age.

- **Simonides.** Simonides wrote, "I praise and love all, whosoever does nothing shameful of his own free will," and "sufficient for me is the man who is not kakos... but knows at least the justice which aids the polis". His Thermopylae epigram praises a glory that is collective and belongs to all Hellas. Donlan concludes that Simonides "found merit in the qualities and potential for good that were common to all men." Fränkel calls him "far the more modern in spirit" than Pindar, the advocate of "bourgeois propriety". Jaeger, tellingly, calls him "the first sophist" and treats his cool, "masterly" art as lacking Pindar's faith.
- **Bacchylides.** "It is possible for all men to attain to straightforward Dika." For Bacchylides, justice as the best form of *arete* is now a commonplace, and he seldom mentions birth.

### 8. The Fifth Century: Kalokagathia and Its Critics

In democratic Athens, Donlan argues, the aristocrats had to suggest their superiority without saying it outright.

- **Vocabulary.** New words appear: *gnorimoi*, *epieikeis*, *charientes*, *beltistoi*, *dynatoi*, against *ochlos*, *poneroi* (from words for toil) and *misthioi* (wage-earners). The word **kaloskagathos** appears only in the fifth century, close to the English "gentleman". This is the very term Jaeger takes as the timeless sign of aristocratic origin. On Donlan's account, the word was coined as the class came under pressure.
- **The case against.** The Old Oligarch says *arete* is "inborn" in the best men and that poverty makes the demos ignorant and base. Aristophanes' *Frogs* praises men "nurtured in palaistrai and choroi and mousike".
- **The case for.** Pericles' Funeral Oration says that what counts "is not membership in a particular class, but the actual ability (*arete*)". Antiphon the sophist says "we are all by nature born the same in every way". Euripides' *Alexandros* says high birth and low "are a single stock; but time, through custom, has made it a thing of pride." In the *Electra* Orestes says of the peasant husband: "There is no sure gauge for manliness... judge mortals by their intercourse, and by their characters judge men eugeneis... hulks of flesh, devoid of mind, are statues in the market place."
- **The weakness of the reply.** Donlan observes that non-aristocrats could only claim the same virtues for themselves; "it was almost impossible for the non-nobles... to list ethically positive qualities of mind and spirit that were exclusively their own". Even Euripides hedges, giving his peasant a noble pedigree and an unconsummated marriage.
- **Alcibiades.** Alcibiades sums up the upper-class style: seven chariots at Olympia, and the claim that it is not unjust "for a man who has a high opinion of himself not to be put on a level with everyone else". Thucydides notes that this same style made the people fear he was aiming at tyranny (6.15–16).

### 9. The Fourth-Century Reversal

After 403 the restored democracy discredited the upper-class style. In Lysias' speech against the younger Alcibiades, the words of praise belong to democrats and the words of blame to oligarchs. Donlan quotes Helen North: "All the familiar aristocratic values have been thoroughly 'democratized' in fourth-century Athens". He adds that "it is now *agathos* to be a democrat per se."

- **Donlan's verdict.** The aristocratic ideal was "a series of flexible 'stances'" under "constant pressure from below". Its points of contact with Greek culture "were not the results of the filtering down and acceptance by the many of the values of the few, but the reflection of a culture-wide homogeneity of values and attitudes which all Greeks shared." He also endorses Chester Starr: "Fundamentally, the Greek upper classes shared the values and ethical standards of Hellenic civilization as a whole, though they voiced and exemplified those views in a more conscious manner." The deepest force was "the profoundly rooted legacy of egalitarianism from the tribal past".
- **Jaeger's counter-image.** Jaeger describes the same decline from the other side: "no great historical type of society has the strength to formulate its own ideal with deep and sure knowledge until the moment when its life is over". So the dying aristocracy produced Pindar, the dying city-state produced Plato and Demosthenes, and the declining medieval Church produced Dante (p. 222). Both men see the aristocratic ideal gaining fullest expression as the class lost power. They disagree about what that expression was worth.

### 10. Assessment

- **Where Jaeger is right.** The vocabulary of excellence (*arete*, *agathos*, *kalos*) was first fixed in poetry for and about warrior chiefs. The high culture that survives is largely aristocratic in patronage and voice. And Plato and Aristotle did take over and universalise aristocratic values: Aristotle's great-souled man is recognisably Achilles. Jaeger himself does not present the aristocracy as serene. His Theognis is defensive and narrowed, and his aristocracy gains "its fundamental conviction of its own innate value" only "through that struggle for spiritual and material survival" (p. 185). On that point he and Donlan are close.
- **Where Donlan is right.** The sentence "culture is simply the aristocratic ideal" confuses who speaks with who shapes. Donlan shows that almost every element of the "aristocratic" ideal was reacting to someone else: the hoplite *arete* of Tyrtaeus, the justice of Hesiod and Phocylides, the usefulness of Xenophanes, the reversal in Solon. The words for noble birth, the "quiet" virtues and *kalokagathia* itself arrive late and under pressure. The Parthenon, Athena of the potters, and Dionysus' theatre are not aristocratic in origin. Jaeger's claim that all higher civilisation springs from "natural variations in physical and mental capacity" assumes what needs proving.
- **Fränkel's corrective to both.** Fränkel is a reminder that "aristocratic" covers very different things. Pindar's grave, contest-centred idealism and Theognis' resentful, self-interested table wisdom are not one ideal. If the *Theognidea* is "bourgeois" in temper, Donlan's crisis of identity was lived out partly in the opportunistic language of ordinary self-interest. And Jaeger's Theognis, who stood for "noble in conduct as well as in birth", is a kinder portrait than the text supports.
- **Jaeger's endorsement of Pindar.** The weakest point in *Paideia* is Jaeger's approval of Pindar's eagle and crows as "actual and right". This is where the historian becomes a partisan. Donlan's word for the same passages, propaganda, is too reductive for poetry of this quality. But Donlan is right that the circular logic, in which victory proves the inborn worth that caused the victory, was a social argument as well as a religious one.
- **The limits of Donlan.** Donlan relies heavily on Athens and on literary sources written by the elite, as he admits. His "pressure from below" is mostly reconstructed from upper-class complaints. His sociological framework (chiefdom, ranked society, stratified society) does a good deal of interpretive work that the fragments alone cannot confirm. And his conclusion, that both sides shared one culture-wide set of values, sits uneasily with his own evidence that non-aristocrats could not state values of their own. That evidence suggests the elite really did set the terms of the argument, which is a weaker form of Jaeger's thesis.
- **A fair summary.** Greek culture was not simply the aristocratic ideal intellectualised. It was formed by an argument, carried on in poetry for three centuries, over who deserved the aristocracy's words. The nobles defined the prizes, and the polis kept changing who could win them.

### Conclusion

Jaeger read Greek culture as the ideal of the *kalos kagathos*, born among Homer's nobles, sharpened by Theognis and Pindar, and universalised by Plato. Donlan answers that the ideal was a defensive class standard. In Homer it was not yet a class code. It was reshaped by hoplites, peasants and lawgivers, then turned inward to birth, style and "quiet" virtues, and finally democratised in the fourth century, so that what Greeks shared was a common culture that the aristocrats voiced most loudly. Fränkel adds that even the two great "aristocratic" poets differ: Pindar is the archaic ideal in pure form, Theognis its embittered and often bourgeois echo. The best answer to Jaeger's sentence is that the aristocracy gave Greek culture its vocabulary of excellence, while the polis made that vocabulary everyone's.
