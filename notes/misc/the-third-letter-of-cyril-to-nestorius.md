In this letter, St. Cyril responds to Nestorius's teachings, which suggested a division between the divine and human natures of Christ. Cyril strongly rejects this view and emphasizes the **distinction between the divine and human natures of Christ** but maintains that this distinction does not imply **separation** or **composition**.

## Key points from this letter

1. **Distinction Without Separation**: Cyril acknowledges the **distinctiveness** of Christ's divine and human natures, but he asserts that this distinction **does not lead to separation** between the two. The two natures (divine and human) are united in the **one person** of Jesus Christ. Therefore, even though the natures are distinct, they are not divided or separated in Christ. They exist in a **perfect union** without loss of either nature.
2. **No Composition**: St. Cyril explicitly rejects the idea that the union of the divine and human natures of Christ forms a new **composite** nature. Christ is not a mixture or a combination of two natures; rather, He is the **one person** who has both the divine and human natures **united without confusion**. This is a key aspect of Cyril's **miaphysitism** (belief in one united nature of Christ).
3. **The Hypostatic Union**: Cyril emphasizes that Christ's two natures are united in the **hypostatic union**, meaning that the Word (Logos) took on a human nature **in the person of Jesus**. This union is real and complete but **without separating the two natures** or blending them into something new. The divine and human natures retain their fullness, but they exist in perfect union in the one person of Christ.
4. **Theological Implication**: For Cyril, this doctrine is essential in affirming the truth of both the **divinity and humanity of Christ**. If the two natures were separated, it would imply that Christ is not truly both God and man. If they were mixed or composed, it would undermine the integrity of either nature. Cyril's teaching on the distinction without separation ensures that Christ remains fully God and fully man, with no compromise to either nature.

## Excerpt from St. Cyril's letter

In one part of his letter, Cyril states:  
*“For the difference of natures does not imply separation or division, and the union does not introduce confusion or blending. For the divine nature remains divine, and the human nature remains human, and yet the union does not alter them.”*

Thus, **St. Cyril's** argument is that **distinction** between the natures in Christ does not mean that they are **separated** or **composed into something new**, but rather that they are **united** in the **one person of Christ**, maintaining the integrity of both the divine and human elements. This was essential to Cyril's Christology, especially in opposition to Nestorius, who was teaching that Christ was two distinct persons (one divine and one human).
