Paul Veyne opens his short book with a child who believes that Santa Claus brings the presents and also that the parents put them there. He follows it with the Dorze of Ethiopia, for whom, as Dan Sperber reports, "the leopard is a Christian animal who respects the fasts of the Coptic church", yet who guard their livestock on fast days all the same (p. xi). The Greeks, Veyne argues, stood in the same relation to Theseus, Heracles and the Minotaur. This note sets out his answer and tests it against three other readers of Greek myth: **G. S. Kirk** on myths and rational thought, **Jon Mikalson** on the gods of cult and the gods of poetry, and **Jean-Pierre Vernant** on how *muthos* came to be opposed to *logos*.

### 1. The Question and Veyne's Thesis

"Did the Greeks believe in their mythology? The answer is difficult, for 'believe' means so many things." Not everyone believed that Theseus fought the Minotaur, and they knew that poets "lie". "However, their way of not believing these things is disturbing to us. For in the minds of the Greeks, Theseus had, nonetheless, existed" (Veyne, p. 1). Their response was to "purify Myth by Reason": strip off the marvellous and keep the historical kernel. Thucydides makes Minos simply the first king with a navy. Veyne refuses to treat this as a victory of reason over superstition: "myth and logos are not opposites, like truth and error"; the purification of myth by logos "is an ancient program whose absurdity surprises us today". It lasted more than two thousand years. Bishop Bossuet could still date "the famous battles of Hercules" to "a short time after Abimilech" (pp. 1–2).

His thesis is philosophical as much as historical. "Instead of speaking of beliefs, one must actually speak of truths, and these truths were themselves products of the imagination." There are only "programs of truth", which differ from one age to another and coexist within one mind. "The Iliad and Alice in Wonderland are no less true than Fustel de Coulanges" (p. xi). At the end of the book he puts it more bluntly: "The truth is that truth varies" (p. 118).

### 2. History as Tradition, Myth as "They Say"

Veyne starts from a puzzle about ancient historians: they do not cite sources. That is not a methodological weakness. For them "historical truth was a vulgate authenticated by consensus over the ages" (p. 6). Livy and Dionysius never asked of a predecessor, "But how does he know that?" "History is born as tradition, not built up from source materials", and the ancient historian "is source and document himself" (pp. 7–8). "The ancient historian believes first; his doubts are reserved for details" (p. 8). History began as *historia*, inquiry, and resembled reporting more than scholarship. Footnotes came later, from theological controversy and lawsuits (pp. 9–11).

Herodotus states the reporter's licence plainly: "I may be obliged to tell what is said, but I am not at all obliged to believe it. And you may consider this statement to be valid for my entire work" (Hdt. 7.152.3).

Myth itself was older than this kind of inquiry. It was an anonymous "they say", which the Muses only repeat. "Myth is information." It was accepted as one accepts the word of the competent, and so "myth was a *tertium quid*, neither true nor false" (Veyne, pp. 23, 28). Mythical time lay beyond the horizon of memory. A Greek put the gods "in heaven", but "he would have been astounded to see them in the sky", or to hear that Hephaestus had just remarried (p. 18). What changed this was not the discovery of reason or of democracy. It was the arrival of "new powers of affirmation (historical investigation and speculative physics)" which, unlike myth, "expressly offered the alternative between true and false" (p. 24). "The criticism of myth arises from the methods of inquiry. It has nothing to do either with the Sophistic movement... or with the cosmologies of physics" (p. 33).

### 3. The Doctrine of Present Things

The critics' tool was what Veyne calls **the doctrine of present things**: "The past resembles the present, or, in other words, the marvelous does not exist. Now today, men with bulls' heads are rarely seen, and kings do exist; therefore the Minotaur never existed, and Theseus was simply a king" (p. 14). Herodotus applies it to Heracles in Egypt. "The Hellenes tell many different naive stories, and their myth of Herakles is especially foolish" (Hdt. 2.45.1). The Egyptians do not sacrifice humans, and how could one man, still mortal, kill thousands (Veyne, p. 32)? Elsewhere he says that the Hellenes "long ago... distinguished themselves from barbarians by their superior cleverness and freedom from naive stupidity" (Hdt. 1.60.3). Pausanias is ready "to believe that a musician became king of the Ligyes, but I cannot believe that a bird grew out of a man" (Veyne, p. 71).

**Palaephatus** applied the rule mechanically: "what is possible is only that which still exists today." The centaurs were the first men to hunt bulls on horseback. Daedalus' "living" statues were just lifelike. Pelops' winged horses were painted on a ship. The underlying conviction is the important part: "One cannot lie *ex nihilo*, one can only distort the truth" (p. 67). For Palaephatus, "a myth is born of a pun" (p. 68). The result, Veyne notes, is that "the Greeks never had a science of myth as such, but only a science of the history that myths transmitted" (p. 69).

### 4. Two Dogmas and a Thousand Years of Half-Belief

Why not simply reject the whole thing? Because, says Veyne, two unspoken dogmas bounded Greek thought: "It was impossible to lie gratuitously, or lie about everything to everyone, for knowledge is only a mirror" (p. 57). Speech, poetry, proverb and myth were "an involuntary and truthful mirror" (p. 64), and a mirror cannot reflect what is not there. The consequence was a set of paradoxes that run through Greek and Roman thought:

- **Heroes were safe, gods were not.** "There were people who did not believe in the existence of the gods, but never did anyone doubt the existence of the heroes" (p. 42). From the fifth century BC to the fourth AD, "absolutely no one, Christians included, ever expressed the slightest doubt concerning the historicity of Aeneas, Romulus, Theseus, Heracles, Achilles, or even Dionysus".
- **The sum of zeros.** Cicero doubts that Castor and Pollux appeared to anyone, jokes about Romulus' ghost, and denies Romulus' divine birth. Yet "almost everything that is told about these characters is only an empty tale, but the total of these zeroes makes a positive sum. Theseus indeed existed" (p. 50).
- **Sceptical about the whole, credulous about each part.** The learned dismissed myth wholesale, then turned "responsible" and believed again when asked about one episode. Veyne calls this "the return to credulity by means of methodical criticism" (p. 51). He gives three working rules: "state no opinion on the marvelous and the supernatural, admit a historical basis, and take exception to the details" (p. 54).
- **One mind, several programs.** Galen denies centaurs in his theory of medicine, then cites the centaur Chiron as a teacher of medicine when recruiting disciples (pp. 54–56). "The Balkanization of the symbolic field is reflected in each mind... Regarding myth, the Greeks lived for a thousand years in this state" (p. 56).

The two attitudes even have a social map. Varro divided the past into an obscure age, a mythical age "so called because it contains many fables", and a historical age from the first Olympiad in 776 BC (p. 49). Diodorus, playing to a mixed audience, asked readers not to scrutinise Heracles "with so sharp an eye", since in the theatre "we are persuaded there have existed no Centaurs... yet we look with favour upon such products of the myths" (pp. 47–48).

### 5. Rhetoric, Etiology and Ruler Cult

Much myth survived as civic and diplomatic language. Every city had its founder and genealogy, and orators praised them. Rival cities claimed legendary kinship, as Sparta did with Jerusalem and Rome with Troy (p. 77). Veyne says myth here became "rhetorical truth": the content of such speeches was perceived "not as true or, moreover, as false, but as verbal" (p. 79). After Salamis, Athens backed its claim to precedence with victories over the Amazons and in the Theban war, and everyone understood the force behind the words (p. 80). The Greeks laughed at this themselves. In Aristophanes, a speaker tells the Athenians they sit up proudly the moment anyone calls their city "brilliant" (p. 82). "Compared to the Christian or Marxist centuries, Antiquity often has a Voltairean air" (p. 83).

Ruler cult shows the same split. Emperors were gods, yet "not one ex-voto offered to an emperor-god exists": when worshippers needed a real god, they did not turn to the emperor (p. 89). Hence Veyne's summary: the Greeks "believe in them, but they use them and cease believing at the point where their interest in believing ends" (p. 84). He does not think this is hypocrisy. "The plurality of truths, an affront to logic, is the normal consequence of the plurality of forces" (p. 90). It is like a homeopath he knows who prescribes antibiotics for serious cases in complete good faith (p. 86).

### 6. Pausanias Entrapped

Pausanias is Veyne's test case. For most of the *Description of Greece* he reports legends without comment. He is sometimes a historian, sometimes "a philologist, whose task it is to tell what is said" (p. 95). Early on, Pausanias rationalises Theseus and complains of "many false beliefs current among the mass of mankind, since they are ignorant of historical science" (p. 13). Then in Arcadia he changes his mind: "When I began to write my history, I was inclined to count these legends as foolishness; but on getting as far as Arcadia I grew to hold a more thoughtful view of them... in the days of old, those Greeks who were considered wise spoke their sayings not straight out but in riddles" (p. 11). He comes to believe the story of Lycaon turned into a wolf, because in those days men "were guests of the gods, eating at the same board". Now, "when sin has grown to such a height... no longer do men turn into gods, except in the flattering words addressed to despots" (pp. 72, 99).

Veyne's reading is that "it is piety that keeps Pausanias from believing most of the legends he faithfully assembles. We have to dissociate demythologization from irreligion." In this period, "disbelief was recognized not in criticism of myths but in criticism of oracles" (p. 98). Pausanias believed in the gods and in the epiphany at Delphi against the Galatians. What he refused were the stories that dishonoured them. Veyne admits he cannot always tell when Pausanias is joking. Faced with "Agamemnon's sceptre" at Chaeronea, Pausanias concludes "So probably the scepter is the only work of Hephaestus", and Veyne gives up deciding the case (pp. 100–101).

### 7. Kirk: No Such Thing as "Mythical Thinking"

Kirk approaches from the other side: what kind of thing a Greek myth was. He insists that "there are myths, which are traditional tales of many different kinds and functions, but no such thing as 'myth'" (Kirk, p. 278). By the time we meet them, Greek myths were "already fixed in relatively inflexible literary forms" and had "ceased to be part of an oral culture" (p. 95). His target is the idea, which he traces to German idealism and to Snell and Cassirer, of a special pre-logical "mythical thinking" that philosophy displaced. He calls it "the unnatural offspring of a psychological anachronism, an epistemological confusion and a historical red herring" (p. 286). "The dichotomy between myths and reason has done further damage by encouraging the belief that myths are completely irrational. Naturally they are nothing of the kind." Homer's gods and heroes reason constantly. Zeus in the *Odyssey* complains that men blame the gods for evils they bring on themselves (pp. 286–88).

Kirk's most useful idea for Veyne's question is the **exemplum**. By Homer's time, myths had become paradigms: Orestes was held up to Telemachus, and Meleager to Achilles in *Iliad* 9. Fifth-century Athenians still took Achilles, Hector and Penelope "as a guide to the proper demeanour in analogous situations". Here, he notes, "one no longer had to believe that the mythical exempla were true, merely that they were appropriate", like people today who "live by literature" (pp. 289–91). That is a further mode of belief to add to Veyne's list. Kirk agrees, too, that criticism such as Xenophanes' complaint that Homer and Hesiod gave the gods "everything that is a shame and disgrace among men" "made little impression on the majority of people, who continued to perform their religious duties (and incidentally to admire Homer) even when they did not believe every word about the gods in the myths" (p. 98).

### 8. Mikalson: Gods of Cult and Gods of Poetry

Mikalson brings the most important correction, from the evidence of practised religion. Collecting what ordinary Athenians said about religion in orators, inscriptions and dedications, he was surprised "how very separate from these gods of poetry were the everyday beliefs, practices, and religious concerns of fifth- and fourth-century Athenians" (Mikalson, p. 3). "The gods of cult and poetry shared names... but, to put it simply, they shared first names only." The Athenian making a morning offering to Zeus Ktesios, guardian of the storeroom, need not have thought of Homer's cloud-gatherer at all (p. 4). The gods of epic and tragedy "did not have temples, sanctuaries, or altars in Greek cities. They did not receive dedications, sacrifices, or prayers. The gods of poetry are, I would claim, the products of literary fantasy and genius, not of the Greek religious spirit" (pp. 4–5).

Several things follow:

- **Criticism of myth was not criticism of religion.** Piety meant honouring the city's gods, keeping oaths, respecting asylum and hospitality, sacrificing properly, tending the dead, loyalty and care of parents (p. 5). "The morality and justice of the gods was a concern of literature and philosophy, not of cult" (p. 226).
- **Tragedy's gods are made for each play.** The Artemis of the *Hippolytus* and the Artemis of *Iphigeneia among the Taurians* have "almost nothing—apart from their names—in common", and Athena Polias, Athena Skiras and Athena Hygieia were "for all practical purposes independent deities" (p. 10). Aeschylus' Erinyes are "hypothetical, imaginary spirits", invented as the predecessors of the Semnai whom Athenians actually worshipped (pp. 13–14).
- **Tragedy upholds popular piety.** Oracles and seers prove true "so consistently in Greek tragedy that we can claim their accuracy to be convention". Every perjurer, violator of guests or suppliants, and denier of burial in Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides is punished (pp. 129–30).
- **Euripides was not the atheist he is taken for.** His characters attack *literary* myths: the judgment of Paris, Tantalus' feast, divine adulteries ("These are the miserable tales of poets", *Heracles* 1346). He never questions the aetiological myths of Athenian cults, and he ends play after play by founding one (pp. 226, 230, 234). The widow in Aristophanes who complains that Euripides "has persuaded men that gods do not exist" is comedy, no more evidence than the *Clouds* is about Socrates (p. 229). "The fundamental error... is mistaking the myths and anthropomorphic gods of literature for the beliefs and deities of practised religion" (p. 227). His conclusion inverts the usual picture: "Aeschylus, the most theological, is most removed from the practised religion of his time", while Euripides "gives to popular religion, properly understood, the strongest and most consistent support" (p. 236).

### 9. Vernant: How Muthos Became the Opposite of Logos

Vernant explains how "myth" came to mean what we mean by it. Our concept, he says, belongs to a Western tradition "in which myth is defined in terms of what is not myth, being opposed first to reality (myth is fiction) and, second, to what is rational (myth is absurd)" (Vernant, p. 203). Originally *muthos* meant simply "formulated speech" and was not opposed to *logoi*. The opposition was made between the eighth and fourth centuries, above all by **writing**. Oral narration worked like an incantation and drew the audience into sympathetic participation. A written text was placed *es meson*, "at the center of the community", to be challenged by anyone, so that "the rules of political intercourse, as they function in a democratic city governed by *isegoria*... have also become the rules for intellectual intercourse". As a result, "everything that had hitherto given speech the power to impress and convince its audience is now reduced to the level of *muthos*" (pp. 207–8).

Thucydides deliberately gives up "the absence of anything marvellous", which costs his history its charm, in order to write a "possession for all time" (pp. 208–9). Plato's Eleatic Visitor says the earlier philosophers "each appear to me to tell us a myth, as if we were children. One tells us that there are three beings, and that sometimes they're somehow at war with each other... marry, give birth, and bring up their offspring" (*Sophist* 242c–d). Aristotle declines "to consider seriously the subtleties of mythologists" (Vernant, p. 210). By then "there is now such a gap between *muthos* and *logos* that communication between the two breaks down" (p. 211). Within poetry myth changed function too. For Pindar it became paradigm; in tragedy the hero "becomes instead an object of debate". "Myth, in its original form, provided answers without ever explicitly formulating the problems. When tragedy takes over the mythical traditions, it uses them to pose problems to which there are no solutions" (pp. 213–14).

### 10. Plato's Useful Falsehoods

Plato shows the program Veyne describes from inside. In the *Republic*, the stories told about the gods are useful falsehoods for a city that does not know the truth: "the ones we tell because we don't know the truth about those ancient events involving the gods. By making a falsehood as much like the truth as we can, don't we also make it useful?" (*Republic* 382c–d). Veyne reads this not as cynicism but as the normal Greek procedure: rectify an inexact tradition to get as close to the truth as conjecture allows (Veyne, p. 70). In the *Laws* the Athenian Stranger says he has two reasons to think women can fight. He has "faith in a myth" about the Amazons, and he "knows" that Sauromatian women shoot the bow (p. 92). Myth and report are weighed together on one scale.

### 11. How the Problem Ended

Veyne's last historical point is that the Greeks never solved the problem: "Christianization eliminated a problem for which the Greeks had not found the solution and which they were unable to abandon" (p. 112). The Christians expelled the gods, "in whom no one believed", but kept the heroes, "for they believed in them as much as everyone else did". Eusebius dated the battle of Lapiths and Centaurs to the reign of Abimelech, and his chronicle carried this mythical chronology to Bossuet (p. 111).

He also notes that the Christians did not deny the pagan gods so much as dethrone them. "They seem less to censure the pagan gods for not existing than to reproach them for not being good ones", and Eusebius made them demons (p. 114). Belief in the heroes ended only in modern times. Modern history was born of controversy and separated from philology, the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns removed the classics' aura, and Fontenelle declared myths "the history of the errors of the human spirit" (pp. 59–60, 112). With that the question changed. It was no longer "What truth does myth have?" but "What meaning or function does myth have? For one cannot speak or imagine for nothing" (p. 112). Ancients and moderns both believe in the Trojan War, Veyne observes, "but for the opposite reasons. We believe because of its marvelous aspect; they believed in spite of it" (p. 60).

### 12. Assessment

**Veyne's description is excellent; his philosophy is weaker.** His account of modes of belief is the best part of the book: trust in another's word, the doctrine of present things, rhetorical truth, the coexistence of programs within one mind. It fits the evidence, from Herodotus' "I am not at all obliged to believe it" to Pausanias in Arcadia. The other three writers support it from different directions. Kirk's exemplum, believed to be "appropriate" rather than true, is another of Veyne's programs. Mikalson's two kinds of gods explain why criticism of myth did no harm to piety. Vernant's account of writing explains where the critics' new "powers of affirmation" came from.

The final chapter is another matter. Veyne moves from "the Greeks had several criteria of truth" to "truth is a work of the constitutive imagination" and "the truth is that truth varies" (pp. 117–18). That second claim does not follow from the first, and it refutes itself in the way he tries to wave away (p. 127). His own book depends on the difference between what the Greeks *thought* about Theseus and whether Theseus *existed*. He concedes that "the existence or the nonexistence of Theseus... has a material reality that owes nothing to our imagination" (p. 107). His relativism is also least convincing exactly where he tests it hardest. His remarks on a Holocaust denier as a man "close to his truth" show how far the slogan can lead from good sense (pp. 105–6).

**Mikalson changes the question.** Much of the debate about "belief" assumes that the gods of Homer were the gods of the Greek altar. If Mikalson is right, Veyne's question has two halves. Belief in the heroes was historical belief, and it was surprisingly durable, as Veyne shows. Worship of the gods of cult was a practice of reciprocity and was barely touched by critique of myth. Veyne half-sees this: the learned "doubt the gods much more easily than they do the heroes", and Pausanias' piety made him a demythologiser. But he treats religion as one of many programs, "a narrow band" of daily life (p. 87). Mikalson's evidence suggests it was a different kind of thing, not just a different wavelength.

**Kirk and Vernant disagree about the "mental mutation".** Vernant describes a break between *muthos* and *logos* driven by writing and the city's culture of debate. Kirk accepts the social conditions but denies a change in mode of thought: there was rationality in myths and religion in philosophy, and the change took centuries. Their difference is one of emphasis. Vernant is talking about a change in what counted as authoritative speech, Kirk about the capacities of the mind. On this point Veyne sides with Kirk. Myth was not a mode of thought but "knowledge obtained through information", and what changed was the social distribution of knowledge (Veyne, p. 23).

**The answer to the title.** Yes and no, and differently for different objects:

- the heroes: yes, as history, purified of the marvellous;
- the scandalous stories about the gods: generally no among the educated, and often for reasons of piety;
- the gods of cult: yes, as a matter of practice rather than doctrine;
- civic genealogies: as rhetoric;
- the tragic myths: as exempla and as problems to think with.

This answer makes the question harder to dismiss, not easier. Veyne's last line is his best: "But of course they believed in their myths!" What is true of "them" is true of us too (p. 129).

### Conclusion

The Greeks did not divide the world into myth and fact, but into what they had seen and what they had been told. They trusted the word of the poets as one trusts experts. Historical inquiry then taught them to measure the past by the present. The result was a durable half-belief. Heroes became kings, monsters became misunderstandings, and the gods' adulteries became "the miserable tales of poets". Meanwhile cult went on untouched, because the gods of the altar were never the gods of the epic. Veyne shows how this coexistence worked inside single minds for a thousand years. Kirk shows that myths were neither irrational nor a stage of thought. Mikalson separates the gods of poetry from the gods of worship. Vernant explains how writing made "myth" the name for everything that persuades without proving. The question did not end with a refutation. As Veyne says, it ended when Christianity let it be forgotten.
