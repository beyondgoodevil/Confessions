Nobody knows who composed the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*. The two oldest poems of Europe have "no birth certificate, no assignable date, no parents, and no clear place of origin" (Porter, p. 6). The Greeks themselves gave the poet seven birthplaces, half a dozen fathers and several names; modern scholars have made him a single genius, a committee of bards, a whole tradition, a guild's invented ancestor, an illiterate singer who dictated, and a literate poet who revised for years. The set of questions this raises — who, when, where, how, and whether one person made both poems — has been called the Homeric Question since the eighteenth century, though, as Porter argues, the ancients were already asking it.

This note follows the question through its main stages, giving each of the modern positions in its own author's words. The secondary writers disagree with each other at almost every point, and the disagreements are the subject.

### 1. Is the Question Ancient or Modern?

Frank Turner opens his chapter in the *New Companion* with a firm claim: "The Homeric Question, however, is a distinctly nineteenth-century invention, created by the philological enterprise, the romantic concepts of composition, and the historicism of the age" (p. 123). What made it a "question" was the new German university discipline of philology, which "wrested Homer from the world of poets and literature and placed him at the mercy of modern scientific criticism, just as they wrested the Christian scriptures from the realm of sacred reverence" (p. 123). For Turner the debate was fuelled as much by "philology and philological careerism" as by concern for Homer (p. 126).

James Porter disagrees about the date. The Homeric Question, he writes, "was not the invention of the eighteenth century", nor of the Renaissance humanists; "it was alive and well already at an early date in antiquity, almost certainly by the end of the sixth century BCE, if not sooner" — not in so many words, "but with all the features, uncertainties, and urgency of the later versions" (p. 10). Merely to ask who Homer was "is to express a doubt sotto voce as to his identity" (p. 10). Both can be right: the doubts are ancient, the professional industry around them is modern.

Robert Fowler, in the *Cambridge Companion*, dates the modern debate precisely: it "began in 1788 with the publication by Villoison of the scholia in the tenth-century manuscript of the *Iliad*, Venetus Marcianus Graecus 454", whose marginal notes preserved Alexandrian scholarship and "permit inferences about the earlier state of the text" (p. 220).

### 2. What the Greeks Said About Homer

**The poems say nothing.** The poet never names himself. Porter calls him grammatically "no more than a pronoun", a voice that appears in "Sing, goddess" and "Tell me, Muse" and then withdraws (p. 23). Robert Lamberton notes that the Homeric corpus is "exceptionally stingy in the information it provides about its singer", unlike Hesiod, who names himself (p. 35). The closest the poems come is the poet's appeal to the Muses before the Catalogue of Ships:

> Tell me now, you Muses who have your homes on Olympos.
> For you, who are goddesses, are there, and you know all things,
> and we have heard only the rumor of it and know nothing.
> (*Iliad* 2.484–86, Lattimore)

**The Lives filled the silence.** About ten ancient Lives of Homer survive (Porter, p. 65). G. S. Kirk summarises them in his *Iliad* commentary: "Antiquity knew nothing definite about the life and personality of Homer… The horror vacui that was an endemic disease of ancient biographers caused a mass of spurious details to be invented" (p. 1). The commonest version made Homer born in Smyrna, living on Chios and dying on Ios, originally called Melesigenes after his father the river Meles, a descendant of Orpheus and a cousin of Hesiod (Kirk, p. 1). Porter collects the alternatives: up to six fathers, including Telemachus and the bard Phemius; seven or more birthplaces; a lifetime set anywhere from the Trojan War to four centuries later; a love affair with Penelope; a name explained as "hostage", "guide" or "the one who does not see" (pp. 65–66). The pseudo-Plutarchan Life lists the disagreement directly — Pindar for Chios and Smyrna, Simonides for Chios, Antimachus and Nicander for Colophon, Aristotle for Ios, Ephorus for Cyme, Aristarchus and Dionysius Thrax for Athens (Porter, p. 69). The satirist Lucian had Homer tell him in the Underworld that he was really a Babylonian called Tigranes (Porter, p. 70). Porter's conclusion is that the real problem "was not the question whether he existed but the fact that he had too much existence" (p. 71).

Robin Lane Fox gives the claims a more concrete history. By c. 480 BC Smyrna claimed him; the Chian claim was already familiar to the poet of the *Hymn to Apollo*; little Ios struck coins with an imagined portrait of Homer in the fourth century BC, "the earliest portrayal of an individual Greek on a Greek coin", modelled on Zeus; Ios still shows his supposed tomb and holds a festival for him every 15 May (Prologue).

**The earliest witnesses are critics.** Porter makes much of the fact that "the first secure attestations of Homer are without exception harshly critical" (pp. 86–87). Xenophanes, the first writer known to name him, complained that "Homer and Hesiod have attributed to the gods everything that is a shame and reproach among men"; Heraclitus said Homer "deserves to be expelled from the competitions and beaten with a staff" (p. 87). Yet Xenophanes also wrote that "from the beginning everyone learned from Homer", which Walter Burkert calls "the earliest and most comprehensive statement of the authority of Homer as praeceptor Graeciae" (Burkert, p. 95). The oldest and most isolated testimony is the elegist Callinus, who in the seventh century reportedly credited Homer with a *Thebaid* (Burkert, p. 95; Kirk, p. 2). Lane Fox doubts the reading, since the manuscripts of Pausanias give the name as "Calainos" (Prologue).

**The blind man of Chios.** The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* ends its Delian section with the singer asking the Delian girls to remember him:

> "Girls, which man's song is sweetest to you
> of those landing here, and who delights you most?"
> Then you all answer so well about us:
> "The blind man, who lives in rugged Chios,
> whose songs all remain the best forever."
> (*Hymn to Apollo* 169–73, Rayor)

Thucydides (3.104) took this as Homer speaking of himself. Burkert argues it cannot have been: the audience at Delos could see whether the performer was blind, and the singer asks to be named "anonymously", so he must be pointing past himself to the classic poet of Chios. The composite Delian-and-Pythian hymn fits one occasion, the festival Polycrates of Samos held on Delos probably in 522 BC, and there "we hear the voice of a representative of Chian Homerids" (pp. 110–13). The line "all of whose songs are the best among posterity" is, for Burkert, "the clearest expression in epic diction of the notion of a classic" (p. 112). Gregory Nagy reads it as "a formal reflection of the poetry upon its own importance": the archaic poem "presents itself retrospectively as something transmitted by the ultimate poet" (§10). Fowler calls the relation between this blind Chian and the "man of Chios" quoted by Simonides "one of Homeric scholarship's tastiest problems" (p. 231 n. 45).

**The Homeridae.** A guild of rhapsodes on Chios called themselves the Homeridae, "sons of Homer". Pindar calls them "singers of stitched verses" (*Nemean* 2.1–2); a scholium says they were at first Homer's family and later rhapsodes with no blood descent, one of whom, Cynaethus of Chios, first recited Homer at Syracuse in 504 BC (Kirk, p. 2). Kirk compares them to the doctors who called themselves Asclepiadae and notes that the absence of any detailed Homerid biography "suggests that there was no continuous Homerid tradition in Khios" (p. 2). Lane Fox takes the opposite view: the Homeridae were Homer's real heirs, and their existence is good reason "for linking Homer in his lifetime with Chios" (ch. 16).

**Herodotus' date.** Herodotus thought "that Hesiod and Homer were contemporaries who lived no more than 400 years before my time" (2.53, Landmark). Kirk passes on H. T. Wade-Gery's suggestion that "no more than" signals a reckoning of ten forty-year generations that Herodotus himself thought too long; on his own reckoning of three generations to a century, ten generations would put Homer about 760 BC — "not a bad shot!" (Kirk, pp. 2–3). Lane Fox makes the same calculation, giving c. 783 BC as the upper limit (ch. 15). Herodotus was also the first critic to deny Homer a poem: he argued that the *Cypria* could not be Homer's because it contradicted the *Iliad* (2.117), and doubted the *Epigoni* (4.32). Lamberton remarks that "the first prose author we have who cites Homer is (like Xenophanes before him) a debunker" (p. 39).

### 3. How Many Poems Were "Homer's"?

In the archaic period "Homer" meant far more than two poems. Lamberton notes that the cyclic epics "were widely attributed to Homer in the fifth century, along with the Hymns", and that Aristotle accepted the comic *Margites* as genuine (pp. 33, 39). He explains the uncertainty as "the retrospective accommodation of the body of Greek hexameter poetry stemming from oral traditions to criteria of authorship alien to the conditions of their production" (p. 39). When Aeschylus called his tragedies "slices from the great banquets of Homer", he meant this whole body of epic, not the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* in particular (Lamberton, p. 40).

Burkert shows that seventh-century vase-painters were interested in the whole Trojan story rather than "the unique text of the Iliad as the one great classic": the Trojan Horse, the sack of Troy, the Judgement of Paris, Troilus, the duel of Achilles and Memnon (pp. 97–98). The only seventh-century picture tied to the *Iliad*'s own plot is the Euphorbus plate (*Iliad* 17), and even that diverges from our text — "the vase-painter had not read about this in a book" (p. 99). Only by c. 580–570 BC does the whole *Iliad* seem widely known. Lamberton's summary: "The sixth, fifth, and fourth centuries forged a Homer out of disparate elements, with little agreement on the limits of the corpus or the details of the text", and the Hellenistic vulgate finally made "the complete individuation of the figure henceforth known… as 'the Poet'" (p. 44).

### 4. Before Wolf: Josephus to Vico

The first surviving claim that Homer could not write comes from the Jewish historian Josephus around AD 90. Arguing that the Hebrew scriptures were older and more reliable than Greek literature, he said Homer's songs were preserved by memory and only later put together, "and hence its many inconsistencies" (Lane Fox, ch. 5; Fowler, p. 220 n. 1). Lane Fox shows how much weight this polemical remark carried in Europe because it concerned the Bible: Joseph Scaliger endorsed it in the 1560s, and Isaac Casaubon drew the consequence in the 1580s — if Homer's poems were "written down much later, then I do not see how we can ever have them in a correct form even if we have the oldest manuscripts" (ch. 5).

In 1664 the abbé d'Aubignac, during the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns, declared that "Homer was not a good poet, and what is more he never even existed" (Porter, p. 86). Richard Bentley in 1713 gave the English version: Homer "wrote a sequel of songs and rhapsodies, to be sung by himself for small earnings and good cheer at festivals… the Ilias he made for the men, and the Odysseïs for the other sex. These loose songs were not collected together into the form of an epic poem till… above 500 years after" (quoted by Lane Fox, ch. 7, and Turner, p. 124). Lane Fox points out that Bentley, unlike Josephus, still thought Homer wrote.

Two eighteenth-century thinkers anticipated almost everything that followed. Robert Wood, who toured the Troad with the *Iliad* in hand and compared Homer to Bedouin singers he had heard in Syria, suggested in 1767–69 that Homer could not write and composed in the manner of extempore poets (Turner, p. 125; Lane Fox, ch. 7). Giambattista Vico, in the 1730 *New Science*, went further: Homer was "an idea" created by the Greeks, and "the Greek peoples were themselves Homer" (Porter, pp. 33–34). Vico also denied the Trojan War. Porter sees in Vico the pattern of "disavowal" that marks the whole later debate: "Homer was the best poet ever, but he never existed (and here are the proofs for both claims — his poems)" (p. 33).

### 5. Wolf and the Analysts

Friedrich August Wolf's *Prolegomena ad Homerum* (1795), "probably the last major work of European intellectual history to be composed and published in learned Latin" (Turner, p. 125), put the doubts on a philological footing. Wolf's central question, quoted by Turner, was whether "these and the other poems of those times were not consigned to writing, but were first made by poets in their memories and made public in song", altered by rhapsodes, and whether "this entire connected series of the two continuous poems is owed less to the genius of him to whom we have normally attributed it, than to the zeal of a more polite age and the collective efforts of many" (pp. 127–28). Turner sums up: "At the end of the day the epic existed, but at the beginning of the day there may have been no Homer" (p. 128).

Wolf's most famous image argued that a solitary poet without writing could not have made such long poems; they would have been like "an enormous ship, constructed somewhere inland in the first beginnings of navigation: its maker would have had no access to winches and wooden rollers to push it forward, and therefore no access to the sea itself" (Turner, pp. 129–30). He concluded that the received text was "not the one who flourished in the mouths of the Greeks of his own day, but one variously altered, interpolated, corrected, and emended" (Porter, p. 35). Porter notes that Wolf kept "the ghost — the idea — of an original form" all the same, and that his 1804 edition was equivocally titled *Works and Remains of Homer and the Homeridae* (pp. 35–36). Fowler adds an irony: Wolf's premise that Homer lived in an illiterate age is one "which, ironically enough, we now know to be false" (p. 220).

Turner traces the nineteenth-century Analysts who followed. Karl Lachmann (1837–47) cut the *Iliad* into eighteen separate lays, on the model of the *Nibelungenlied*; Gottfried Hermann proposed a "kernel" by Homer expanded by later poets; George Grote found an original *Achilleid* in Books 1, 8 and 11–22; Adolf Kirchhoff (1859) analysed the *Odyssey* into a core and later additions; Wilamowitz called the *Iliad* "a miserable piece of patchwork" (pp. 131–37). The problem, as Turner says, was "the incapacity of any two scholars to discern quite the same building blocks" (p. 133). The Unitarians answered that the poems showed a single design; Nitzsch argued for one Homer who drew earlier songs together (p. 133). Grote also made the important distinction that "disallowance of the historical personality of Homer is quite distinct from the question… whether the Iliad and Odyssey are originally entire poems" (p. 135).

The debate mattered beyond classics because it ran parallel to biblical criticism. The Tractarian E. B. Pusey warned in 1854 that "the skepticism as to Homer ushered in the skepticism on the Old Testament" (Turner, pp. 142–43). Gilbert Murray's *Rise of the Greek Epic* (1907), borrowing from studies of the Pentateuch, treated the *Iliad* as a "traditional book", repeatedly expurgated by generations of poets, "a Lay with encrusted additions" (Turner, p. 144; Lane Fox, ch. 5).

### 6. Neoanalysis

Neoanalysis, named by the Greek scholar J. T. Kakridis in the 1940s, is in Malcolm Willcock's account "consciously and explicitly Unitarian, starting from the belief that the Iliad, virtually as we have it, is the work of one great poet" (p. 174). It still analyses the text, but in order to see the earlier stories behind it. Its central example is the mourning for Patroclus in *Iliad* 18. Achilles lies "stretched out in the dust, mightily in his might"; Thetis and the Nereids come up from the sea and lament; Thetis holds her son's head. Each detail fits Achilles' own death better — the same phrase is used of Achilles' corpse in *Odyssey* 24.39–40, the *Aethiopis* had Thetis and her sisters mourn him, and holding the head is a gesture of mourning for the dead. "Essentially, Patroclus does not rate Nereids: Achilles does" (pp. 176–78). A second example, the rescue of Nestor in *Iliad* 8, mirrors the story told in Pindar of Antilochus dying to save his father from Memnon (pp. 179–81).

Willcock's own conclusion is moderate. The echoes are best seen "not as some kind of imitation of a particular pre-existing poem, whether written or oral, but as the result of conditioning of the mind of the poet by the material with which he was familiar"; Homer "had on other occasions sung of the death of Achilles… There is no need to hypothesize an external source" (p. 188). Understood this way, neoanalysis and oral theory can combine, "restoring the poet to the poetry" (p. 189). Its appeal, he says, is that "we feel that we are looking over his shoulder" (p. 175). Lane Fox is "sceptical" of the Thetis argument and "wary" of the Memnon parallels, because none of the sources for the Cycle is earlier than Homer (ch. 10).

### 7. Milman Parry and the Oral Formula

The question changed in 1928. Milman Parry, a young Californian working in Paris, showed in his French doctoral theses that the noun-epithet phrases of Homer ("swift-footed Achilles", "much-enduring divine Odysseus") form systems of great "economy and scope": for each character, case and metrical position there is usually one phrase and only one (Foley, pp. 147–48; Lane Fox, ch. 9). He defined the formula as "an expression regularly used, under the same metrical conditions, to express an essential idea" (Foley, p. 147). At first Parry called this diction only *traditional*. His examiner Antoine Meillet pointed out at the defence that he had ignored oral composition, and Matija Murko, who had studied Bosnian singers in the field, led him "to the study of oral poetry in itself and to the heroic poems of the South Slavs" (Lane Fox, ch. 9; Foley, p. 148). In articles of 1930–32 Parry argued that only composition in performance explains such a system, and that "the failure to see the difference between written and oral verse was the greatest single obstacle to our understanding of Homer" (Lane Fox, ch. 9).

Lane Fox insists that Parry was not the first to make the comparison: a century of fieldwork lay behind him. Serbian singers were being called "Homers" by 1819; in the 1850s–60s Chokan Valikhanov and Wilhelm Radloff recorded the Kyrgyz *Manas*, twenty times the length of the *Iliad*, from singers who could not read; Radloff saw that a singer "always improvises… so that he is incapable of reciting his poem twice in an absolutely identical manner", and compared him to an improvising pianist (ch. 8). Radloff also judged it "impossible that so enormous a work as Homer's poetry could have survived a decade had it not been written down" (ch. 8).

Parry and his assistant Albert Lord recorded Bosnian and Montenegrin singers in 1933–35. Their great find was Avdo Međedović, a Muslim butcher in his sixties who could not read, who in 45 days composed some 78,000 verses, including a *Wedding of Smailagić Meho* of 12,323 lines, close to the length of the *Odyssey*, worked up from a 2,000-line printed version he had heard read aloud (Lane Fox, ch. 9). Lord's *The Singer of Tales* (1960) extended the analysis from the formula to the "theme" (typical scene) and the "story-pattern"; Foley notes that the South Slavic Return Song, with its absent husband, disguise, scar, weaving wife and suitors, survives in hundreds of versions (pp. 150, 156–57).

The singers had their own idea of what a "word" was. Foley quotes Mujo Kukuruzović, asked whether "Vino pije Ličić Mustajbeg" was one word: "In writing it can't be one… But here, let's say we're at my house and I pick up the gusle… that's a single reč on the gusle for me" (p. 152). This is why, Foley argues, singers could honestly say their performances were "word for word" the same while scholars found them different (p. 159).

Gregory Nagy accepts Parry's findings and draws the furthest conclusion from them. He quotes Denys Page's verdict that the poet "has no freedom to select his adjectives", and calls the problem this raised "a serious problem of esthetics": "how can compositions that have always seemed so deliberate and integral in their artistry result from a system of diction that is so mechanical—one might almost say automatic?" (§§2–3). His answer is to locate the art in the tradition: "the artistic intent is indeed present—but that this intent must be assigned not simply to one poet but also to countless generations of previous poets steeped in the same traditions… For me the key is not so much the genius of Homer but the genius of the overall poetic tradition that culminated in our Iliad and Odyssey" (§5). Foley reaches a similar result by a different route, "traditional referentiality": a formula such as "swift-footed Achilles" calls up "the entire heroic portrayal… as known to the tradition", so that traditional phrasing carries meaning rather than merely filling the line (pp. 167–68).

### 8. Could Oral Poetry Be This Good?

The obvious objection to the oral theory is quality. G. S. Kirk in 1962 found in the Yugoslav songs "an effect of monotony and lack of imagination" and placed their singers in a "reproductive" stage after the creative one; Hugh Lloyd-Jones judged them inferior even to Walter Scott's narrative poems. Foley answers that both critics read the South Slavic material only in translation, and that the argument from quality is "tautological": Homer's place at the head of Western taste disqualifies every rival in advance (pp. 161–62).

Fowler calls it "an old canard that Homer is simply too good to be oral": Avdo "need hardly be Homer's equal for the comparative case to be made… At bottom, the argument 'these poems are so good they must be written' simply begs the question" (p. 223). He grants, though, "a stubborn percentage of non-formularity, particularly in the similes and speeches", and asks where the onus of proof should lie (p. 223).

Adam Parry, Milman Parry's son, accepts the difference in quality but explains it historically. Yugoslav oral poetry was "a rural, one might almost say, backwoods phenomenon, existing alongside a literary urban culture", so much of the best talent was lost to it; the Greek tradition worked at the centre of a sophisticated society (p. 24). He warns against "a confusion of Homer and the tradition", which he calls "an error from which Homeric studies are only now beginning to emerge" (p. 23). Lane Fox likewise thinks no living oral poem approaches Homer — the Bosnian line has ten syllables, the *Manas* line seven or eight, against the complex hexameter — and singles out Homer's "supreme art of saying much by saying little" (ch. 10).

Kirk's commentary offers a more interesting explanation than his 1962 book. The Greeks were unusually late in acquiring a usable script: Linear B was too clumsy for literature and then disappeared altogether. As a result the oral tradition "continued and expanded" for two or three centuries in a society that was growing richer and more experimental; by the eighth century "traditional poetical methods survived into an age when traditional restraints on the scope and form of oral verse had virtually disappeared". The alphabet and Homer were therefore "not so much cause and effect as parallel products of the new expansionism", and "the eighth century B.C. was exactly the period in which conditions were at their best for the production of a monumental oral epic" (pp. 10–11).

### 9. The Coming of the Alphabet

Everything turns on when Greeks could write. Barry Powell argues that the Greek alphabet was a single invention by one person, "the adapter", who took the West Semitic consonantal script and required every vowel to be written — "His law is never violated" (p. 14). The arbitrary choices (which Phoenician signs became vowels, the confusion of the sibilants) are "only explicable on a theory of monogenesis… A single man at a single time" (p. 18). Following Rhys Carpenter, he dates the invention to about 800 BC (pp. 18–19). The earliest Greek inscriptions are not commercial at all — "we look in vain in early alphabetic inscriptions for any business practice" — but verse, which suggests that the adapter's aim was "to record metrical verse" (pp. 24–25, 29). Powell revives H. T. Wade-Gery's 1952 thesis: the alphabet was invented in order to write down Homer (p. 29). He even suggests that the legend of Palamedes, credited by Stesichorus with inventing letters, preserves the adapter's name (pp. 25–26).

Lane Fox reviews the more recent finds — inscribed sherds from Eretria (c. 770–760), Lefkandi (c. 770) and Gabii in Italy (c. 775), and a Phrygian alphabet borrowed from Greek at Gordion — and dates the invention to c. 790–780 (ch. 12). He rejects Powell's thesis because these earliest inscriptions are mostly marks of ownership, made before writing is known to have been used for hexameters (ch. 12).

Two famous inscriptions bear on Homer's date. The Dipylon jug from Athens (c. 750–740) carries a hexameter offering the jug to "whoever of the dancers sports most gaily" (Lane Fox, ch. 12). "Nestor's cup", buried on Ischia c. 725–720 and now shown to have been made at Teos, near Chios, reads: "I am the cup of Nestor, good to drink from. Whoever drinks from this cup, desire of fair-crowned Aphrodite will at once seize him" (Lane Fox, ch. 12). The joke plays against Nestor's huge golden cup in *Iliad* 11.632–37. Powell calls it "an apparent deliberate reference to Homer's Iliad", and therefore "a terminus ante quem for the text of our Iliad" (p. 24). Kirk agrees that the couplet refers to *Iliad* 11 (p. 3). Others think it refers to a cup already famous in older poetry; Lane Fox answers that Homer's cup looks invented for its episode (ch. 12).

Kirk lists the reasons for doubting that Homer used writing on a large scale: the newness of the alphabet, its practical uses, the gradual growth of written literature in Hesiod and Archilochus, and the cost and clumsiness of papyrus rolls. The one strong argument for writing is "the length, complexity and subtlety of the Iliad as a whole", but that feeling "is one belonging to habitual literates, which is a reason for regarding it with some suspicion" (pp. 9–10).

### 10. How Did the Poems Get Written Down?

Here the modern answers divide most sharply.

**Lord: oral dictation.** Albert Lord laid down two principles: "an orally composed poem cannot be handed on by the tradition of oral song without fundamental change", and "the [oral] poet's powers are destroyed if he learns to read and write" (A. Parry, p. 2). Together they mean that the *Iliad* must have been dictated by an illiterate Homer to a scribe, as Avdo dictated to Parry's assistant (A. Parry, p. 3).

**Kirk: oral transmission for a century or more.** Kirk accepted Lord's second principle but rejected the first. He thought the monumental *Iliad* was composed orally and passed down by singers with only minor changes until it was written, probably "in the second part of the sixth century B.C. and as a result of rhapsodic competitions at the Panathenaia" (Kirk, p. 4; A. Parry, p. 4).

**Adam Parry: written at the moment of composition.** Adam Parry's essay "Have We Homer's *Iliad*?" attacks Kirk. Singers do not memorise in our sense. Asked whether he sang another singer's song "word for word, and line for line", the singer Đemo Zogić said yes — and in the same interview admitted that "two singers won't sing the same song alike" (p. 7). Zogić's own two versions of one song, seventeen years apart, grew from 1,370 to 1,430 lines with twenty-three alterations; carried over six generations, the changes would make "an 'Iliad'… vastly different", so that "it is surely the singer at the end of this process whom we should think of as the author of our text" (p. 8). The *Iliad*'s quality depends on fine details — the closing lines of the View from the Wall, Helen's "slut that I am. Did this ever happen?" — that would not survive such change: "The margin of flexibility of language and structure in our version of the Iliad is, I submit, rather small" (pp. 13–16). Hence "the name 'Homer,' if by this we mean the author of our poem, must be reserved for the poet who composed the Iliad at the time when it was put into writing" (p. 16). Parry also rejects Lord's second principle: an Ionian of 725 BC who learned to write "learned only that: how to put down marks… These marks entailed no new style of saying or singing whatever" (p. 26). So Homer may have written himself, and "it seems difficult not to see in the use of writing both the means and the occasion for the composition… of poems which must have transcended their own tradition in profundity as well as length" (p. 27).

**Nagy: gradual fixation.** Nagy's "evolutionary" model treats the fixed texts as "a cumulative process, entailing countless instances of composition/performance in a tradition that is becoming streamlined into an increasingly rigid form as a result of ever-increasing proliferation" (§15). The poems became Panhellenic, synthesising local traditions "into a unified Panhellenic model that suits most city-states but corresponds exactly to none" (§14). Powell (p. 30 n. 54), Fowler (p. 225 n. 18) and Lane Fox (ch. 16) all reject this model, chiefly because Richard Janko's statistical study of the language places the *Iliad* consistently earlier than the *Odyssey*, and both earlier than Hesiod — a sequence that only makes sense if each poem was fixed at a particular time (Fowler, p. 225).

**Fowler: a spectrum.** Fowler argues that the poems "were conceived as new kinds of text by their oral poet(s). The notion that they wrote and lovingly revised their texts with pen in hand seems anachronistic, but it is possible that large portions of the songs, through premeditation, came to be fixed" (p. 230). He proposes four stages: "(1) non-fixed, because oral; (2) oral, with many consciously fixed passages; (3) mostly fixed, and therefore written; (4) written to start with", and suggests that "in Homer's day… Greek epic moved through (2) to (3)" (p. 230). He uses Herodotus as an analogy: an oral performer who decided to write and still kept parataxis and ring-composition (p. 226).

**Lane Fox: an illiterate master who dictated for his heirs.** Lane Fox argues that the *Iliad* cannot have been transmitted orally for long, since its consistent language, its one-way influence on the *Odyssey* and the absence of anything later than c. 680 in its similes all point to an early fixed text (ch. 10). Nor was Homer a writer: if he had written or reread his text he would have corrected the famous slip in *Iliad* 9, where the embassy of three men is described seven times with dual verbs meant for two (ch. 11). "An oral composer, working in performance, would not ever think of returning and correcting" (ch. 11). So Homer dictated, at perhaps a thousand lines a day, "just over a fortnight" for the whole poem; and he did so not to imitate Near Eastern texts or to serve kings, but to leave a valuable asset to his family, the Homeridae (ch. 11). "Oral composition in performance is my answer… I believe he did so because he was illiterate" (ch. 11). He likens Homer to the jazz pianist Erroll Garner, who could not read music: "We are still buying the sheet music" (ch. 11).

**West: years of writing and insertion.** M. L. West, by contrast, thinks both poems were "composed and set down in writing over many years", and that the key to their difficulties is that each poet "worked forward… without having worked out in advance exactly how the narrative was to go" and "made many insertions in what he had already written" (pp. 1, 4). The poet of the *Odyssey* was "trained as an oral performer of epic, but he resolved to create an Odyssey that would emulate the Iliad in scale and be likewise stabilized in writing" (p. 2).

**Burkert's agnosticism.** Burkert imagines "the first written Iliad as a set of leather scrolls in the first half of the seventh century, emerging from an oral tradition that had been flourishing mainly in the eighth", but admits: "I frankly do not know why and under what conditions the Iliad was originally written down, nor who originally read and preserved the scrolls" (pp. 93, 114).

### 11. The Peisistratids and the Making of a Classic

An ancient story credited the tyrants of Athens with collecting Homer. The Platonic *Hipparchus* says that Hipparchus, son of Peisistratus, "was the first to bring the Homeric epics into this country and forced the rhapsodes at the Panathenaia to go through them in due order, one taking the cue from the other" (Burkert, p. 104). Later versions grew more elaborate; one scholium has Peisistratus buy up every scrap of Homer and set seventy-two scholars to reassemble the poems (Lamberton, p. 34 n. 2). Wolf and Lachmann made this "Peisistratean recension" the moment the poems were first written.

Fowler rejects that reading. The evidence "suffices to show that a text was produced for the Athenian festival", but "it does not show that this was the first such text; indeed it rather suggests the opposite… The story implies that liberties were being taken, not that there was no text" (p. 224). Burkert likewise reads the *Hipparchus* as a rule about order, ensuring "that Hector's Death came after Hector and Andromache and not vice versa", since the whole *Iliad* alone would take thirty to forty hours to recite (pp. 103–04).

Burkert's own interest is in reception: how these two poems, under one name, came to exclude all others. His answer is a change of performers. The *aoidos* who improvised to the lyre gave way to the rhapsode with a staff, who recited a fixed text "learned by heart and available also in book form" (p. 101). The rhapsodes, he argues, were reacting to the success of Stesichorus' choral narratives, which retold epic stories with music and spectacle; epic answered "by reduction to the essentials of a narrative text" (pp. 108–09). The result was "a frozen classic… a fixed text, a set of books no doubt… This is the making of Homer in the sixth century, possibly due to Homeridai of Chios" (p. 103). A second step was Homer's adoption as a school text, so that Xenophanes' line came true; a third, by the end of the fifth century, was "the classic on a shelf" (pp. 114–16).

The text itself remained fluid for centuries. Lane Fox notes that fourth-century speakers like Aeschines quote lines in forms that differ from ours, and that early Ptolemaic papyri contain many extra lines (ch. 16). Lamberton cites a third-century BC papyrus of about 90 lines of *Iliad* 8 that includes some 30 lines missing from our text (p. 34). Only after the Alexandrian scholar Aristarchus (c. 216–144 BC) did copies agree on the number of lines (Lane Fox, ch. 16; Fowler, pp. 231–32). Even so, Martin West estimated that uncertainty about exact wording and intrusive lines affects only about 1.1 per cent of the *Iliad*, excluding Book 10 (Lane Fox, ch. 16).

### 12. When?

The scholars considered here date the *Iliad* across more than a century.

| Scholar | Date of the *Iliad* | How written down | Same poet for the *Odyssey*? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kirk (1985) | 8th century; Herodotus' figure gives c. 760 | Mostly oral; fixed in writing in 6th-century Athens | Probably; *Odyssey* "within a generation or so" |
| Powell (1997) | Early 8th century | Written down by the adapter of the alphabet, perhaps in Euboea | Treats both as Homer's; *Odyssey* "a Euboian poem" |
| Lane Fox (2023) | c. 750–740 | Dictated by an illiterate Homer for his heirs | Yes, most of it, c. 735–725 |
| Fowler (2004) | About 700 | Oral poet moving to a fixed, recorded text | Perhaps not (citing Scodel) |
| Burkert (1987/2001) | First written Iliad in the first half of the 7th century | Leather scrolls; then fixed by 6th-century rhapsodes | Not discussed |
| West (2014) | 680–640 | Composed in writing over years, with insertions | No: a different poet, "Q", c. 630–600 |
| Nagy (1979) | Fixation completed gradually over a long period | No single moment of writing | The question is not decisive |

Lane Fox gives six arguments for an eighth-century date (ch. 15). The alphabet was not available before c. 800. Janko's statistics put the *Iliad*'s language before Hesiod's *Theogony* (c. 710). Homer's teachers are anonymous, whereas poets from the 720s on were remembered by name. Nestor's cup dates to c. 725. The simile of "Arima, where they say is the bed of Typhon" (*Iliad* 2.781–83) knows Ischia's volcano rumbling but not its great eruption of c. 700. And Lane Fox thinks Hesiod's Muses, singing *homēreusai*, "fitted together", make a pun on Homer's name (*Theogony* 39) — "as yet I am in a minority of one". He adds that Homer shows no knowledge of the overthrow of the Lydian kings by Gyges in the 680s or of hoplite warfare, established by c. 675: changes so great that they would have shown through, "if only in a simile" (ch. 15). He also answers the argument from vase-painting: scenes from Shakespeare first appear in English painting only with Hogarth, a century and a half after the plays (ch. 12).

West dates later. His case concerns the *Odyssey*, which he places firmly in the last third of the seventh century: its Egypt reflects the Greek trading post at Naucratis under Psammetichus; its "Libya", where sheep lamb three times a year, is the Cyrenaica settled from c. 631; it comes after the *Iliad*, after the post-Iliadic Doloneia and Memnon story, and after an *Argonautica* that knew the northern Black Sea (pp. 35–41). "I do not see how anyone in the face of these arguments can entertain a date for the Odyssey before 650, let alone the eighth-century dating that is often taken as a given by those who have not gone into the matter" (p. 41). He dates the *Iliad* between 680 and 640 (p. 40). Kirk notes that West's earlier argument for such late dates "few people have been won over" by (p. 7).

Kirk's own survey of the material evidence explains why dating is so hard. The poems mix objects from many periods: a Mycenaean tower shield and boar's-tusk helmet; Iron Age cremation and paired throwing spears; possible hints of hoplite fighting. Supposedly late items "are nearly all chimerical", and only one detail is securely later than 700, a couplet at *Iliad* 7.334–35 already rejected by Aristarchus (pp. 6–7). Lane Fox makes the same point through the cinema: a historical film gets its armour from the past and its social values from its own day, and Homer gave his heroes Mycenaean bronze and the social customs of his grandparents' generation, c. 800–780 (ch. 14).

### 13. Where?

Most scholars place the *Iliad*'s poet in Ionia, on the Aegean coast of Asia Minor. Kirk argues from the dialect: its Ionic forms show developments not found on the mainland, so "a mainland origin for the Homeric oral tradition is out of the question"; the poem's landscapes point the same way — Samothrace seen from Troy, the birds at the mouth of the Cayster, storms on the Icarian sea, Niobe on Mount Sipylus (p. 4).

Lane Fox builds the fullest case. The similes assume an audience that knows the Icarian sea in a south-east winter wind, the bull dragged round the altar of Poseidon at the Panionion on Mount Mycale, the waterbirds of the Asian meadow by Ephesus, and the rock of Niobe near Smyrna; Homer therefore performed "somewhere between, say, Ephesus and Miletus" (ch. 4). He also thinks Homer visited the Troad himself. From Troy one can see the peak of Samothrace over Imbros, where *Iliad* 13 seats Poseidon to watch the battle; Lane Fox quotes the traveller Alexander Kinglake: "now I knew that Homer had passed along here, that this vision of Samothrace over-towering the nearer island was common to him and me" (ch. 3). The gods' vantage points, Kallikolone to the east and Heracles' mound on the Sigeum promontory to the west, match real hills on either side of the plain (ch. 3). He even suggests visits to Lycia, home of Sarpedon and Glaucus (ch. 4). Against Powell's Euboean Homer he notes that only three "west Ionic" forms occur in the *Iliad*, that the first Greek killed in the poem is the Euboean leader Elephenor, and that Euboea never claimed Homer (ch. 4).

Powell's Euboea rests on different evidence: the earliest alphabetic writing, at Lefkandi and Eretria; Aulis, across the strait from Euboea, as the fleet's harbour; and the Catalogue of Ships, which begins with Boeotia. "The Iliad and the Odyssey must have been written down there in the early 8th century B.C." (p. 31). West places the *Odyssey*'s poet on the other side of the Aegean from the *Iliad*'s: "The Iliad poet is clearly at home in Asia Minor… For Q, by contrast, Asia Minor (apart from Troy) lies quite outside his field of vision"; Q probably lived in Attica or Euboea, had seen Delos and Ithaca, but knew the inland Peloponnese so poorly that he has Telemachus drive a chariot straight over the Taygetus range (pp. 82, 86–88).

### 14. One Poet or Two?

Ancient scholars called *chorizontes*, "separators", already gave the two poems to different authors, and the pseudo-Longinus explained the difference by making the *Odyssey* the work of Homer's old age (Porter, pp. 22, 112). Kirk says the latter "cannot be disregarded" (p. 3).

West is the strongest modern separator. He devotes a long section to fifty-two places where the *Odyssey* borrows lines from the *Iliad* and uses them "inaccurately or inappropriately" (pp. 70–76). Hermes' sleep-giving wand, essential in *Iliad* 24, is carried pointlessly in *Odyssey* 5; Hector's words to Andromache, "war is the business of men", become "speech" or "the bow" in the *Odyssey*; a line about two men levering a rock onto a wagon becomes twenty-two four-wheeled wagons that could not lever the Cyclops' door-stone — "complete nonsense" (pp. 70–74). Older scholars reached the same verdict on which poem borrowed from which: Gemoll found in 1883 that of 136 parallels, the *Odyssey* was the original in only three, all in suspect lines (p. 25). For West the *Odyssey*'s poet "is a chronically inconsistent narrator", with "a fluency of invention rather than of expression" (pp. 66, 69).

Lane Fox, on the other hand, believes "most of the Odyssey to be by Homer, poet of most of the Iliad" (Preface). He uses the one-way borrowing differently: the *Iliad* influenced the *Odyssey* but never the reverse, so the *Iliad* must already have been a fixed text when the *Odyssey* was composed (ch. 10). Fowler finds the separators' case "inconclusive, as ever, though the cumulative case is hard to resist", and passes on Ruth Scodel's suggestion: "Perhaps Homer wrote the Iliad, one of the Homeridae the Odyssey" (p. 230 n. 43).

### 15. Homer the Idea

Porter's book is not an attempt to answer the Homeric Question. His subject is why it has never gone away. "Homer is an impossible object, an entity who only becomes tangibly real and actual in the very failed effort to grasp him" (p. 2). Following the young Nietzsche's question of 1869 — whether "a person [has] been made out of a concept, or a concept out of a person" — he treats "Homer" as "the ongoing problem of his own identity" (pp. 4–5). The ancients answered with too many Homers; the moderns with none; Thomas De Quincey summed up the debate in 1841: "Some say, 'there never was such a person as Homer.' 'No such person as Homer! On the contrary,' say others, 'there were scores'" (p. 6).

Several of Porter's points bear directly on the question.

- **Portraits.** No portrait of Homer is a likeness; they form "a series of copies that lacks any original" (p. 56). Pliny the Elder knew this: "those whose likenesses we no longer have are modeled ex nihilo, and a sense of loss gives birth to countenances that have not been handed down to us, as in the case of Homer" (p. 57). Christian Gottlob Heyne in 1801 described the process exactly: "there were the ancient poems, there was a name, the two were put together, and the result was a Homer" (p. 62).
- **Blindness.** Homer's blindness, Porter argues, is "a topos rather than a fact", whose purpose was "to index the range of uncertainties and doubts" about how a poet so remote could know what he described (p. 145). The *Odyssey*'s blind Demodocus — the Muse "had taken his eyes but gave him honey-sweet music" (*Odyssey* 8.64, McCrorie) — supplied the model, and Eustathius called Demodocus Homer's "self-portrait" (p. 135). Odysseus praises Demodocus for singing of Troy "as though you were there somehow or heard from a good source" (8.491, McCrorie): exactly the claim that later readers made, and doubted, for Homer.
- **Parry's ghost.** Even the oral theory could not banish the single poet. Parry "merely displaced Homer's unity onto that of the oral tradition", and when he wrote that hearing the South Slavs gives "the overwhelming feeling that, in some way, he is hearing Homer", Porter comments: "Parry is not listening to Homer. He is listening to his idea" (pp. 36–37).
- **Debunkers.** The debunkers are part of the same tradition as the worshippers. Porter traces a line from Stesichorus, whose *Palinode* claimed Helen never went to Troy ("Homer did not know it, but Stesichorus did"), through Zoilus the "Scourge of Homer", to Samuel Butler, who argued in 1897 that the *Odyssey* was written by a young unmarried woman of Trapani in Sicily and that Troy never fell (pp. 95–96, 110, 118–25).

Porter's conclusion is that Homer's importance lies in "his capacity to continuously produce culture in his wake": he is "the total ensemble of his literary and nonliterary representations, high and low" (p. 128).

### 16. Assessment

Several points now command wide agreement among these writers. The poems come out of a long oral tradition: the formulaic diction, the mixed dialect, the digamma that sometimes counts and sometimes does not, and the mixture of objects from different centuries all prove it (Kirk, pp. 4–7; Lane Fox, ch. 7; Foley). The old Analyst project of cutting the *Iliad* into original lays failed, as Turner shows, because no two analysts could agree on the pieces; the inconsistencies they found are mostly explained by composition in performance (Fowler, p. 220). The poems were fixed in writing well before the sixth-century Athenian festival; Janko's linguistic sequence makes a single sixth-century redaction impossible (Fowler, p. 225). And Homer's status as the Greeks' one classic was itself made, mainly in the sixth century, by rhapsodes, festivals and schools (Burkert; Lamberton).

The real disagreements are about the moment of writing and the person behind it. Here the most persuasive single argument is Adam Parry's: oral transmission changes a poem too much, so whoever composed the text we have must have composed it at the moment it was written. Whether that person wrote with his own hand (Adam Parry, West), dictated it illiterate (Lord, Lane Fox), or fixed it gradually through premeditated performances (Fowler) cannot be settled. Lane Fox's argument from the uncorrected duals of *Iliad* 9 tells against a poet who revised his own written text, but Scodel's reading of the duals as deliberate, and West's evidence that the *Odyssey*'s poet left even worse inconsistencies in a written text, weaken it.

On the date, the eighth-century camp (Kirk, Powell, Lane Fox) and the seventh-century camp (Burkert, West) rest on different kinds of evidence. Lane Fox's arguments from silence — no hoplites, no Gyges, no Ischian eruption — and from Nestor's cup are strong but not conclusive, since a poet might archaise deliberately and the cup might allude to a story rather than a text. West's arguments are strongest for the *Odyssey*, where the knowledge of Naucratis and Cyrenaica is specific; they bear on the *Iliad* only through his view that the two poems had different authors.

Nagy's tradition-centred view and Porter's "idea" of Homer can look like answers to a different question, but they explain something the others leave unexplained: why every account of Homer, ancient or modern, tends to produce the poet it needs. Even Lane Fox, who insists Homer was "a 'he', not a long impersonal tradition, an 'it'" (ch. 6), fills the gaps with imagined walks to the crocus slopes of Gargaron and a dictation for his daughter, as he cheerfully admits.

### Conclusion

The Greeks had a poet called Homer and a set of poems they attributed to him, but no reliable facts about the man; the Lives, the birthplaces and the portraits were invented to fill that gap, and the doubts are as old as the inventions. Modern scholarship has settled what the poems are — the end product of a long oral tradition, fixed in writing at an early date, made into a classic in the sixth century — while remaining divided about who fixed them, when, where, and whether the same person made both. The scholars considered here range from an illiterate Ionian master dictating c. 750 (Lane Fox) to a writer revising for years c. 650 (West), from a single genius (Adam Parry) to "the genius of the overall poetic tradition" (Nagy). Porter's lesson is that the question survives because it cannot be answered: "Homer" is the name for that gap, and every generation has filled it with the poet it wanted.
