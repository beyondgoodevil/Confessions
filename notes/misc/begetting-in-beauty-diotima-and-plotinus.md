Plato's Diotima says something stranger than "we love beauty": love is **"of engendering and bringing to birth in the beautiful."** Beauty is not the goal but the **midwife**. Plotinus, six centuries later, turns this into a programme: beauty is the visible trace of **form**, and to see true Beauty you must **become beautiful yourself**. Together they give the classic account of why beauty matters morally. (Diotima's ladder and the debate over eros and agape are in [[Eros and Agape: Plato, Nygren, Dionysius and the Fathers]]; this note is about the begetting and about Plotinus.)

### 1. Love Is Not of the Beautiful

The argument runs in steps (*Symposium* 204d–207a):

1. Love is of **good things**, and of **possessing** them, and of possessing them **always**.
2. So what is love's work? "Their deed is bringing to birth in beauty both in terms of the body and in terms of the soul."
3. Why birth? Because a mortal can't hold anything forever. "Engendering is born forever and is immortal as far as that can happen to a mortal being." So "eros be of immortality too" (206e–207a).

> "All human beings, Socrates, conceive both in terms of the body and in terms of the soul, and whenever they are at a certain age, their nature desires to give birth; but it is incapable of giving birth in ugliness, but only in beauty." (206c)

**Beauty as midwife.** "Kallone [Beauty] is the Moira [Fate] and Eileithyia for birth": the goddesses who preside over childbirth. Near beauty, the pregnant one "becomes glad and in its rejoicing dissolves and then gives birth"; near ugliness it "contracts inwardly, turns away, shrinks up" and has a hard time of it (206d). Hence the punchline: "eros is not, Socrates, ... of the beautiful, as you believe," but "of engendering and bringing to birth in the beautiful" (206e).

### 2. Two Kinds of Children

- **Pregnant in body.** These turn to women and seek "immortality, remembrance, and happiness (as they believe)" through children (208e).
- **Pregnant in soul.** These conceive "prudence and the rest of virtue." All the poets and inventive craftsmen are such procreators, but "by far the greatest and most beautiful part of prudence" is ordering cities and households, "moderation and justice" (209a).
- **How it happens.** Meeting a beautiful body with "a beautiful, generous, and naturally gifted soul," the pregnant lover becomes "fluent in speeches about virtue" and "tries to educate him." They raise their offspring together and form a friendship firmer than parents', "because the children they share in common are more beautiful and more immortal" (209b–c).
- **Examples.** The poems of Homer and Hesiod, and the laws of Lycurgus and Solon, are children everyone would rather have than human ones (209d–e).

### 3. Reeve: Why Beauty, and Not Just the Good?

C. D. C. Reeve asks why the permanent possession of good things should consist in begetting **in beauty** specifically. His answer has three parts.

1. **Beauty is "incandescent."** In the *Phaedrus* beauty alone "could be seen blazing out." Of all the Forms, beauty alone "has acquired this privilege, of being most clearly visible and most lovable" (250b–e). The **Good**, by contrast, is "explanation-elusive": everyone wants it but nobody can say what it is. So beauty is the one good we can **just see** to be good. It is a reliable starting point on the road to the Good (Reeve, pp. 142–148).
2. **Persistence through becoming.** Diotima says even a single person survives only by renewal: hair, flesh, blood, habits, opinions and even each "piece of knowledge" are constantly lost and replaced, and remembering is "creating a new memory in place of the one that is leaving" (207d–208b). Reeve calls this "persistence through becoming." Self-love turns out to be a kind of begetting too, and the line between oneself and one's children (or one's pupils, laws and poems) softens (pp. 150–152).
3. **"In beauty" means "in conformity with beauty."** The beautiful thing provides the pattern the offspring copies. Achilles' courageous deeds incite him and give the template for the next ones and for the memory others keep of them. That's why begetting also requires **transmitting values** across generations, which is where laws and education come in (pp. 154, 159–161).

His provocative conclusion: if what we really are is our reason, then the philosopher talking about virtue to a beautiful pupil is having "real sex," and nothing in that requires the lover to be male (pp. 169–170).

### 4. Plotinus: Beauty Is Not Symmetry

*Ennead* I.6, "On Beauty," is the first treatise in Porphyry's chronological list. It starts by attacking the common (Stoic) view that beauty is "the symmetry of parts in relation to each other and to the whole added to fine coloration" (1.6.1):

1. **Simple things would be excluded.** "The light of the sun," gold, "lightning in the night and the stars" have no parts in proportion, yet they are beautiful.
2. **Parts must be beautiful too.** "Beauty is certainly not made up of ugly things."
3. **The same face** with the same symmetry is sometimes beautiful and sometimes not, so beauty is something other than the symmetry.
4. **Virtues and theories** have no proportions, yet they are beautiful. And bad theorems can agree with each other perfectly well.

**What beauty is.** Things are beautiful "by participation in Form." What is shapeless and unformed is ugly, "outside divine reason." Form comes to matter and "guides it into being a completed unity," and "beauty is, then, situated over that which is shaped" (1.6.2). The soul **recognizes** beauty as something "to which it has a kinship" and "is both delighted and thrilled and returns to itself and recollects itself"; ugliness it "rejects ... and recoils from" (1.6.2). (Compare Diotima's pregnant one contracting near ugliness.) Fire is the most beautiful body because it is closest to the incorporeal and "shines and glows as if it were form" (1.6.3).

### 5. The Beauty of Souls and the Mud

- **Higher beauties.** Virtues have a beauty that cannot be described to those who haven't seen it, just as colours can't be described to the blind. The right response to beauty is "astonishment, and sweet shock, and longing, and erotic thrill" (1.6.4). Plotinus keeps the erotic language right up to the top.
- **Ugliness is added, not native.** The ugly soul, licentious, cowardly and envious, is "as if someone fell into mud or slime and the beauty he had is no longer evident." His job is "to wash it off." The pure soul is like gold with the earth removed (1.6.5).
- **Virtue as purification.** Self-control, courage and wisdom are purifications, as "the ancient doctrine" says; the soul purified "becomes form ... and wholly divine." For god, "the Good and Beauty are identical" (1.6.6). (Plotinus also qualifies this at the end: the Good is "the source and principle" of Beauty, which belongs to the intelligible world, 1.6.9.)

### 6. The Ascent: Shut Your Eyes

1. **Don't chase reflections.** Bodily beauties are "images and traces and shadows." Whoever runs after them is like the man who tried to grasp "a beautiful reflection in water" and drowned. Plotinus means Narcissus, and the man sinks "with his soul" into Hades (1.6.8).
2. **Flee home.** "Let us flee to our beloved fatherland," as Odysseus left Circe and Calypso despite their pleasures. But not on foot, horse or ship: "just shut your eyes, and change your way of looking, and wake up. Everyone has this ability, but few use it" (1.6.8).
3. **Carve your own statue.** "Go back into yourself and look." If you don't yet see yourself as beautiful, act like a sculptor: remove the superfluous, straighten the crooked, brighten the dark, and "not stop 'working on your statue' until the divine splendour of virtue shines in you" (1.6.9).
4. **Become what you see.** "For no eye has ever seen the sun without becoming sun-like, nor could a soul ever see Beauty without becoming beautiful. You must first actually become wholly god-like and wholly beautiful if you intend to see god and Beauty" (1.6.9).

- **Significance:** Diotima's lover begets beauty **in others** (pupils, cities, poems). Plotinus turns the work **inward**: the first work of art is your own soul. But both tie beauty to transformation. Beauty is not something to own; it makes the one who loves it give birth or become something.

### 7. Murdoch and Konstan: Why This Matters

- **Murdoch.** Beauty is the first and most persistent experience of **transcendence**: "We cannot acquire and assimilate the beautiful." Falling in love rips "the centre of significance ... out of the self," and Plato's claim is that "a sense of beauty diminishes greed and egoism and directs the energy of the soul in the direction of the real and the good" (*The Fire and the Sun*, pp. 17–18).
- **Konstan.** Platonic beauty never stops being the **object of eros**, even at the top of the ladder. It is still seen, with "the mind's eye" (a phrase Plato may have coined). Plotinus' lyrical description of the vision is "the language of erotic desire." See [[Kalos and Kallos: Did the Greeks Have a Word for Beauty?]].

### 8. An Orthodox Assessment

- **What the Fathers kept.** Gregory of Nyssa took over Plotinus' image almost word for word: God paints his own likeness in us, not with colours but with "purity, freedom from passion, blessedness" (*On the Making of Man* 5, quoted by Konstan). Purification as washing off mud from the image matches the Fathers' teaching that sin is not our nature but a coating on the image of God. See [[Image and Likeness]].
- **"Become sun-like."** Plotinus' maxim has an Orthodox echo in the teaching that one sees the uncreated Light only by becoming light, by grace. The difference is that for the Fathers the likeness is a **gift** received in communion with a personal God, not just the soul's return to its own nature. See [[2 Peter 1:3-4: Theosis]] and [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]].
- **What changes.** Diotima's begetting is driven by mortality: we beget because we can't last. In Christ, immortality is given rather than produced, so the "children of the soul" (virtues, teaching, communities) are offered rather than clung to as our only survival. And the beloved is not a step to climb past: as [[Eros and Agape: Plato, Nygren, Dionysius and the Fathers]] argues, the top of the ladder is a Person.

### Conclusion

Diotima's surprise is that love is not of the beautiful but of **giving birth in it**: mortals possess the good forever only by producing something that lasts, whether children, poems, laws or virtue in a pupil, and beauty is the midwife that makes such birth possible. Reeve explains why beauty plays this role: it is the one good we can simply see. Plotinus deepens it. Beauty is not symmetry but form shining through matter. The soul recognizes it as kin, ugliness is mud to be washed off, and the way to see Beauty is to shut your eyes, carve your own statue and become sun-like. In both, beauty matters because it changes the one who loves it.
