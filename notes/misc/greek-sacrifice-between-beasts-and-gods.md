Greek cities, G. S. Kirk reminds us, "had no abattoirs; the slaughtering was done mainly in front of the temples. The priests were butchers." Zeus' holiest place at Olympia was a mound of ash from burnt offerings. Horns were piled high at Delphi and Delos. At the Panathenaia of 335 BC about fifty cattle were killed on the Acropolis, and the meat was carted down through the Agora to be shared out (Kirk, p. 227). Animal sacrifice was the central act of Greek religion and the main source of meat. This note asks what the Greeks thought they were doing. It sets **Jean-Pierre Vernant**'s structural reading against the "killing" theories of Walter Burkert and René Girard, with **Kirk**, **W. K. C. Guthrie** and **Richard Seaford** alongside.

### 1. The Scene at the Altar

Vernant describes the standard rite, the *thusia*, the sacrifice whose meat is eaten. A perfect animal, one never yoked, is garlanded and led to the altar to the sound of the flute. It is sprinkled with water and barley. Its spine is struck with an axe and its throat cut with the *machaira*. At that moment "the soul departs from the bones" (*Odyssey* 3.455), and the women raise their ritual cry, the *ololugē* (Vernant, *Mortals and Immortals*, p. 292). The thigh-bones wrapped in fat go up in smoke to the god. The entrails are roasted and shared first, then the rest of the meat is boiled and divided.

Guthrie sets this festive morning scene, "a pure white ox gaily garlanded... an air of festival", against the night-time rites for the powers below the earth. He lists the contrasts "with the proviso... [that] strict logic and the absolute rule have no more place here than in other phenomena of Greek or any religion":

| | Olympian | Chthonian |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *thuein* | *enagizein* |
| Victim's throat | turned upward | turned down, "so that blood may most easily soak into the earth" |
| Altar | high altar | low hearth or pit |
| Victim | white | black |
| Time | morning | evening or night |
| Hand in prayer | palm up | palm down |

Medea's instructions to Jason in Apollonius are his example. Jason is to dig a pit at midnight, kill a ewe-lamb, lay it "raw and whole" on the pyre for Hekate, and leave without looking back (Guthrie, pp. 221–22). The chthonian victim was burnt whole and not eaten. The Olympian victim was a meal.

### 2. Hesiod's Founding Myth: The Trick at Mekone

The only major Greek myth about sacrifice is Hesiod's. Kirk notes how strange this is: sacrifice is everywhere in ritual, but "the theme occurs so little in myths", with Mekone "the one prominent exception" (Kirk, p. 228). In Athanassakis' translation:

> When the gods and mortal men were settling their accounts at Mekone, Prometheus cheerfully took a great ox, carved it up, and set it before Zeus to trick his mind. He placed meat, entrails, and fat within a hide and covered them with the ox's tripe, but with guile he arranged the white bones of the ox, covered them with glistening fat, and laid them down as an offer. (*Theogony* 535–41)

Zeus "knew the guile and took note of it", chose the shining fat, and found the bones. "From that time on the tribes of mortal men on earth have burned the white bones for the gods on smoky altars" (556–57). In return Zeus withheld fire. Prometheus stole it in a hollow fennel stalk. Zeus then had the first woman made, "not something good but a mixture of good and bad" (585).

### 3. Kirk: Guilt, Butchery and the Lost Golden Age

Kirk calls the Mekone story "of fundamental importance": it shows, "more clearly than any other Greek instance, a myth that is working towards the resolution of a real problem". To treat it as a mere just-so story about why gods get the worst bits "is rather inadequate" (p. 138). His reading has four strands:

- **Economics.** "Meat was even rarer and more expensive than in Greece today and sacrifice was in one sense a by-product of the profession of butchery" (p. 139). Men had to keep the edible parts.
- **Logic.** The token offering made sense: only smoke and savour could rise to the gods, and fat gives the best smoke. Bones "put in good order" stood for the whole animal (p. 139).
- **Guilt.** A rational defence "might not in the end have been emotionally satisfying. It could easily fail to remove the guilt men felt over keeping the best parts of the animal for themselves. Guilt is a cardinal feeling" (p. 139). So the myth makes men pay: no fire, then woman, toil and disease. In Kirk's paraphrase of Zeus: "you will have to eat your appetizing portion of flesh and innards all raw. Just try that!"
- **The Golden Age.** "Not the least interesting implication of the myth is that the practice of making sacrifices is itself a relic of a Golden Age when gods and privileged mortals dined together" (p. 138). The quarrel over the portions "may be held to symbolize, in a way, the whole dilemma of the relation between men and gods" (p. 142).

He adds that Hesiod's text "vacillates" over whether Zeus saw through the trick, which suggests that different versions have been merged (p. 138).

### 4. Vernant: The Myth as a Map of the Human Condition

Vernant reads the *Theogony* and *Works and Days* versions as one interlocking story, and his analysis is the deepest available (*Myth and Society*, pp. 183–201). It is a duel of cunning (*mētis*) between Prometheus and Zeus. Every move is a concealed gift or a theft: the verbs are *kaluptein* and *kruptein* (to hide) and *kleptein* (to steal). At Mekone gods and men are still together, and the division of the ox is what makes them separate (p. 186). The outcome defines the human condition through three things:

1. **sacrifice**: flesh for men, bones for gods;
2. **Promethean fire**, which must be kept alive and fed, and which makes cooking possible;
3. **marriage**, through the woman who is Zeus' counter-gift (p. 188).

To these *Works and Days* adds **grain and labour**. The gods "hid" men's livelihood, so wheat that once grew of itself must now be ploughed and sown.

The key move is an equation. For the gods, "not to give = to hide a good thing so that it can only be obtained through the evils which envelop it", and "to give = to hide an evil under its beguiling appearance of something desirable" (p. 192). The images match with remarkable precision:

- The edible meat was hidden in the ugly stomach (*gastēr*), the bones under white fat.
- Pandora is a beautiful exterior, in white robe and veil, over a "bitch's mind".
- Pandora is herself a *gastēr*, a belly that devours what men produce and must be fed. "Is there anything more bitchy than the hateful belly?" asks Odysseus (*Odyssey* 7.216; Vernant, pp. 193–94).
- She is *anti puros*, the counterpart of fire, a fire that burns her husband without a brand.
- She is the furrow that must be ploughed, as the earth must be ploughed for grain (pp. 195–96).

The result is that man stands "midway between that of the beasts and that of the gods". He eats cooked meat according to rule, not raw flesh like the beasts nor nectar and ambrosia like the gods. He eats bread, whereas the gods are immortal "because they do not eat bread or drink wine" (*Iliad* 5.341–42). He marries instead of mating at random (pp. 198–99).

Vernant's reading of **Hope**, left in the jar, completes the picture. "Whoever is immortal, as the gods are, has no need of Elpis. Nor is there any Elpis for those who, like the beasts, are ignorant of their mortality." Hope belongs to a creature that knows it must die but not when. His closing sentence summarises the whole system: "Contact can only be made with the gods through sacrifice, which at the same time consecrates the impassable barrier between mortals and immortals; there can be no happiness without unhappiness, no birth without death, no abundance without toil, no Prometheus without Epimetheus—in a word, no Man without Pandora" (p. 201).

### 5. Is Killing the Core? Burkert, Girard and Vernant

The most influential modern theories put death at the centre. Burkert's *Homo Necans* ("man the killer") traces sacrifice to the hunt and to inherited aggression. Kirk notes that it links the rite to "the relentless but sometimes almost apologetic attitudes of primitive hunters to their prey" (Kirk, p. 227). Girard derives it from the founding murder of a scapegoat. Vernant observes that the two differ in method but "agree on the idea that the fundamental feature of the sacrificial rite is a scenario in which the victim is slain and that the victim's ritual murder constitutes the entire ceremony's center of gravity" (*Mortals*, p. 292). The older theory of Hubert and Mauss led to the same place. The victim dies in the sacrificer's stead, so that "the gods take the victim instead of the immolator", and "violence and murder are thus found at the core of sacrifice" (p. 293).

Vernant tests this against the evidence, and the evidence keeps refusing to show the killing:

- **The myth.** Hesiod has Prometheus bring a live ox and then divide it. "There is no account of butchery and slaughter" (p. 293).
- **The ritual.** It is built to deny violence. The animal walks without a leash and must give consent by a nod or a shiver. The knife is hidden in the basket under the barley. Vernant's definition: "the sum of procedures permitting the slaughter of an animal under such conditions that violence seems excluded and the slaying is unequivocally imbued with a characteristic that distinguishes it from murder" (p. 294).
- **The images.** In the vase paintings of sacrifice, "not one of them presents the slaying and death of the victim". Jean-Louis Durand: "The act which paves the way to death in the throat of the beasts is never represented." Yet the same painters show human sacrifice with brutal clarity: "One sees Polyxena's blood spurt, but not that of pigs and sheep" (pp. 294–95).

One could call all this ideology: Karl Meuli's "comedy of innocence", a cover-up of the real act (p. 295). Vernant refuses. The only Greeks who said sacrifice *was* murder were Pythagoreans and Orphics, who rejected it in order to reach a closeness to the gods that the city's religion denied. Their equation of sacrifice with murder "is no less 'ideological'" than the city's distinction between the two (pp. 296–97). Hence his conclusion: "If the act of slaying lies at the heart of the thusia, it resides there like a subversive threat that is repeatedly conjured away... It admits that we must slaughter animals in order to eat, yet at the same time it aims to banish acts of murder and savagery from what is human" (p. 301).

### 6. The Bouphonia: Putting the Axe on Trial

The Athenian Bouphonia ("ox-murder"), at the festival of Zeus Polieus, stages this paradox openly. Porphyry gives the founding story, after Theophrastus. A plough-ox ate the grain offering on the altar. A man named Sopatros killed it in a rage with an axe, buried it, and fled to Crete. Drought and famine followed. Delphi ordered the Athenians to "avenge the murder and resurrect the dead in the same sacrifice", and everyone was to taste the meat. Sopatros was given citizenship so that the whole city could share the killing. Then came a trial. The water-carriers blamed the men who sharpened the blades, they blamed the man with the axe, he blamed the throat-cutter, and he blamed the knife, "which, being without a voice, was condemned". It was thrown into the sea. The hide was stuffed with hay and the ox set up, yoked to the plough, "as if ready for work" (*Mortals*, pp. 301–2).

Kirk takes the usual view that the rite was meant "to appease man's feeling of guilt at slaughtering tame animals who are also his friends and helpers". He notes too that Aristophanes already found it old-fashioned and that it generated very little myth (Kirk, pp. 233–34). Vernant reads it as a myth of the city's foundation. The victim was a plough-ox, so close to the household that it normally stood outside sacrifice. By the end of the story everything has vanished: the murderer, now a citizen; the crime, since all ate "without any scruple"; the victim, standing again; and the weapon, at the bottom of the sea. The narrative moves from a confused pre-political state to Athens, "with its plowed land, town, acropolis, religious rites and priesthoods, communal feasts, law, and status of citizenship". The ox ends in its proper place, meat for men, bones for the god, and a worker in the fields (*Mortals*, pp. 298–301).

### 7. Other Forms: Sphagia, Holocaust and Human Sacrifice

Not every Greek sacrifice was a meal:

- **Battlefield *sphagia*.** Before battle the Spartans cut a goat's throat to Artemis Agrotera. This was a "slaughter of a victim by cutting its throat (*sphazein*, unlike *thuein* in the other rites)". The goat sits at the edge of domestication, and its blood foreshadows the warriors' (*Mortals*, p. 251). After Marathon the Athenians, having vowed a goat for every Persian killed, "were unable to find goats enough; so they resolved to offer five hundred every year" (Xenophon, *Anabasis* 3.2.12, quoted p. 247).
- **Holocausts.** At the Diasia "sacrificial animals [were] burnt whole", and only afterwards came "relief and feasting" (Kirk, p. 233). Chthonian victims were usually burnt whole.
- **Human sacrifice** belongs to myth, not cult, and myth treats it as monstrous. Lykaon served Zeus a child and became a wolf. Tantalus served his son Pelops. Each ended a Golden Age of shared meals with the gods (Kirk, pp. 238–40). Vernant draws the rule: "there is no human 'sacrifice' which is not also a deviant or corrupted sacrifice, a monstrous offering" (*Mortals*, p. 295).

### 8. Escaping the System: Pythagoreans, Orphics and Maenads

Because sacrifice fixed man's place, refusing or inverting it was a religious statement. Vernant's 1975 formulation: "Greek sacrifice differs from Vedic sacrifice in that the latter is a prototype for the act of creation... Much more modest, Greek sacrifice recalls Prometheus's act, which alienated man from the gods. In a ritual that seeks to join the mortal with the immortal it consecrates the unattainable distance that henceforth separates them" (*Mortals*, p. 297).

There were two exits:

- **From above.** The Pythagoreans and Orphics kept a meatless diet, treated killing an animal as killing kin, and sought purity. Some Pythagoreans graded the animals: cattle and sheep were too close to man to kill, while pigs and goats were far enough off to be sacrificed (pp. 298–99).
- **From below.** Dionysiac *ōmophagia* and *sparagmos*, tearing a hunted animal apart and eating it raw, reversed the rite.

Either exit could produce "a state of total communion that can be taken just as easily as a return to the tender familiarity of all creatures in the Golden Age or as a descent into the chaos and confusion of savagery" (p. 298).

Seaford adds caution and context. Maenads are shown "occasionally as eating raw flesh". "Eating raw flesh, which distinguishes animals from humans, assimilated the maenads of myth to animals." An inscription from Miletus of 276 BC "suggests that some meat may have been actually eaten raw in Dionysiac cult, albeit not necessarily with the savagery that it symbolised" (Seaford, p. 24). Dionysiac cult, he shows, could also *strengthen* the city's communal sacrifice. Its festivals were "for everybody", and an unofficial cult even honoured a god called Isodaitēs, "Equal divider in the feast" (pp. 28, 35–36). Through mystery-cult he links sacrifice to tragedy itself: the initiate "might undergo a fictional death (just like the tragic actor) expressed in the actual death of a sacrificial animal ('tragedy' derives its name from song at goat-sacrifice)" (pp. 89–90).

### 9. Assessment

**Vernant's structural reading is the strongest account of Greek sacrifice as Greeks understood it.** It explains features that the "killing" theories leave unexplained:

- the hidden knife, the required consent of the victim, and the absence of the death blow in art;
- the many fine links between sacrifice and cooking, agriculture and marriage;
- why refusing meat was a heresy and eating it raw a frenzy.

It also respects the evidence. Vernant declines to impose a hidden meaning "contrary to the one explicitly given to it by the Greeks" (*Mortals*, p. 295). On this point his refusal of the "comedy of innocence" is decisive. If myth, ritual and image all agree, then "suppression" is just a name for what the Greeks were consciously doing.

**But Burkert and Girard are not refuted, only relocated.** Vernant concedes that the slaying lies at the heart of the *thusia* "like a subversive threat that is repeatedly conjured away" (p. 301). That concession grants the theories their starting point. Something has to be conjured away. The elaborate machinery of consent, concealment and trial in the Bouphonia shows that the Greeks felt the killing as a problem, which is Kirk's point about guilt. Where Vernant is right is that the Greek *answer* to the problem was not catharsis of violence but classification: putting the act in its proper category and man in his proper place. Burkert's hunter and Girard's scapegoat are theories of origins. Vernant's is a theory of meaning. A full account probably needs both, as long as the theory of origins is not mistaken for what the participants believed.

**Kirk is shrewdest about the plain facts.** Sacrifice was butchery, meat was scarce, and men kept the good parts. The Mekone myth turns an economic necessity into a cosmic settlement and makes men pay for it in toil and mortality. His Golden Age reading fits with Vernant's. Sacrifice is the memory of a lost common table, and every sacrifice both recalls it and confirms that it is over. Vernant's comparison with Vedic sacrifice shows how unusual the Greek conception is. In Greece sacrifice is not a cosmic act of creation but a modest, almost legal, settlement of shares.

**Guthrie's Olympian/chthonian table is useful but too neat.** He offers it as what happens "either always or for the most part", and Vernant's *sphagia* show a third kind of rite that fits neither column. The categories describe tendencies, not rules.

### Conclusion

A Greek sacrifice did two things at once. It opened a channel to the gods, and it confirmed the distance that separated men from them. Hesiod's Prometheus myth makes this a single founding event at Mekone. A trick over the shares of an ox gave men meat and the gods smoke. It cost men the easy fire and the self-sown grain, and brought them woman, labour, disease and hope. Vernant shows that the rite set man between the beasts, who eat raw and mate at random, and the gods, who neither eat bread nor die. The ritual so carefully kept the act of killing out of sight that the knife itself could be tried and drowned. Kirk adds the hunger and the guilt underneath. Burkert and Girard ask where that guilt came from. Pythagoreans, Orphics and maenads, in their different ways, tried to escape the whole arrangement. For the city, to sacrifice was to be human in the Greek sense: neither beast nor god, a creature who shares a meal with the gods only by being reminded that it can no longer sit at their table.
