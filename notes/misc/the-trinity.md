The Holy Trinity is the mystery of one God in three Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The foundation of this divine reality is the Father, who is the eternal source (ἀρχή, principium) of the Son and the Holy Spirit. The Father eternally begets the Son and eternally causes the Holy Spirit to proceed.

To understand how the three Persons are one God, we must distinguish between nature (or essence) and person (or hypostasis). The divine nature refers to *what* God is—one, indivisible, and uncreated. Each of the three divine Persons fully and wholly possesses the one divine nature without division or composition. However, they are distinct in *who* they are, meaning their personal properties:

- The Father is unbegotten and the sole source (ἀρχή) of the Son and the Holy Spirit.
    - **The monarchy of the Father** is the centre of Gregory's doctrine (Beeley). The famous passage (*Or.* 29.2): Christians honour "monarchy—not a monarchy that is delimited by a single person... but one that is held together by an equal dignity of nature, a common accord of will, and an identity of action, and by the convergence to the One of the things that come from it". "Hence from the beginning a Monad, being moved toward a Dyad, stops at a Triad."
    - There is one God because the Son and Spirit "refer back" to the Father as one cause (*Or.* 20.7). "The union is the Father, from whom and to whom the order of the persons runs its course" (*Or.* 42.15). Ware's version: "there is one God because there is one Father."
    - Gregory's creed for Maximus (*Or.* 25.15–16): the Son is called "God" when named on His own, "Lord" when named with the Father ("the first on account of the nature, the second on account of the monarchy"). Don't put a source above the Father, "a first of the First". And the Son and Spirit are "not without source—and yet in a sense they are without source, which is a paradox": they have a cause, but no beginning in time.
    - Gregory would even call the Father "**greater**", except that his opponents assume a cause produces inferiors rather than equals (*Or.* 40.43). On John 14:28 he prefers the eternal reading: saying the Father is greater than the Son "considered as a human being is true, but trivial" (*Or.* 30.7). Augustine went the other way and read it of the Son's humanity.
    - But not "a perverse reverence for the monarchy", "contracting or truncating deity" (*Or.* 25.18).
    - Property (*idiotes*): **unbegottenness** (*agennesia*). He is the cause (*aitia*), without cause.
    - "Unbegotten" is not God's essence but the Father's property (Nazianzen, *Oration* 29, against the Eunomians).
- The Son is eternally begotten of the Father.
    - Property: **begottenness** (*gennesis*): from the Father, by generation. Begotten without time, without passion (*Or.* 29).
- The Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father.
    - Property: **procession** (*ekporeusis*): from the Father, by procession.
    - Ware: the Spirit is "God within us", the Son "God with us", the Father "God above or beyond us". The Spirit shows us the Son, the Son sends the Spirit. Distinguish the Spirit's **eternal procession** (from the Father alone) from His **temporal mission** (sent by the Son).
    - On the *filioque*: added to the Creed without the East's consent, and making the Son a source too "risks confusing the distinctive characteristics of the persons" (Ware).
    - The objection Gregory faces in *Or.* 31.7–9 is that a Spirit from the Father would be a second Son. Beeley notes that Augustine, though firmly Nicene, shared that assumption: that the difference between begetting and procession doesn't by itself stop the Spirit being a second Son.

This distinction of Persons does not divide the divine nature, because each Person fully possesses the one, undivided divine essence. Their unity is not an abstract unity of essence but a real communion of life and love, rooted in the Father as the unoriginate source. Thus, God is one because there is only one divine nature, yet He is three because there are three distinct hypostases who exist in an eternal communion of love and will.

Nazianzen: "No sooner do I conceive of the One than I am illumined by the splendour of the Three; no sooner do I distinguish them than I am carried back to the One" (*Oration* 40.41).

- Everything else (goodness, power, will, energy) is **common** to all three. So there is one will and one operation of the Trinity: every act of God toward creation is "from the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit" (Gregory of Nyssa, *To Ablabius*).
- What's the difference between generation and procession? Nazianzen: "You tell me what the unbegottenness of the Father is, and I will explain the generation of the Son and the procession of the Spirit, and we shall both be struck mad for prying into the mystery of God" (*Oration* 31.8).
    - God's **nature is unknowable**: Moses on Sinai saw only God's "back parts" (Exod 33:23) (*Or.* 28).
    - **Who** may theologise: not everyone, not always. Purify yourself first (*Or.* 27).
- Ware's way in: a **person** isn't an **individual**. An isolated unit is only "a bare unit as recorded in the census"; we become persons in relation. Love likewise "presupposes the other": "Hell is not other people; hell is myself, cut off from others". So God is "not just a unit but a union, not just unity but community". Motto: *Amo ergo sum*, "I love, therefore I am".
- So think of God dynamically: images of wind, running water, flame. Abba Joseph of Panepho held up his hands and his fingers became "as ten blazing torches": "If you wish, you can become completely as a flame." The three persons "are completely as a flame".
- **Three humans vs. the Three**: Peter, James and John share a nature but each has his own will and energy; they're three men. Father, Son and Spirit have "only one will and not three, only one energy and not three"; "None of the three ever acts separately". Each has "not one third of the Godhead, but the entire Godhead".
- **Why three, and why Father/Son?** Ware: no logical proof. The symbols are "given", not chosen. Aphrahat and Julian of Norwich use mother language as exceptions; but to pray "Our Mother" would be "replacing Christianity with a new kind of religion".
- **The two hands of God** (Irenaeus): the Father always acts through the Son and the Spirit together, and Ware runs through five places it shows:
    1. creation: "By the word of the LORD were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth" (Ps 33:6)
    2. the Annunciation, which also needed Mary's free consent
    3. the Baptism in the Jordan ("the worship of the Trinity was made manifest")
    4. the Transfiguration (the Spirit as the cloud of light)
    5. the Eucharistic **epiclesis**: the Father sends the Spirit to make the Son present in the gifts

## Terms

| Greek | Latin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *ousia* | *essentia*, *substantia* | essence: **what** God is. One |
| *physis* | *natura* | nature: used as near-synonym of ousia |
| *hypostasis* | *persona* (confusingly, *substantia* in older Latin) | a concrete existent: **who**. Three |
| *prosopon* | *persona* | person, face. Equated with hypostasis by the Cappadocians |
| *homoousios* | *consubstantialis* | of the same essence (Nicaea, 325) |
| *perichoresis* | *circumincessio* | mutual indwelling: "I am in the Father, and the Father in me" (John 14:11) |
| *monarchia* | | the Father as the one source |

Before the Cappadocians, *ousia* and *hypostasis* were near-synonyms (Nicaea's anathemas use them interchangeably), which caused years of confusion between East and West. Basil fixed the vocabulary: **one ousia, three hypostases**.

- Beeley's qualification: the tidy formula "one ousia, three hypostases" as such first appears in **Augustine**, and calling it "the Cappadocian achievement" overgeneralises about three different thinkers. In Gregory it's secondary: a way of clarifying the life of the Trinity flowing from the Father, not the foundation.
- Beeley also argues against Lossky's charge that Gregory is muddled about whether we see the Father in Christ by the Spirit or see the whole Trinity. For Gregory both are true: the Trinity is known within the economy, and the knowledge leads deeper into it.

## Analogies (and Why They Fail)

| Analogy | What it gets right | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|
| sun, ray, light | one light, distinct aspects | the ray and light aren't persons; tends to modalism |
| spring, river, sea | one water, source and flow | the parts are divided; tends to tritheism or subordinationism |
| Adam, Eve, Abel (one human nature, three persons) | the Cappadocian favourite: one nature, three hypostases | three humans are three separate beings; God's three are one God (no separation, one will, one act) |
| mind, word, spirit (Nazianzen, Palamas; Augustine's memory, understanding, will) | images of the Trinity in the soul. See [[Nous]] | these aren't hypostases |
| water, ice, steam | | **modalism**: one thing in three states, not three persons at once |
| shamrock | | three parts: **partialism** |

Nazianzen's conclusion: "I have thought of every image I could, and found none to rest on... It is best to keep to the few words, taking the Spirit as guide" (*Or.* 31.31-33).

Ware's favourite image is **three torches burning with a single flame**, but "the least misleading ikon is to be found... in the human heart": loving someone and knowing the love is returned.

## Heresies to Avoid

- **Modalism** (Sabellius): one person wearing three masks.
- **Tritheism**: three gods.
- **Subordinationism / Arianism**: the Son and Spirit are lesser. See [[Council of Nicea: Arianism]].
    - The **Eunomians** said God's essence is "unbegottenness", so the begotten Son is a different essence. St. Gregory the Theologian's *Five Theological Orations* (380) were preached in Constantinople against them and the Pneumatomachians; they're why he's called "the Theologian".
    - The **Bible verses** Arians used ("the Father is greater than I", John 14:28; "the Lord created me", Prov 8:22) are answered by referring the lowly things to the Incarnation and the high things to the divinity (*Or.* 30).
- **Pneumatomachianism**: the Spirit is a creature.
    - Against it: the **Spirit** is God; revelation is gradual (OT the Father, NT the Son, now the Spirit dwells among us) (*Or.* 31).

## Why It Matters

*Mine.*

- **God is love** (1 John 4:8) only makes sense if God is Trinity: love needs a beloved, eternally. A unipersonal God would need creation in order to love.
- **Personhood**: being a person is grounded in God's own life. Lossky: the Trinity is "our social programme"; Zizioulas builds a whole ontology on it. See [[Being as Communion]].
- **Prayer and worship** are Trinitarian: to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit (Eph 2:18).
    - Ware: daily Orthodox prayers are Trinitarian from the first sign of the Cross: the Trisagion ("Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal"), the Gloria, "Most Holy Trinity, have mercy upon us". Even the Jesus Prayer names the Son and implies the Father and the Spirit (1 Cor 12:3). "We think the Trinity, speak the Trinity, breathe the Trinity."
    - Before the Creed in the Liturgy: "Let us love one another, so that with one mind we may confess Father, Son and Holy Spirit". Note the "so that": mutual love comes before confessing the Trinity.
    - **Living the Trinity**: St Sergius dedicated his monastery to the Trinity so the monks would love each other as the three Persons do. Every family, parish and workplace is meant to be an icon of the Trinity, and for Ware that includes the struggle against oppression and injustice. Chrysostom: "to seek what is for the benefit of all" is the "highest summit" of Christianity.
    - Gregory too: the best summary of his doctrine is his congregation in Constantinople, "ruled by each other, by us, and by the Trinity" (*Or.* 42.15). Teaching about the Trinity should be done "like the fishermen's Gospel, not Aristotelian philosophy" (*Or.* 23.12).
- **Theosis** is participation in the life of the Trinity, not absorption into an essence.
- Lossky's line (from Florensky): "Between the Trinity and hell there lies no other choice."
