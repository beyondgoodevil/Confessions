> "I will go unto the altar of God, unto God my exceeding joy." (Ps 43:4)

> "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." (John 1:14)

*Ulysses* begins with a man on a roof pretending to say Mass and ends with a woman in bed saying "Yes". In between, two Dubliners walk around the city on one ordinary day, 16 June 1904, the day Joyce first walked out with Nora Barnacle. The title points to Homer. Joyce gave each of the eighteen chapters the name of an *Odyssey* episode in his private schemata, then took the names out of the book.

## 1. What Joyce Said He Was Doing

- To Carlo Linati (1920): "It is the epic of two races (Israel–Ireland) and at the same time the cycle of the human body as well as a little story of a day (life)… It is also a kind of encyclopaedia." He meant "to render the myth *sub specie temporis nostri*" and to let each episode "condition and even create its own technique".
- **Three parts**, as in the *Odyssey*:
  - the **Telemachiad** (1–3): Stephen, the son;
  - the **wanderings** (4–15): Bloom;
  - the **Nostos**, the homecoming (16–18).
- Each episode also has an hour, an organ of the body, an art and a technique.
- Eliot praised the Homeric parallel as a "mythic method" for ordering modern chaos. Pound called it "mere mechanics… Joyce had to have a shape on which to order his chaos." Flynn's introduction argues it does neither: it raises questions. What do Odysseus' *polytropos* ("many turns") and his courage look like in a modern city?
- **The great reversal**: Odysseus regains his house by killing the 108 suitors. Bloom comes home to a bed his wife has shared with Blazes Boylan that afternoon and meets it with "Envy, jealousy, abnegation, equanimity."

## 2. Stephen: The Son Without a Father (episodes 1–3, 9)

**"Telemachus"**
- Buck Mulligan comes up the stairs of the Martello tower "bearing a bowl of lather on which a mirror and a razor lay crossed" and intones "*Introibo ad altare Dei*", the opening of the Latin Mass (Ps 43:4). The lather is "the genuine Christine: body and soul and blood and ouns." Flynn reads this as the epic invocation of the Muse, turned into blasphemy.
- Stephen's first silent word is "**Chrysostomos**", golden-mouthed, for Mulligan's gold-filled teeth. Even in revolt his mind thinks in the Fathers' language.
- He is in mourning. His mother is dead, and he refused to kneel and pray at her deathbed. Mulligan: "beastly dead". Stephen's word for his guilt is "**Agenbite of inwit**", the Middle English for "remorse of conscience" and the title of a 14th-century moral manual (*Ayenbite of Inwyt*). It "keeps biting back" all day.
- "**I am the servant of two masters**… an English and an Italian… The imperial British state… and the holy Roman catholic and apostolic church. And a third… who wants me for odd jobs."
- The old milkwoman is Mother Ireland, and also a disguised Athena come to send Telemachus out.

**"Nestor"**: to Deasy, who says "All history moves towards one great goal, the manifestation of God", Stephen answers: "History is a nightmare from which I am trying to awake."

**"Proteus"**: alone on Sandymount Strand: "Ineluctable modality of the visible", Aristotle's *De Anima* on colour, set against Berkeley's "signs on a white field". It ends in a chain of metamorphoses: "God becomes man becomes fish becomes barnacle goose becomes featherbed mountain." Like Menelaus wrestling Proteus, the reader must hold on to a shape-shifter.

**"Scylla and Charybdis": Hamlet and the heresies**
- In the National Library Stephen gives his Shakespeare theory. Shakespeare played the **Ghost**, King Hamlet, speaking "I am thy father's spirit" to his own dead son Hamnet. He was a cuckold (the "secondbest bed" left to Anne Hathaway), and the villains he named Richard betray a brother who seduced her. Mulligan had mocked it: "He proves by algebra that Hamlet's grandson is Shakespeare's grandfather and that he himself is the ghost of his own father."
- Underneath is a theology of fatherhood. "Paternity may be a legal fiction." Real fatherhood is a "mystical estate, an apostolic succession, from only begetter to only begotten". "*Amor matris*", the love of and for a mother, "subjective and objective genitive, may be the only true thing in life", a thought he first has in "Nestor" while helping a schoolboy with his sums.
- He runs through the Trinitarian heresies as images of the artist: **Arius** and consubstantiality, Valentinus, and "**Sabellius, the African**", who held "that the Father was Himself His Own Son". The artist creates himself as father and son together.
- This touches the *Odyssey* itself. Telemachus, asked if he is Odysseus' son, says: "My Mother tells me it's so – how can I know it myself? No one is ever sure of his bloodline" (*Od.* 1, McCrorie).

## 3. Bloom: Odysseus Without a Sword (episodes 4–15)

**Who he is**
- Leopold Bloom is an advertising canvasser, son of a Hungarian Jewish immigrant (Rudolph Virag) who killed himself. He is baptised, outside every Dublin tribe, and has a dozen names ("Poldy, Henry Flower… Bloowho… Bloom Elijah"). His son Rudy died at eleven days old.
- He is the opposite of Stephen: "practical rather than intellectual, sensual rather than spiritual, husband and father… flexible rather than intransigent". Joyce told Budgen: "Stephen no longer interests me… He has a shape that can't be changed."

**"Hades"**: the *katabasis*
- Bloom rides in the coach to Glasnevin for Paddy Dignam (Elpenor, who died drunk), past O'Connell's statue and "Foundation stone for Parnell. Breakdown. Heart." Joyce maps Dante's rivers onto the Dodder, the Grand Canal, the Liffey and the Royal Canal.
- **Nussbaum's essay**: the chapter is a lesson in how to face death without **projective disgust**. Bloom fears death, thinks frankly about corpses, gas and worms, but does not project the disgust onto others. Meanwhile his companions quietly needle "the Jew". She had earlier praised Whitman's love of the body. Now she thinks Bloom is wiser: "avoid disgust… while disliking and even fearing death." She also says Joyce "turn[s] the Platonic ladder of love upside down". He ends the chapter with "Warm beds: warm fullblooded life."

**"Cyclops"**: the citizen
- In Barney Kiernan's pub the one-eyed "citizen" (based on Michael Cusack of the Gaelic Athletic Association) holds forth with his dog Garryowen. Thirty-five parodic "gigantist" interruptions inflate Irish legend. A catalogue of "Irish heroes" includes Columbus, Dante, Muhammad, "Brian Confucius", Adam and Eve.
- They talk of hanging. Emmet's execution is narrated as a society wedding. Bloom, against capital punishment and violence:
  > "Force, hatred, history, all that. That's not life for men and women, insult and hatred. And everybody knows that it's the very opposite of that that is really life… Love… I mean the opposite of hatred."
- They jeer: "A new apostle to the gentiles… Universal love." He is in fact on an errand of charity, arranging the Dignam widow's insurance.
- Driven out, he turns: "Mendelssohn was a jew and Karl Marx and Mercadante and Spinoza. And the Saviour was a jew and his father was a jew… **Christ was a jew like me.**" The citizen, defending his God by blaspheming Him, hurls a biscuit tin. The chapter ends with Bloom ascending in a chariot like **Elijah**.

**"Circe"**: Nighttown
- In Bella Cohen's brothel, set out as a play, Bloom's guilts and desires come alive. He is crowned, tried, burnt, turned into a woman and a farmyard of animals. This is Homer's Circe turning men to swine. Bloom has a potato as his *moly*, the protecting herb, and surrenders it.
- At the end, after Stephen is knocked down by British soldiers, Bloom stands guard over him and sees **Rudy**, his dead son, as an Eton schoolboy of eleven. The father finds a son, for a moment, in a vision.

## 4. Homecoming (episodes 16–18)

**"Ithaca"**
- The episode is written as a catechism of questions and answers: "What parallel courses did Bloom and Stephen follow returning?" Bloom makes Epps's cocoa, a "massproduct" that answers the opening Mass. Stephen declines to stay the night and leaves to the "double reverberation of retreating feet on the heavenborn earth."
- At the last minute Joyce added a list giving each episode of the day a Hebrew ritual name. Breakfast is "burnt offering", Glasnevin "rite of Samuel", the pub "holocaust", Nighttown "Armageddon", and the walk home "**atonement**", at-one-ment.
- There are no recognition scenes. Bloom's bee-sting "cicatrice" barely corresponds to Odysseus' scar, which Eurykleia recognised (*Od.* 19). The trial of the bow becomes a competition in urinating.
- The great Homeric symbol is the **bed**. Odysseus built his around a living olive tree, and that immovable bed is the secret by which Penelope knows him (*Od.* 23, "The Olive-Tree Bedpost"). Bloom's bed has been used by another man, and his furniture has been moved.

**"Penelope"**
- Eight unpunctuated sentences of Molly's night thoughts, beginning "Yes because he never did a thing like that before as ask to get his breakfast in bed" and ending in a memory of Bloom's proposal on Howth: "and yes I said yes I will Yes."
- Flynn's essay reads her through **Spinoza**, *Deus sive Natura*. Bloom had told her "you have no soul only grey matter". Her freedom is "joyful expression of her nature": "what else were we given all those desires for Id like to know." She doubts the Church but prays; she commits adultery but stays.

## 6. Assessment

*Mine.*

**A book that cannot stop being liturgical**
- Joyce left the Church, and Stephen says *non serviam* to it. Yet the book is shaped by the Mass from beginning to end. It opens with *Introibo*, gives the day's episodes sacrificial names, and closes with an "atonement". Stephen's brain is full of Arius, Sabellius, Chrysostom and Aquinas. The point of Mulligan's parody is that he can only mock the one thing that still means something: "turning the word into flesh", as Flynn puts it. Even the hostile modern novel needs the Eucharist as its shape. Schmemann's thesis was that secularism is not the absence of liturgy but its parody. See [[For the Life of the World]] and [[The Divine Liturgy Explained]].
- Stephen's theology of fatherhood gets the heresies exactly backwards for his purpose. "The Father was Himself His Own Son" (Sabellius) is the dream of the self-made artist, who needs no one before him. The Church condemned it because it abolishes real relation: if the Father is the Son, no one is ever *given* anything. The Orthodox doctrine of the **monarchy of the Father** is that the Son is begotten, really other, really loved. That is the answer to "paternity may be a legal fiction". Fatherhood is not a fiction because it is first an eternal relation in God, "of whom the whole family in heaven and earth is named" (Eph 3:15). See [[Monarchical Trinitarianism]] and [[Council of Nicea: Arianism]].

**"Agenbite of inwit": remorse without repentance**
- Stephen's tragedy is a very Orthodox one. He has *remorse* (*agenbite*) all day but no *metanoia*. He refused to kneel for his dying mother, and the refusal rules him. That is Judas' sorrow, not Peter's. In Auerbach's chapter, Peter's denial ends in tears and return. Stephen's ends in "Proteus", turning forever on the strand. "Godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation… but the sorrow of the world worketh death" (2 Cor 7:10). See [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]] and [[Auerbach's Mimesis: Odysseus' Scar, Peter's Denial and Figural Realism]].
- "History is a nightmare" against Deasy's "manifestation of God" is a real dilemma, and both answers are wrong. History is not a smooth march to God, nor a nightmare to escape. It is the place where God enters: crucified under Pontius Pilate, risen on a datable day. See [[Augustine's City of God]].

**Bloom: closer to the Gospel than his persecutors**
- The irony of "Cyclops" is the Gospel's own irony. The baptised Jew does the work of mercy for a widow, refuses hatred and says "Christ was a jew like me". The pious nationalist, swearing by the Christ he defends, throws a tin at him. That is the parable of the Good Samaritan set in Dublin (Luke 10:30–37). Bloom's "love… the opposite of hatred" is a Christian sentence spoken by an outsider to people who had forgotten it. See [[Girard's Scapegoat Mechanism: Sacrifice, Myth and the Gospels]]: the crowd needs its victim, and the "apostle to the gentiles" jeer is accurate.
- But Bloom's love has no *source* beyond his own decency, and his meekness slides into resignation. Odysseus' slaughter is not the Christian answer, but neither is "equanimity". The Gospel's answer to betrayal is **forgiveness** that names the wrong, and **marriage** as a bond to be healed, not merely tolerated. Bloom's "abnegation" is the natural virtue Lewis describes in *The Four Loves*: real, and moving, but needing to be "taken up" into charity. See [[Eros and Agape: Plato, Nygren, Dionysius and the Fathers]].

**Molly's "Yes" and Mary's**
- The book ends on the most famous "Yes" in English fiction: an affirmation of nature, desire and memory, read through Spinoza's God-or-Nature. Its Christian counterpart is the "Yes" Joyce knew better than anyone: "Be it unto me according to thy word" (Luke 1:38). Molly's yes is to her own nature ("what else were we given all those desires for"). Mary's is to God's word, and through it nature is not denied but made the place where God takes flesh. Joyce puts the yes at the end; the Gospel puts it at the beginning. See [[Mary's Consent to the Angel Gabriel]].
- Nussbaum's reading is right that Joyce "turns the Platonic ladder upside down" and refuses disgust at the body. Christianity did this first, and more radically. The Word became *flesh*, the body is raised, and relics are kissed. Disgust at the body is Gnostic, not Christian. Bloom at the grave, unafraid of corpses yet loving life, is close to the Orthodox funeral, which kisses the dead in the open coffin and sings "I weep and wail when I think upon death". See [[The Fall of Humanity and Its Consequences]] and [[Personal Identity and the Resurrection]].

**What I take from it**
- *Ulysses* is a cathedral built by a man who left the Church, and you can still see the old floor plan. Read it with the *Odyssey* open, and keep asking the question Joyce wanted us to ask: what do courage, homecoming and fatherhood look like now? The Christian answer is not Odysseus' bow or Bloom's shrug, but the Father running to meet the son "when he was yet a great way off" (Luke 15:20).
