In 1959 a small group of anthropologists met at Burg Wartenstein, the Wenner-Gren Foundation's castle in Austria, to talk about honour and shame. Most of them met again in Athens in 1961 and 1963. The result, edited by the Cypriot anthropologist J. G. Peristiany, was *Honour and Shame: The Values of Mediterranean Society* (1965). The volume made "honour and shame" a standard way of describing the moral life of southern Europe, North Africa and the Levant, and later critics spent decades arguing with it. It has seven essays: a theoretical and Andalusian study by Julian Pitt-Rivers, a history of Spanish honour by Julio Caro Baroja, and four ethnographies (Greek shepherds, a Cypriot village, Algerian Kabyles and Egyptian Bedouin). This note goes through each essay in turn. It then sets beside them Maurice Keen's account of how medieval chivalry argued over whether nobility came from blood or from virtue. That was the same tension between honour as precedence and honour as virtue that Pitt-Rivers puts at the centre of his theory. Page references are to these two books. Related Greek material is in [[Xenia: The Homeric Hospitality Scene]] and [[The Agonal Greek: Burckhardt, Pindar and the Contest]].

### 1. The Volume and Its Thesis

Peristiany's introduction starts with a definition. "Honour and shame are social evaluations and thus participate of the nature of social sanctions, the more monolithic the jury, the more trenchant the judgement." They are "two poles of an evaluation", "the reflection of the social personality in the mirror of social ideals" (p. 9). Honour "is at the apex of the pyramid of temporal social values and it conditions their hierarchical order"; it divides people into those who have it and those who do not (p. 10). He calls these values "temporal" because every society also knows a higher ideal: "saintliness is above honour and … there is nothing above saintliness" (p. 18 n. 4).

He also gives a warning. When a Greek Cypriot, a Bedouin and a Berber, provoked, all answer "I also have a moustache", the shared phrase does not prove their cultures are related (p. 9). Every society has some form of honour, so the question is why some talk about it so constantly: "Do they protest too much?" (p. 10). His answer became the volume's thesis:

> Honour and shame are the constant preoccupation of individuals in small scale, exclusive societies where face to face personal, as opposed to anonymous, relations are of paramount importance and where the social personality of the actor is as significant as his office. (p. 11)

Inside the family or clan, roles are fixed and do not compete. Outside it, rank in honour is insecure. Even inherited honour must be asserted and defended, and this means accepting "the all-powerfulness of public opinion rather than that of a hierarchical superior". The individual stands for his group, "constantly 'on show'", in a "world where nothing is accepted on credit" (p. 11). Peristiany contrasts this with modern Western youth, who belong to so many groups that they no longer know who their peers are. A Greek chorus would have found those questions riddles (p. 12).

### 2. Pitt-Rivers: The Structure of Honour

Pitt-Rivers wrote the volume's longest essay, and the most often quoted. He compares honour to magic: its principles can be found everywhere, but local forms differ, and it "validates itself by an appeal to the facts (on which it imposes its own interpretations)" (p. 19). His definition is the classic one:

> Honour is the value of a person in his own eyes, but also in the eyes of his society. It is his estimation of his own worth, his claim to pride, but it is also the acknowledgement of that claim, his excellence recognized by society, his right to pride. (p. 21)

Honour therefore has two sides, the claim and its recognition. "The right to pride is the right to status." An English girl who says she is "not that kind of a girl" is talking about her honour, and Calderón's heroes say *Soy quién soy*, "I am who I am". A claim that others do not grant is only vanity. Where everyone agrees, the sequence is simple: "Honour felt becomes honour claimed and honour claimed becomes honour paid" (p. 22). In a complex society, though, groups disagree about who deserves what.

Pitt-Rivers sees two poles. One is honour as conduct ("All is lost save honour"). The other is honour as precedence, the titles "piled by the usurper upon the traitors who helped him to power" (p. 23). Honour as precedence is competitive. Hobbes ignored the moralists and treated honour as a ranking, "the pecking-order theory of honour" (p. 23). The winner gains what the loser loses, "as true on the street-corner as in the lists". Churchmen from the sixteenth century on insisted that true honour lay in virtue, but they never convinced the people who competed for it. A dangerous man gets precedence over a virtuous one so long as no one dares challenge him: "On the field of honour might is right" (pp. 24–25).

Several features follow from this structure.

- **Honour lives in the body.** Honour is conferred and taken away through the head: crowning, bowing, uncovering, the touch on the head with which Oxford confers degrees, and beheading. "Decapitation recognized that there was something worth chopping off" (p. 25). These acts are not symbols of a moral state but "transactions of honour — not the bill of goods, but the goods themselves" (p. 26).
- **Resentment is the test.** To leave an affront unavenged is cowardice, hence the aristocratic motto *nemo me impune lacessit* (p. 26). Public opinion is "the court of reputation", and "public ridicule kills" (p. 27). An ambiguous affront, like the thumb-biting in *Romeo and Juliet*, puts the victim in a bind: react and look quarrelsome, or ignore it and look a coward. "The victim of an affront is dishonoured only at the point where he is forced to recognize that he has been" (p. 28).
- **Only equals can dishonour each other.** The Chevalier de Rohan had his servants beat Voltaire instead of fighting him, and lost no honour among his peers. "A man is answerable for his honour only to his social equals, that is to say, to those with whom he can conceptually compete" (p. 31).
- **The state is honour's rival.** The duel was an ordeal that bypassed the king. So the sovereign is "the 'fount of honour' in one sense", but "also the enemy of honour in another, since he claims to arbitrate in regard to it" (p. 30). Going to court means admitting publicly that one has been wronged. Street gangs and aristocrats both look down on legality, the first because they stand outside the law and the second because they stand above it (p. 31).
- **The lie.** It is honourable to deceive and a grave affront to be called a liar. King Ferdinand boasted of the nine times he had deceived the King of France, and Tirso's Don Juan dies declaring himself a man of honour. Citing Ernestine Friedl's work on a Greek village, Pitt-Rivers notes that Greek peasants think a deceiving lie quite honourable. He resolves the paradox this way: "while to lie in order to deceive is quite honourable, to be called a liar in public is a grave affront". A man owes the truth only to those he respects, so deceiving someone humiliates him, while the charge of lying accuses the liar of having been afraid to give the affront openly (pp. 32–34).

### 3. How Honour Turns "Is" into "Ought"

The first half of Pitt-Rivers's essay ends with its most ambitious claim. Honour as precedence and honour as virtue are different things, yet Europe's languages use one word for both. The reason is that they merge in the person who has honour: "no man of honour ever admits that his honour=precedence is not synonymous with his honour=virtue". Honour also protects itself: "just as capital assures credit, so the possession of honour guarantees against dishonour". The king cannot be dishonoured, because what he *is* guarantees how his actions are judged. Pitt-Rivers calls this "the principle of *Honi soit qui mal y pense*", after the Garter motto (p. 37). This lets honour do

> something which the philosophers say they cannot do: derive an ought from an is; whatever is becomes right, the de facto is made de jure, the victor is crowned with laurels, the war-profiteer is knighted, the tyrant becomes the monarch, the bully, a chief. (p. 38)

The confusion of the two meanings serves "the function of social integration by ensuring the legitimation of established power" (p. 38). It works only while nobody notices it. When people do notice, the result is the long quarrel between Church and aristocracy, and between Montesquieu, who made honour the principle of monarchy, and Voltaire, who answered that there is least honour at court. Pitt-Rivers reads this as the realist's "is" against the moralist's "ought" (pp. 23, 36, 72). His closing sentence makes honour "the clearing-house for the conflicts in the social structure, the conciliatory nexus between the sacred and the secular, between the individual and society and between systems of ideology and systems of action" (p. 73).

### 4. Shame, Sex and Class in an Andalusian Town

The second half of the essay applies this theory to a small town in the Sierra de Cádiz of a few thousand people, the setting of Pitt-Rivers's *The People of the Sierra*. There honour is discussed without embarrassment, and a good name is a practical asset (p. 39). Below the moral line are the *sinvergüenzas*, the shameless: petty thieves, beggars, promiscuous women and, in local opinion, gypsies (pp. 40–41). Manuel "el Conde", a small, ugly agricultural expert, taps his chest and says he has "that which is worth more than fortune, my honour". His favourite pun on *perdiz*, "partridge" (*la perdí*, "I lost it"), alludes to the shame he has *not* lost. In such talk honour and shame are synonyms, and once lost they cannot be recovered (p. 41).

*Vergüenza* is "a concern for repute, both as a sentiment and also as the public recognition of that sentiment". Where honour and shame overlap they make up virtue, but they divide by sex. "A woman is dishonoured, loses her vergüenza, with the tainting of her sexual purity, but a man does not." Honour "obliges a man to defend his honour and that of his family, a woman to conserve her purity" (p. 42). Outside the overlap, shame becomes a feminine shyness and honour becomes a masculine willingness to give offence. Each is disgraceful in the wrong sex. Shame also has a passive meaning: to be "given shame" is to be humiliated. "Just as honour is at the same time honour felt, honour claimed and honour paid, so vergüenza is dishonour imposed, accepted and finally felt" (p. 43).

The male ideal is *hombría*, manliness. Its physical basis is crudely named (*cojones*), and its opposite is *manso*, which means both tame and castrated. A man who stays chaste is suspected of lacking manliness, so male sexual purity is left out of the popular idea of male honour. "The honour of a man is involved therefore in the sexual purity of his mother, wife and daughters, and sisters, not in his own" (p. 45). This explains the cuckold. His horns are the Devil's insignia, and he, not the adulterer, is mocked, because he failed in his duty to defend. "The pander, not the libertine, is the prototype of male dishonour." Pitt-Rivers compares the Nuer, among whom the wronged husband, not the adulterer, falls ill, and reads the horns as "not a punishment but a state of desecration" (pp. 46–47). The town's sanctions are ridicule (*burla*), nicknames, Carnival songs and the *vito*, a charivari with cowhorns and bells. Oddly, the vito was aimed mostly at husbands who had left their wives, not at cuckolds (pp. 47–49).

Social class changes the code. The pueblo sees itself as a community of equals: everyone uses the familiar form of address and "first come, first served". When a statue of Our Lady of Fatima visited the town and church seats had to be ranked, quarrels followed (pp. 53–55). Honour as precedence "increases with social status" (p. 55). Patronage turns money into honour: "Beneficence therefore transforms economic power into honour" (p. 58). Bargaining requires lies ("a bargain is not a bargain unless you have told forty lies"), honesty is owed to persons, not to companies or the state, and paying the bill is a privilege worth fighting over (pp. 59–60). Honour comes "from the domination of persons, rather than things", which makes it "inimical to capitalist accumulation" (pp. 60–61).

Then there is the paradox: "those whose claim to honour is greatest, and also most dependent upon lineal descent, are most careless of their sexual honour" (p. 65). Upper-class women smoke, drive, travel alone and are credited with lovers. Upper-class men keep second households without public disgrace. Pitt-Rivers does not put this down to foreign fashion. His answer is the *honi soit* principle again. The aristocratic lady's honour does not depend on male protection, because her status cannot be forfeited: "if she does not do so, she is wicked, she is not dishonoured" (p. 71). Honour has no fixed content. Its meaning shifts with a person's place in the social structure.

### 5. Caro Baroja: A History of Spanish Honour

Julio Caro Baroja, a Basque historian and ethnographer, offers the volume's only long historical view. He opens with three cautions. No proper history of the concept exists. The words may be "rhetorical commonplaces", since "those people who make most use of the words 'honour' and 'shame' … are not those whose lives are most strictly governed by the principles which those words express". And literary sources, Calderón's honour plays above all, may echo Italian *novelle* more than Spanish life (pp. 81–82). He therefore relies on law, theology, memoirs and chronicles.

He traces Spanish honour to three roots: classical *honos* and *honestas*, the barbarian "pride of blood", and the Christian "community of the faithful" (p. 83). The thirteenth-century *Siete Partidas* define *honra* as "preferment as a mark of praise, which a man gains by reason of the position he holds, or by doing some great deed, or by the goodness that is in him", and they treat defamation as equal to murder (pp. 84–85). They list the "infamous": bastards, adulteresses, widows who remarry within the year, panders, mountebanks, paid fighters of beasts or men, usurers, and knights who traded (p. 86). Shame (*verecundia*) covered everything from blushing at a lewd word to respect for parents, law and king (p. 87).

Then comes what he calls "a sort of somersault": the late medieval idea of *más valer*, "being worth more". Prestige was won by arms, rarely with any regard for moderation or justice. The Basque chronicler Lope García de Salazar traced the clan wars of the north to the desire "for greater worth (mas valer), as it was in ancient times throughout the whole world" (pp. 88–89). *Más valer* belonged to collective, patrilineal honour. Kinsmen answered a battle cry ("Gamboa! Oñaz!") to avenge an affront, and feuds lasted centuries (pp. 89–91). Vengeance drives the Spanish epic as Nemesis drives Greek tragedy (p. 92). Fray Luis de Granada explained the popularity of romances of chivalry by people's admiration for deeds of "effort and courage and a disdain for death", and Caro Baroja compares their ethic to a "ruling class morality", even Nietzsche's superman (p. 94).

The Catholic Monarchs crushed the faction leaders, and royal service became the new source of honour (pp. 98–99). The Inquisition then created an honour system of its own. Statutes of "purity of blood" required proof that all four grandparents were Old Christians. That made a great lord's patrilineal nobility vulnerable to a single converso ancestor in any line (pp. 101–03). Denunciations, *sambenitos* hung in churches and registers of tainted families kept these stains alive for generations (pp. 103–04). Money worked against the whole structure: Quevedo's "*Poderoso caballero / Es Don Dinero*" (p. 106), and Góngora's puns on the coins that buy crosses, coats of arms and dukedoms (p. 105). In reaction, Golden Age drama placed pure honour in the Old Christian peasant, in *El alcalde de Zalamea* and Lope's *Fuenteovejuna*, where the whole village answers "as one man" (pp. 107–09).

Caro Baroja reads Don Juan sociologically. He is "the literary result of a society in which personal 'prestige' or 'disgrace' has come to exercise an obsessive influence". He must kill most, gamble most and seduce most, and because female honour mattered so much to families, seduction "constituted one of the most remarkable triumphs" (p. 110). The picaresque hero is the opposite figure, the *sinvergüenza* (p. 112). Meanwhile a bourgeoisie with rules of its own grew up unnoticed by the drama. The Enlightenment called the old code "Gothic", and the nineteenth century abolished the purity statutes and laughed at the duel (pp. 114–17). In modern villages, honour survives in the left-wing idea that only the poor have it. Usury is the hidden cancer of the south, and "Today wealth, and nothing else, seems an almost physical force, against which there is no means of fighting" (pp. 119–24).

### 6. Campbell: Honour and the Devil

J. K. Campbell studied the Sarakatsani, about 4,000 Greek transhumant shepherds in Zagori in Epirus. He states his problem at once: honour "is profoundly important to Greeks whether they are peasants or cabinet ministers", yet "the self-regarding values of honour … recommend behaviour that is very different from the prescriptions of Christian charity and fellowship" (p. 141). How do devout people live with both?

The family is "a kind of social isolate". Kinsmen are trusted, in-laws trusted with reservations, and unrelated Sarakatsani are "almost enemies" whom it would be "unmanly not to exploit … within the limits conventionally allowed" (p. 142). *Timē*, honour, also means a price. All but the poorest families are born with it, so honour is "a condition of integrity, of being 'untouched'" by homicide, insult, seduction or a broken betrothal. Any outrage must be answered at once and with violence (pp. 143–44). The blood of an honourable man "takes fire" at an insult, and Campbell insists this is not a metaphor (p. 144). Very poor families are denied honour without any charge against them: they are "lost" families (p. 145).

The two sex-linked qualities are the man's manliness (*andrismos*) and the woman's sexual shame (*dropí*). A woman's shame is "an instinctive revulsion from sexual activity, an attempt in dress, movement, and attitude, to disguise the fact that she possesses the physical attributes of her sex". Because "honour is always something imputed by others", she protects it by outward conformity (p. 146). The models are the Mother of God, Christ's continence, the warrior saints George and Demetrius, and the klephtic outlaw hero (p. 147). Over a lifetime, a young unmarried man (*pallikari*) must win his first quarrel, and a head of family must cultivate cleverness, guile and the skilful lie (p. 148). Self-regard (*egoismos*) is "the inner necessity and obligation to achieve identity with the image of the ideal self", and shame is "the emotion experienced by an individual when he clearly fails to do so" (p. 149).

Campbell does not soften the ethic. It is "almost a virtue … to denigrate and deceive non-kinsmen"; no Sarakatsanos would offer an apology; "Co-operation, tolerance, love, must give way to autarky, arrogance, hostility"; "In the appearance of being a pallikari, he becomes a pallikari", and knives are drawn most boldly when others are there to stop them (pp. 150–51). "there is no shame in delivering insults, only in receiving them", and "the weak, the humble, the modest, even the merely good-hearted and co-operative, are not virtuous". These values "contradict in most respects the principles of Christian fellowship" (p. 152).

The reconciliation lies in the shepherds' theory of sin. Personal sins (perjury above all, sins against the family, and hubris against the very poor or an unmarried girl's name) are few and rarely committed (pp. 154–55). The great sins, sensuality and envy, are "ancestral", part of the fallen world and not of the individual will: "Sensuality was the consequence of Adam's sin, not his. Envy was the sin of the Devil in his act of rebellion against God" (p. 158). Sensuality is contained by external restraint: sex in darkness and silence, and hands washed before milking a ewe. The burden falls mainly on women (pp. 155–56). Envy grows out of competition for wealth, children and good marriages, not out of honour itself, and it shows itself as the evil eye (pp. 157, 165–67). The Holy Family as archetype sanctifies the earthly family and its duty of honour, and also sets a limit on how far one family may exploit another. But "One ought not to forgive a kinsman who has betrayed his trust, at least until he is dead", and "The poor are not blessed" (pp. 167–68). The Devil carries the guilt, and Christ saves the Sarakatsani "in free recognition of their innate nobility of soul" (p. 169).

### 7. Peristiany: Philotimo in a Cypriot Village

Peristiany's own essay describes Alona, a highland village of 650 people in Pitsilia, as it was in 1954. Under foreign rulers, Frankish, Turkish and British, the priest was the only legitimate authority. Every Westerner is a "Frank", and "A Greek is a Greek insofar as he is an Orthodox" (p. 176). The respected man is an *archontas*, generous and punctilious, loyal to local values. The returned emigrant who struts is asked, "Does he take himself for a Frank?" (pp. 177–78).

The key word is *philotimo*, love of honour, said of the person "who places considerations concerning his good name … above other — and more immediately profitable — considerations". The *atimos* lacks honour. If he does nothing about it he becomes *adiantropos*, shameless, "outside the moral order", and "a menace to the community as his actions are unpredictable" (p. 179). The family is again "a social isolate". The "moral vacuum increases" as one moves outward to the village, the city and the Franks (p. 180). A woman must be "virginal as a maiden and matronly as a wife" (p. 182). A man may sell firearms and still be a man, but a procurer "has abdicated his manhood" (pp. 183–84).

Between people with no social identity for each other, the rule is "*homo homini lupus* and the more *lupus* … the more *homo*" (p. 184). Peasants therefore turn every impersonal dealing, with a shopkeeper or a civil servant, into a personal one by claiming some tie, because only then does *philotimo* apply (pp. 185–86). What marks such a society, he says, is "the non-recognition of impersonal moral obligations in terms of citizenship or common humanity" (p. 187). Peristiany's conclusions became famous. Every man asserts his *isotimia*, his right to equal esteem: "*Ki' esy moustaki ki' ego moustaki*", we both have a moustache. The highlander is "not antagonistic but agonistic", and when he finds no worthy human opponent he pits himself against Fate, which is hubris (p. 188). Nepotism is "a moral duty" (p. 189). And:

> The punctiliousness of honour must be referred to the code of an exclusive and agonistic microsociety; that of honesty to an inclusive, egalitarian macrosociety. (pp. 189–90)

### 8. Bourdieu: Challenge and Riposte among the Kabyles

Pierre Bourdieu's essay, based on fieldwork in Kabylia between 1957 and 1961, during the Algerian war, is the most formal. It begins with three stories in which Kabyles who had lived abroad misread the "Kabyle rhetoric". One tries to settle a neighbour's theft of his stones as a money matter. A city landlord demands unconditional apology when custom allows the offender a face-saving formula ("If I have acted well, praise be to God; if I have erred, pardon before God"). A man rebuffs a peace delegation of marabouts and accuses them of taking bribes (pp. 193–97).

From these Bourdieu derives his rules. A challenge assumes that the challenged man is "his peer in honour": "Recognition of one's adversary as one's own equal in honour is therefore the basic condition of any challenge" (p. 197). Offence is the opposite of disdain, which refuses dialogue and so denies equal humanity. "Only dung swells", the Kabyles say of braggarts, and a rich family's European-style tomb had its ironwork stripped the night it was finished (p. 198). "The man without enemies is a donkey." An old man told Bourdieu, "There is nothing worse than to pass unnoticed, like a shadow … The challenge … is a highlight in the life of the one who receives it" (p. 199). To challenge someone who cannot reply dishonours the challenger: "Better that he strip himself … than that I should strip him" (p. 199).

Bourdieu's central point is that a gift works like a challenge. A gift too large to be returned dishonours the giver, just as an insult to someone unable to reply does. Every exchange carries a "more or less dissimulated" challenge, so communication is "a compromise between contract and conflict" (pp. 204, 213). Feuds between factions and tribes were "a strictly regulated game", "a competition of merit played out before the tribunal of public opinion". They ended when one side seized the enemy's main roof beam, and prisoners were sent home in a new robe that stood for a shroud. A man asked to join a real war, where Europeans were simply shot on sight, refused: "when I am not angry, I can't fire on people" (pp. 201–03).

He separates *nif*, the point of honour, the active readiness to reply, from *h'urma*, honour as the sacred (*h'aram*) that can be violated. The Kabyle summary of what is sacred is "One's home, one's wife, one's rifles" (p. 219). An insult to *h'urma* allows no settlement. A man who accepts blood money has "agreed to eat the blood of his brother" (p. 216). The sacred exists only through the *nif* that defends it, and *nif* matters only to someone who has sacred things to defend (pp. 218–19). Bourdieu maps this onto a mythical scheme of oppositions: left and right, inside and outside, house and assembly, wet and dry, female and male (pp. 219–25). Its maxims are blunt: "Your tomb is your house", "Shame is a young girl", and the son-in-law is "the veil cast over shame" (pp. 223, 227).

His conclusions are among the volume's sharpest. "Only the devil (Chit'an) says 'I'" (p. 210). "Man is man through men; God alone … is God through himself." The man of honour "is one man, and of one word" (p. 211). "He who has lost his honour no longer exists" (p. 212). And:

> The ethos of honour is fundamentally opposed to a universal and formal morality which affirms the equality in dignity of all men and consequently the equality of their rights and duties. (p. 228)

Theft from an enemy tribe was a feat of arms, and cheating strangers carried no dishonour. Within the group the rule was "Help your own kinsmen … whether they are right or wrong" (pp. 228–29). Law, politics and economics were not separate spheres. Even a sale was dressed as an exchange of honour, with the seller handing back part of the price "so that the latter may buy meat for his children" (pp. 229–31). Most strikingly, the system is "lived rather than clearly conceived". Kabyles recognise a breach of honour the way a speaker spots a grammatical error "without being in command of the syntactical order which it infringes" (pp. 231–32). Here, in embryo, is the theory of practice that Bourdieu later developed.

### 9. Abou-Zeid: Sharaf and 'Ird among the Bedouin

The Egyptian anthropologist Ahmed Abou-Zeid wrote about the Awlad Ali of the Western Desert near Sallum. He finds honour and shame the vaguest of Bedouin value terms. *Sharaf* comes from a root meaning "highness" and grows through the deeds of a man and his agnates, living and dead (p. 245). He distinguishes *'aib*, shame that falls on the doer alone, from *'ar*, shame that falls on the whole kin group (p. 246). A man may raid a strong camp for a camel, which counts as heroism, but stealing a sheep is "as mean as the seized animal itself" (p. 246). Pride in kin is like pride in a flock: both measure power (p. 249). Hospitality reaches the point of "nothing less than reckless" generosity (p. 250).

The tent (*beit*) is a *haram*, a sanctuary, because it shelters the women, who are sacred, and anyone staying in it is inviolable (pp. 253–54). This gives the "right of refuge". Beyond it lies the "right of the *wajh*" (face): a murderer may go to his victim's own tent and claim immunity, and the bereaved must grant it and persuade their kin to accept blood money (pp. 254–55). Women's honour has its own word, *'ird*, and it differs from *sharaf* because it "can only be lost by the misconduct of the woman. And once lost, it cannot be regained." The duty of vengeance falls on her father's brother's son, not her husband (pp. 256–57). Abou-Zeid ranks three grades of honour: conformity; superiority won at others' expense; and the highest, "when the ideal can only be realized at the expense of the performer himself", as in granting sanctuary to an enemy, where "honour is larger than life". Excess turns honour into shame: reckless raiding and generosity are blamed (pp. 258–59).

### 10. Comparing the Societies

| | Andalusia (Pitt-Rivers) | Sarakatsani (Campbell) | Alona, Cyprus (Peristiany) | Kabylia (Bourdieu) | Awlad Ali (Abou-Zeid) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Key terms | *honra*, *vergüenza*, *hombría* | *timē*, *andrismos*, *dropí*, *egoismos* | *timē*, *philotimo*, *isotimia* | *nif*, *h'urma*, *esser* | *sharaf*, *'ird*, *'ar* |
| Unit of honour | Nuclear family and pueblo | Family as "social isolate" | Family, village, nation | Agnatic lineage | Tent (*beit*) and lineage |
| Women's part | Sexual purity, passive | Shame as revulsion from sex | Modesty, "skinless" if lost | Sacred "left hand", inside | *'ird*, can only be lost |
| Strangers | Hospitality to status, suspicion otherwise | "Almost enemies", may be exploited | Moral vacuum, *homo homini lupus* | Theft from them a feat | Refuge and protection |
| What limits honour | Equality of the pueblo, ridicule | Sanctity of the family, sin | Personal ties | Mediators, the "door" out | Excess becomes shame |
| Relation to religion | Plebeians more puritan than the devout rich | Devil takes the guilt | Orthodoxy as national identity | Mythico-ritual system | Sanctuary of the *haram* |

The family resemblances are strong. Honour is collective. Male honour depends on female chastity. Strangers fall outside moral obligation, public opinion is the court, and the state is alien. The differences are just as clear. Andalusian honour is graded by class, Sarakatsan honour is set against Christianity, Kabyle honour is a formal game of exchange, and Bedouin honour is bound to sanctuary and kinship.

### 11. Keen: Nobility, Virtue and Honour in Chivalry

Maurice Keen's *Chivalry* shows the same tension in the medieval nobility. From the thirteenth century, emphasis moved from the ceremony of dubbing to eligibility by descent. Frederick II's ordinances, Beaumanoir and the later Templar rule demanded knightly ancestry (pp. 143–44). As Frederick's chancellor Peter de Vinea put it, nobility passes to those in the line of descent, but the dignity of knighthood does not (p. 146). The order was legally closed but never closed in fact. Marriage to rich commoners, purchase of estates, war service and royal letters of nobility kept bringing in new men (pp. 145–48).

That forced the question of what nobility really was. Bartolus of Sassoferrato distinguished three kinds: theological nobility (election to salvation), natural nobility (virtue fit to rule) and civil nobility (whatever the prince recognises) (p. 149). Oliver de La Marche listed five routes into nobility and concluded that "he is the happier, and is to be the more esteemed, who commences his nobility in virtue, than he who brings his to an end in vice" (p. 150). Both agreed that nobility required princely recognition, a calling, wealth and style of life, virtue and descent (p. 151). On calling, Jean de Bueil wrote, "We poor soldiers … are noble by lineage, and those who are not noble by lineage are so by the exercise and profession of arms, which is noble in itself" (p. 152). On wealth, a lawyer remarked that "nobility without riches is like Faith without Works". On style, the nobleman was expected to give largesse, not to save: "Riches were for redistribution, not for re-investment" (pp. 154–55). That parallels Pitt-Rivers's Andalusian beneficence.

On virtue against blood, Keen finds the learned witnesses almost all on the side of virtue. Lancelot in the prose romance asks how one man can have more gentility than another when all descend from one couple (p. 157). Andreas Capellanus, Jean de Meun, Froissart and Dante all said much the same. The heralds observed that in Rome the temples of Virtue and Honour had a single door, so that one entered Honour only through Virtue (pp. 157–58). The compromise was to treat lineage as a school of virtue: the noble-born should take their ancestors as examples and try to resemble them, for "nobility of manners always overtops nobility of race" (pp. 160–61).

Chapter IX asks whether this theory had any effect in practice, and Keen answers that it did. English heralds granted arms "to men of virtue and noble courage", and Charles VII ennobled soldiers for single feats such as being first into a tower at Pontoise, as well as Joan of Arc's family (pp. 163–65). Honours were finely graded by kind of fighting (tourney, assault, pitched battle, crusade), down to how a knight's tomb effigy should be posed (pp. 169–70). The Teutonic Knights' Table of Honour, where heralds seated the most travelled crusaders, ended with a badge reading "*Honneur vainc tout!*" (p. 173). Dishonour had its rituals too: arms displayed upside down for a defaulted ransom, and Sir Ralph Grey sentenced to have his spurs struck off by the master cook (pp. 174–76). Keen sets this against anthropology. These rituals resemble those "whereby primitive peoples maintain and uphold the pecking orders of their society". The difference is that the chivalric system rested on "a reasoned and reasonably coherent social ideology". Its insistence that nobility must be related to virtue "modified the rigidity of class exclusiveness" (p. 177). As Jean de Bueil put it, "we poor soldiers will save our souls in arms just as well as we might be living in contemplation upon a diet of roots" (p. 178).

### 12. Assessment

**What the volume achieved.** *Honour and Shame* took a word that moralists had owned and made it an object of social analysis. The essays agree on a core that still holds up. Honour is a claim that needs ratification by others. It is collective, and it is staked on the conduct of the family's women. It flourishes where personal relations outweigh impersonal ones and where the state is weak or foreign. Pitt-Rivers's double formula (honour felt, claimed and paid; shame imposed, accepted and felt) and his account of how honour legitimates power are real theoretical gains. Bourdieu's analysis of the gift as challenge, and of honour as a practical sense rather than a code, reaches well beyond Kabylia.

**Its internal tensions.** The authors disagree more than the title suggests. Pitt-Rivers presents honour as structured, but in his own town its meaning changes with class: the aristocrat is wicked but not dishonoured, while the plebeian is easily shamed. Caro Baroja doubts that the vocabulary governs conduct at all, warns against literary sources, and shows that Spanish honour was rebuilt several times over, by lineage, Inquisition, monarchy and money. Peristiany's own introduction warns that the shared "moustache" proves nothing about cultural affinity, yet the book's subtitle claims a single "Mediterranean society". The evidence also tilts toward honour as precedence among men. Women appear almost entirely as the vulnerable point of male honour. Only Campbell's line about the girl who "feels shame" for being a woman at all, and Bourdieu's remarks on the women's fountain, come close to their own view.

**The Mediterranean frame.** This is the volume's weakest point, and outside the books read here it later drew sustained criticism. Anthropologists such as Michael Herzfeld argued that "honour and shame" glossed many different local terms with two English words and turned a region into a moral type. The essays themselves support that worry. *Philotimo*, *nif*, *sharaf* and *vergüenza* do different work. And Pitt-Rivers's examples come as readily from Shakespeare, Voltaire, the Garter and English schoolgirls as from Andalusia. That suggests he had found something about honour as such, not about one sea.

**Honour against universal morality.** The volume's most lasting finding may be negative. Campbell, Peristiany and Bourdieu each conclude, independently, that the honour code is particularist and conflicts with any ethic owed to strangers as such. For Campbell that ethic is Christian fellowship. For Peristiany it is "honesty" owed in an "egalitarian macrosociety". For Bourdieu it is "a universal and formal morality". Campbell's shepherds manage the conflict by giving the Devil the blame. Pitt-Rivers's plebeians resolve it the other way, and are more puritan about sex than the pious rich. Keen's knights tried to bridge it by making virtue the door to honour. Read together, the essays describe a recurring human solution, not an exotic survival. Where trust cannot be extended to strangers, worth is measured within the group, and the group's women become the boundary it must guard.

**Shame and guilt.** The volume is also the background to the familiar contrast between "shame cultures" and "guilt cultures" (see [[Did Homeric Heroes Have Minds? Decisions, the Gods and the Soul]]). It complicates the contrast more than it supports it. Campbell's shepherds feel shame before others and guilt for perjury. Pitt-Rivers notes that a person may feel dishonoured even when no one knows. Bourdieu's man of honour has a conscience that is "a kind of interiorization of others" (p. 211). Honour turns out to be a way of holding together self-regard and public regard, not a primitive absence of conscience.

### Conclusion

*Honour and Shame* argued that across the small communities of the Mediterranean, worth was a claim made before an audience of equals, defended by men, staked on women, and denied to strangers. Pitt-Rivers gave this a structure. Honour fuses precedence with virtue, and so lets power pass for merit. Caro Baroja gave it a history in which lineage, Inquisition and money each remade the code. Campbell, Peristiany, Bourdieu and Abou-Zeid showed it at work among shepherds, villagers, Berbers and Bedouin. Each found that it clashed with universal ethics, and each found a different local accommodation. Keen's chivalry shows Europe's own nobility arguing the same question in learned terms and deciding, at least in theory, that virtue must open the door to honour. The single "Mediterranean" honour did not survive later criticism. The anatomy of honour these essays worked out has survived, and it describes the street corner and the officers' mess as accurately as the Sierra.
