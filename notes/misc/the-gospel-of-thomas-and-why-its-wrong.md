## 1. Apostolic Tradition and Early Reception

- **Non-Apostolic Authorship**: The *Gospel of Thomas* was not written by the Apostle Thomas. Scholars date the text to the late 1st or early 2nd century, long after Thomas’s death. Unlike the canonical Gospels, which were either written by apostles or their close associates (e.g., Mark for Peter, Luke for Paul), the *Gospel of Thomas* lacks this connection.
- **No Early Church Usage**: The text was not widely recognized or used in the liturgical or theological life of the early Church. The canonical Gospels were read during worship, quoted by Church Fathers, and accepted in the broader Christian community. The *Gospel of Thomas* had no such widespread acceptance.

---

## 2. Rejection by Early Church Fathers

- **Irenaeus of Lyons (ca. 180 AD)**: In his work *Against Heresies*, Irenaeus rejected texts like the *Gospel of Thomas* for their association with Gnostic heresies. He emphasized that the canonical Gospels—Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John—were the only legitimate accounts of Christ’s life.
- **Origen and Eusebius**: These early theologians categorized the *Gospel of Thomas* as apocryphal, meaning it did not belong to the recognized body of Scripture. Eusebius, in his *Ecclesiastical History*, explicitly listed it among heretical and spurious writings.

---

## 3. Gnostic Influence and Theological Problems

- **Gnostic Worldview**: The *Gospel of Thomas* reflects Gnostic teachings, which were condemned by the Church. Gnosticism taught that salvation came through secret knowledge (*gnosis*), a view contrary to the apostolic proclamation of salvation through Christ’s life, death, and resurrection.
- **Unorthodox Teachings**: The text contains sayings of Jesus that conflict with Orthodox theology. For example:
    - **Denial of the Incarnation**: Gnosticism often downplayed or denied the physical reality of Christ’s incarnation, a central tenet of Christian belief.
    - **Disparagement of Women**: Saying 114 of the *Gospel of Thomas* suggests that women must become male to enter the Kingdom of Heaven, a concept completely alien to Orthodox teaching on the dignity and equality of men and women in Christ.
- **Lack of Crucifixion and Resurrection**: Unlike the canonical Gospels, the *Gospel of Thomas* does not focus on Christ’s passion, death, and resurrection, which are foundational to Christian faith.

---

## 4. Church Councils and Canon Formation

- **Councils of Laodicea (ca. 363 AD), Hippo (393 AD), and Carthage (397 AD)**: These councils discussed and affirmed the canon of Scripture. The *Gospel of Thomas* was absent from these lists, which recognized only the four canonical Gospels.
- **Synod of Jerusalem (1672 AD)**: This synod reaffirmed the traditional canon and explicitly excluded texts like the *Gospel of Thomas*.

---

## 5. Liturgical and Theological Consistency

- **Canonical Books in Worship**: The Church only includes texts in the canon that are divinely inspired and suitable for public worship. The *Gospel of Thomas* was never part of the lectionary or used in Orthodox liturgy.
- **Consistency with Apostolic Teaching**: The canonical Gospels consistently present a coherent narrative of Christ’s life and teaching, aligning with the Church’s doctrinal framework. The *Gospel of Thomas* diverges significantly, lacking the theological and salvific depth required of Scripture.

---

## Conclusion

The Eastern Orthodox Church rejects the *Gospel of Thomas* because it lacks apostolic authority, was not accepted by the early Christian community, promotes unorthodox theology, and conflicts with the core teachings of Christ. The Church relies on the guidance of the Holy Spirit, the consensus of the early Church, and the teachings of the Fathers to discern which writings are truly inspired.
