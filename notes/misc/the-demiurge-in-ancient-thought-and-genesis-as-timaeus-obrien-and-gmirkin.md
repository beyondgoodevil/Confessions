Two recent books take up the craftsman god of the *Timaeus*. They come at him from opposite ends. Carl O'Brien starts with Plato and follows the Demiurge forward through six centuries: Jewish exegesis, Middle Platonism, the Gnostics and the Hermetists, Origen, and Plotinus, who did without him. Russell Gmirkin starts with Genesis and argues that its first eleven chapters were built on the *Timaeus* and its sequel, the *Critias*. If he is right, the earliest reader of the *Timaeus* who wrote a creation account of his own was not Philo but the author of Genesis 1.

The two books are given equal weight below. O'Brien gets sections 1–8, Gmirkin sections 9–16, and section 17 sets them side by side. O'Brien is cited by page. Gmirkin's edition has no page numbers in my copy, so he is cited by section (§) and by biblical verse.

### 1. What Demiurgy Is

O'Brien defines his subject narrowly. Demiurgy is "world-generation via the ordering of pre-existent matter by an entity, sometimes represented as endowed with only limited abilities, according to some sort of model". The activity is intellective. It is set against creation *ex nihilo*, in which God makes simply by willing (p. 2). By this test the potter-God of Genesis 2 is not a demiurge (p. 3). What the traditions share is a minimum: a mind orders pre-existing matter after a pre-existing model, on geometric lines (p. 4). Beyond that there is no single system. The figure takes different shapes because people read the *Timaeus* differently (p. 4).

O'Brien puts five questions to each author (p. 16):

1. How does the Demiurge relate to the First Principle?
2. How does he act on matter?
3. What is the status of the Forms?
4. Where does evil come from?
5. What limits him, such as Providence or Necessity?

His thesis is a trend. Beginning with Numenius, the demiurgic function comes apart from the highest principle and sinks down a chain of intermediaries. Two things end this. Christianity puts the Son inside a Trinity, and Neoplatonism replaces deliberate making with automatic procession (p. 17).

Two background points matter. First, the Old Academy let the Demiurge fade once it stopped holding the Theory of Forms. He came back in the first to third centuries because he was useful to dualist systems (p. 5). Second, the Stoics kept the language of craft, God as a crafting fire (πῦρ τεχνικόν), but preferred a biological picture. God works on matter from inside, as seed works in a womb (pp. 5–9). That rival image runs through the whole book.

### 2. The Timaeus and Its First Readers

Plato's choice of word is odd. A *dēmiourgos* was a tradesman, often a slave or close to slaves, and Plato himself shut such men out of politics. Dillon calls the Demiurge "a bizarre figure, introduced in a bizarre manner". Vlastos calls the title a "triumph of the philosophical imagination over ingrained social prejudice" (p. 19). The word did also mean "magistrate" in some Dorian states (p. 23).

Read literally, the *Timaeus* has the Demiurge do the following:

- bind the four elements by proportion;
- make the world a sphere and set it turning;
- mix soul out of Being, Sameness and Difference, cut it by the series 1, 2, 4, 8 and 1, 3, 9, 27, and bend it into the circles of the Same and the Different;
- make the Young Gods, then hand the making of mortals over to them (pp. 20–1).

Necessity, the "erratic cause" (ἡ πλανωμένη αἰτία), limits him. Given the choice, he gives man a thin skull for quick thinking over a thick one for long life (Tim. 75b; p. 21). Matter already shows "traces" of order before he arrives. What he adds is intelligibility, not order as such (p. 21).

The Demiurge is called good but never "the Good". He looks to a model he did not make. That leaves his relation to the Forms open, and later Platonists filled the gap in different ways (pp. 22, 29–30). Read allegorically, as most of Plato's own pupils read it, the myth yields a few theses (pp. 22–3):

- the visible world depends on a higher one;
- the world copies a beautiful model;
- the world falls short because copying in matter needs compromise, not because anyone is malicious;
- the world is always coming to be and never complete.

On that reading the Demiurge does nothing the World-Soul does not already do. He is, in a phrase from the *Philebus*, merely "the cause of the mixture" (p. 23). O'Brien adds that Plato blurs ordering and creating: in the *Symposium* every craftsman is a "maker" (p. 24). Still, "The Greeks did not mention the idea of creatio ex nihilo, even satirically" (p. 24).

Aristotle read the story literally, but only to attack it (p. 25). Speusippus kept a One, a Dyad and a World-Soul and dropped the Forms. Xenocrates said the myth was told "for the purposes of exposition". He made Number and Soul do the Demiurge's work, and by adopting Aristotle's self-thinking Mind as first principle he began pushing demiurgy below the top level (pp. 25–7). The modern debate repeats the ancient one. Tarán argues for allegory: the story is told out of order, and soul is both prior to body and made after it. Vallejo argues for a literal beginning. O'Brien sides with allegory (pp. 32–5). He notes, though, that between the first and third centuries nearly everyone took the Demiurge as real (pp. 27–8).

### 3. Philo: Logos, Noetic Cosmos and the Logos-Cutter

Philo is the first witness, which O'Brien finds awkward: "the first witness to the notion of demiurgy in later Platonism is not, strictly speaking, a Platonist at all" (p. 15). Philo's allegiance was to the philosophy of Moses. He used Greek structures to expound Scripture, not to convert anyone to Plato (p. 36). Yet he quotes the *Timaeus* more than all of Plato's other dialogues together (p. 39), and *De Opificio Mundi* reads as the *Timaeus* rewritten in Genesis' terms (p. 57).

O'Brien credits Philo with three things:

1. **The noetic cosmos.** Before making the visible world, God first designs the intelligible one. An architect plans a city in his mind and then builds it in stone (Opif. 17–19). The intelligible world has no place of its own. It is "the noetic cosmos is simply the Logos of God as he is engaged in the process of making the cosmos" (Opif. 24; p. 60 n. 80). Philo is the first extant writer to use the phrase κόσμος νοητός. O'Brien warns against making him the inventor of the doctrine that the Forms are God's thoughts: "we must be careful not to foist originality upon Philo" (p. 43). Philo's craftsman is "upwardly mobile". He has been promoted from tradesman to architect because he now designs the pattern instead of copying one (p. 61).
2. **The Logos as mediator.** God made everything out of matter "without touching it himself – for it was not right for his happy and blessed nature to touch unlimited and jumbled matter". He worked through incorporeal powers (Spec. i.329; p. 47). The Logos stands "between the Lord and you" (Her. 205–6; p. 43). It does the work of the Young Gods, of the mixing bowl and of the World-Soul, which Philo can therefore drop (p. 37).
3. **The Logos-Cutter.** In *Who is the Heir*, God sharpens "his all-cutting Logos". He divides formless being into heavy and light, then into the four elements, and on down by equal halves (Her. 133–40). Here the God of Genesis becomes the inventor of Platonic division (pp. 48–51). O'Brien thinks this image is Philo's own, perhaps prompted by the flaming sword of Genesis 3:24 and by the animals Abraham cut in half in Genesis 15 (pp. 47–8, 55).

On evil, Philo will not blame matter, which God had in the right amount (p. 52). In *Opif.* 75 he blames the unnamed helpers of "Let us make man" (pp. 68–9). He also keeps a double creation of man: the man "after the image" is a genus, bodiless and neither male nor female; the moulded man is mortal (Opif. 134; p. 69). On the eternity of the world, *De Aeternitate Mundi* weighs three positions: the world unmade and imperishable, made and perishable, or made and imperishable. It treats the third as the view Plato took from Moses (pp. 78–9). O'Brien's verdict is that Philo calls God "creator" (κτίστης) because God makes the noetic realm, not because Philo clearly taught that matter came from nothing (p. 81).

### 4. Plutarch and Maximus: Dualism, Isis and the Nod of Zeus

Plutarch reads the *Timaeus* literally and dualistically. He has two reasons. A temporal creation is what Plato said, and a second principle frees God from blame for evil (pp. 84, 115). He attacks the Stoics for making God responsible for everything, vice included. God does not need "pirates and slayers of men and parricides and tyrants" in a city he shares with men (Comm. Not. 1065e; p. 93). A god who is "an intellectual body and enmattered mind" is not a principle but a compound (1085b–c; p. 94).

*On Isis and Osiris* reads an Egyptian myth through the *Timaeus*:

- **Osiris** is the Demiurge, or transcendent reason, and he is strangely passive: dismembered, with his phallus lost.
- **Isis** is Receptacle, matter and World-Soul at once. She has "an innate love" of the Good, desires it, and is impregnated by it (372e–f). This is generation from below (pp. 99–101).
- **Typhon** is the irrational part of the World-Soul, the cause of eclipses and disorder (371b). He is not destroyed but disabled: Hermes makes lyre-strings of his sinews (373c–d; p. 104).
- **Horus** is the visible world, a bastard because matter is mixed into him (373a–b; p. 103).

In the *Table Talk* the Demiurge continually engages in geometry. Without geometry matter would slip back into indeterminacy (719e). In Plutarch's own words, "matter is the most disorderly substrate, Form is the most beautiful paradigm and God is the best cause" (720b; p. 110). God's making is "by other contacts and attachments", not by seed. O'Brien reads this as Plutarch keeping the technological image of the Demiurge against the Stoics' biological one (pp. 108–9).

Maximus of Tyre is interesting because he is not original. His orations show the stock views of the second century (p. 117). He gives two incompatible pictures:

- **The Demiurge at work.** Like a smith whose anvil throws off sparks, he produces evils as by-products: "What we call evil and destruction and what we cry over, the craftsman calls the conservation of the whole" (Or. 41.4; p. 122). O'Brien objects that "an industrialist is still responsible for harmful by-products" (p. 121).
- **The nod of Zeus.** The world is ordered merely by Zeus' nod of assent, which spares him all work (p. 123).

Maximus also offers a machine image, in which the whole moves and the parts share the work (Or. 13.4; p. 130). He lists four causes: Providence, Destiny, Fortune and craft (Or. 5). He never fits them together (pp. 125–8). O'Brien sums up his attitude: "What is important is that God generated the world; how is irrelevant" (p. 138).

### 5. Numenius and His Three Gods

Numenius is the hinge of the book. He separates a First God from the Demiurge and counts three gods. Plotinus was accused of copying him (p. 139). O'Brien works mainly from the fragments Eusebius quotes, and treats the reports in Calcidius and Proclus with caution (p. 139).

- **The First God** is Being and pure intellect. He is "inactive during the whole process of generation, he is the King", while the Demiurge goes about the heavens (Fr. 12; p. 146). His stillness is an "innate movement" from which the order and stability of the world flow (Fr. 15; p. 151).
- **The Second and Third Gods "are in fact one".** In contact with matter, the Demiurge unifies it but is split by it. He looks at matter and grows forgetful of himself (Fr. 11; p. 144). He works like a helmsman: his hand is on the tiller of matter while his eyes are on the God above, from whom he takes the Forms (Fr. 18; p. 156).
- **The First God is the Good itself** (αὐτοάγαθον). The Demiurge is good only by participation (Fr. 16, 20; pp. 153, 157).
- **The farmer and the planter.** The First God sows the seed of every soul, and the Demiurge plants it out and transplants it (Fr. 13; pp. 146–9).

Matter is evil and has a "soul" of its own. God corrects its faults without destroying them, and "God uses persuasion and Necessity obeys" (Calcidius 299; p. 162). Numenius found the doctrine of three gods in the Second Platonic Letter, as Valentinus and Justin also did (p. 167). He also left Neoplatonism two principles. First, all is in all, but in each according to its nature. Second, "undiminished giving": a lamp lights another without losing its flame (pp. 150, 168). Dodds dismissed Numenius as a man who debased Greek thought. O'Brien answers that "leading Neoplatonic philosophers are not in the habit of plagiarising from charlatans" (p. 167).

For the Gnostics, the decisive move is this. The Demiurge becomes a distinct second god, less good than the first, damaged by matter, and set making by desire (*orexis*) (p. 168).

### 6. Hermetism and Valentinus: The Demiurge Demoted

In the *Poimandres*, Mind brings forth a second, demiurgic Mind. That Mind makes the seven Governors, whose rule is Fate (CH i.9; pp. 176–7). Here the Demiurge is "largely irrelevant" to the story (p. 171). The real drama is Man. God "was really in love with his own image" in Man, and gave him leave to make things too. Man breaks through the planetary spheres, sees his reflection in Nature, falls in love with it and sinks into matter (CH i.12–14; pp. 179–83). On the way back, the soul hands back the Governors' gifts zone by zone (CH i.25; p. 181). The other tractates differ. CH ix makes the cosmos a second craftsman "of life". The *Asclepius* lets Necessity follow God's pleasure and keeps evil in matter, "fertile in malice" (pp. 192, 197, 203). Jonas puts the problem well: "The Gnostic descensus cannot do without the contingency of subjective affect and will" (p. 187).

In Valentinian myth, demiurgy turns hostile. The youngest Aeon, Sophia, reaches for the Father without her consort. Her passion is cast out of the Pleroma as Achamoth, and Achamoth's emotions become matter: her tears become the moist substances and her laughter the bright ones (Iren. i.4.2; pp. 220–4). The Demiurge is her offspring and an "abortion". He builds the seven heavens "without knowing Heaven" and moulds man "although ignorant of Man" (Iren. i.5.3; p. 208). In the *Apocryphon of John*, Ialdabaoth boasts "I am a jealous god, besides me there is none". The text comments that the boast gives him away, for of whom else would he be jealous (p. 228).

O'Brien draws distinctions here. The Valentinian Demiurge is "an entity with limited resources", ignorant rather than evil. He can repent and be rewarded with the middle place (pp. 230, 242). Ptolemy's *Letter to Flora* makes him "just", set between the true God and the devil (pp. 236–7). The Sethian Demiurge is darker and owes "more to Iranian dualism than to Platonism" (p. 241). O'Brien sees Gnosticism as "an extreme evolution of the concept of the Demiurge". The aim of insulating the First Principle from matter, pushed far enough, sets the two against each other (p. 206). In the end the universe is "nothing other than a divine aberration" (p. 243).

### 7. Origen: The Son as Immediate Craftsman

Origen keeps the pattern of a First and Second God but changes what it means. The Father is a Monad, beyond mind and being. The Son is Wisdom, born without beginning. In him lie "the principles, the reasons and the species of all creation" (Princ. i.2.2; pp. 252–4). So the Forms sit in the Son, not in the Father's thought and not above the Demiurge as they did in Numenius (p. 256). The Son is begotten eternally, as a ray from a light, and not by any division of the Father's substance (pp. 251–2, 256). He is the instrument: the Father creates *ex ipso*, the Son *per ipsum*. Origen calls Christ the "immediate craftsman" (προσεχῶς δημιουργός) (CCels. vi.60; p. 258). Against the Gnostics, the Son does not imitate a higher world with lesser copies. Scripture "does not say that the Son makes similar works, but that He similarly makes the same works" (Princ. i.2.12; p. 257).

Origen rejects uncreated matter. Matter is a substrate that never exists without qualities, and God made it (pp. 261, 271). He also answers the old question of what God did before the world, without making the world eternal. Only the intelligible pattern, held in the Son, is co-eternal with God (pp. 261–2). The visible world exists because rational souls, created equal, fell by their own free choice. Free will takes the place that recalcitrant matter held in Platonism as the root of evil (pp. 265, 283). Origen seems to have held that God's power is limited, so that he made only as many minds and as much matter as he could hold in his providence. Justinian quotes him: "if the power of God were unlimited, it would necessarily be unable to intelligise itself". Rufinus softened the passage (pp. 280–2). O'Brien remarks that this makes God "akin to James Clerk-Maxwell's calculating demon" (p. 281). O'Brien calls Origen's system "a democratic version of Gnosticism": the soul does not belong in matter, yet salvation is open to everyone and creation is not a split inside God (p. 267).

### 8. Plotinus, Proclus and O'Brien's Conclusions

Plotinus ends the Demiurge's career. The One does not order anything. It overflows. What it produces then shapes itself by turning back in contemplation. The One makes without deliberation, without change and without knowing its products (pp. 291–2). A craftsman who plans has to hesitate first, and that would put doubt in the divine (p. 295). Plotinus does concede that the world is as good as if it had been planned (p. 295). The Demiurge survives in later Neoplatonism only as a lower office. In Proclus he is Intellect, third in the triad of Being, Life and Intellect. Below him run partial demiurges: a second Zeus, Dionysus, the Titans (pp. 297–301).

O'Brien's closing chapter draws the threads together:

- **One motif.** Each system has a maker who uses a model, orders matter on geometric lines, often does not make the matter, and acts from goodness. All of them answer to the *Timaeus*: "ordering of disorderly matter by an entity of limited powers in accordance with rationality, or 'a beautiful model'" (pp. 304–7).
- **Falling rank.** The wish to keep the First Principle clean of matter pushes demiurgy down the scale. It ends in antagonism with the Gnostics (p. 304). The Demiurge sits low partly because he must hand the Forms on to the sensible world (p. 309).
- **Complexity.** Each thinker goes one level further back than the last: "Plato may have had difficulty in finding the Father of the universe, but Numenius seems to have had no trouble in discovering its Grandfather!" (p. 306).
- **Continual or once-off.** For Plutarch and Philo, demiurgy never stops. For the Gnostics and Hermetists it was a single catastrophe (pp. 308–9).
- **Why he faded.** The concept had no answer to Plotinus' argument that the world's generation "could not be the result of deliberation, but must be spontaneous" (p. 309).

O'Brien compares the ancient quarrel between creation and demiurgy with the modern one between Darwinism and design. He cites Behe, and notes that Behe's claims are disputed (p. 305). He thinks creation and demiurgy differ more in mechanism than in their basic account of cause. Both are on the "deistic" side against atomism (p. 306). He also says that a God who creates from a model is "clearly not a Jewish or Christian concept". In Jewish and Christian thought, he says, it comes from Platonism, by way of Philo and Origen (pp. 302, 305). That claim is exactly what Gmirkin disputes.

### 9. Gmirkin's Thesis and the Date of Genesis

Gmirkin sets out "to demonstrate the systematic use of Plato's Timaeus and Critias in the Primordial History as a whole" (ch. 1). He draws three large conclusions:

1. Genesis 1–3 is a unity, built on the *Timaeus*' two stages. A supreme god makes the cosmos, and then his offspring make mortal life.
2. Yahweh Elohim of Genesis 2–3, and the sons of God of Genesis 6, were first meant as terrestrial offspring of the Creator Elohim of Genesis 1.
3. "The origin of Jewish monotheism is to be traced to Plato's Timaeus" (ch. 1).

The argument depends on a date. Gmirkin works in the "Hellenistic paradigm" begun by Niels Peter Lemche in 1993. On this view, nothing outside the Bible shows that the biblical texts existed before the third century BCE (§1.2.3.1). His own earlier books argued several points (§1.2.3.2–4):

- The supposed early witness in Diodorus comes from Theophanes of Mytilene (62 BCE), not Hecataeus.
- The Septuagint of about 270 BCE is therefore the first evidence of a Pentateuch.
- Genesis used the *Babyloniaca* of Berossus, and Exodus answered Manetho.
- The Mosaic laws draw on Plato's *Laws*.
- The Pentateuch was composed about 273–272 BCE by the same Jewish and Samaritan scholars who translated it for the Library at Alexandria.

He argues that the older "pre-Hellenistic paradigm" was circular. Because it assumed a Persian-era Pentateuch, it ruled Greek sources out from the start (§1.1.3, §1.2.2.4). He points to the Elephantine papyri of about 450–400 BCE as "the elephant in the room" (Granerød). They show a Jewish colony that kept Passover without Moses and a seven-day week without a sabbath. Polytheistic Yahweh worship there was tolerated by Jerusalem, with no trace of biblical writings (§1.2.2.4).

### 10. Genesis 1 Is Not a Creation Myth

Gmirkin begins with genre. Ancient Near Eastern cosmogonies come in four types (§2.2):

- **Theogonies**, in which nature is itself the gods, as in Hesiod and with El and his seventy sons.
- **Theomachies**, in which a god defeats the sea, as Marduk defeats Tiamat and Yahweh defeats Rahab.
- **Palatial world-myths**, in which the world is God's palace, as in Job's architect with his plumb line, his storehouses of snow and his beaten-brass sky.
- **Local palace myths**, centred on Zion or Gerizim.

The Bible knows all four. "Yet it is striking that Gen 1:1–2:3 conforms to none of these tropes" (§2.3). There is no battle, no palace, no bureaucracy of angels and no sacred geography. God speaks in monologue, not dialogue. *Tehom* and *raqia* are only verbal echoes of older myth (§2.3). The scholarly habit of calling Genesis 1 "demythologized" admits the point without explaining it.

Instead, Genesis 1 asks the three questions of Greek natural science (§3.1):

1. What did the world begin as?
2. What forces acted on it?
3. How did those forces produce the present order?

Gmirkin surveys every cosmogony from Thales to Zeno (§3.2). He concludes that Genesis 1 belongs to a hybrid genre that only Plato had used: the "scientific-theological-mythical" cosmogony, in which theology and philosophically grounded myth are laid over a scientific base (§2.1, §3.2.13). God in Genesis 1 has only a few carefully limited human traits: intention, command, craftsmanship, blessing and approval. Many chances to tell a story are passed over (§2.3).

### 11. Genesis 1 as Platonic Curriculum

Why would learned authors write science as a story? Gmirkin answers from Plato's own theory of education. He identifies seven modes of discourse about the origin of the world. Five of them first appear in Plato (§3.4):

- **Enchanting myth for children.** Approved by censors, called "legislators of the arts", who strike out anything bad about the gods.
- **Classroom astronomy.** Allowed only insofar as it teaches that the heavenly bodies are gods. In the *Laws* Plato would rather students learn no astronomy than learn atheist physics. Gmirkin calls this "a precursor to what is now commonly called creation science" (§3.4.4).
- **The persuasive preamble to the impiety law.** Laws 10 is "the first known example of legislation that criminalized thought" (§3.4.5). It sets out three required beliefs: the gods exist, they care for humans, and they cannot be bribed.
- **The philosophical argument of the Timaeus itself.** Addressed to the few who can turn right belief into knowledge.
- **Re-education of convicted atheists by the Nocturnal Council.** Those not reformed after five years were executed (§3.4.7).

Genesis 1 fits the classroom mode exactly. It has a great deal of science but none of the reasoning. It is offered for belief to children and ordinary citizens, and tied to the sabbath (§3.4.4). On Gmirkin's reading, its authors were a ruling elite carrying out Plato's plan for a national literature. Their aim was "to promote simple orthodox belief in a supreme cosmic deity as benevolent creator of the kosmos" (§3.5).

### 12. Genesis 1 and the Timaeus: Order, Words and Ideas

Gmirkin's main evidence is threefold.

**First, the Septuagint's vocabulary.** Following Martin Rösel, he notes that the Greek translation renders Hebrew terms with words from the *Timaeus*:

| Hebrew | Septuagint Greek | Timaeus |
|---|---|---|
| *tohu wabohu* | "invisible and unformed" (ἀόρατος καὶ ἀκατασκεύαστος) | 51a "invisible and unshaped" |
| *tov* | καλός | 29a |
| *raqia* | στερέωμα | 31b |
| *tselem*, *demut* | εἰκών, ὁμοιότης | 29b, 30c |
| *yatsar* | ἔπλασεν | 42d |
| *nefesh* | ψυχή | 42e |

The first of these, he says, "can hardly be explained other than as reflecting the language of Timaeus" (§4.1, quoting van der Meer). Rösel thought a free translator had harmonised an older Hebrew text with Plato. Text critics now hold that the Septuagint is a literal translation of a different Hebrew original (Hendel, van der Louw, Tov). A Qumran fragment of Genesis 1:9 agrees with the Greek against the Masoretic Text. Gmirkin concludes that the Platonism was already in the Hebrew, and that the translator was one of the authors (§4.1–4.2).

**Second, the order of events.** Gmirkin rejects Rösel's reading, which followed Philo in taking Genesis 1 as the creation of the noetic world. All three of the *Timaeus*' accounts are about the material world (§4.3). He then lines the two up:

| Step | Genesis | Timaeus |
|---|---|---|
| chaos and darkness | 1:2 | 30a, 52a–53b |
| light; day and night | 1:3–5 | 31b, 39b–d |
| earth and sky separated | 1:6–8 | 32b, 33b |
| land and sea | 1:9–10 | 53a |
| plants | 1:11–12 | 77a |
| days, months, years; sun, moon, stars | 1:14–18 | 37e–40a |
| creatures of water, air and land | 1:20–25 | 40a |
| humankind | 1:26–28 | 41c–42a |
| rest and completion | 2:1–3 | 42e |
| mortal life made by the lesser gods | 2:4–25 | 40d–47e, 69a–92c |

**Third, the ideas.** Gmirkin finds several Platonic ideas in Genesis 1 (§4.4):

- **Teleology.** It runs through the refrain: "And God said", "And it was so", "God saw that it was good". In his words, "Plato's unique contribution was to posit a divine purpose and guidance at every stage of creation" (§4.4.2).
- **Craftsmanship.** In Plato's account, from the *Gorgias*, a craftsman needs materials, a model and excellence in the finished work. Genesis 1 has all three: the chaos, the "image and likeness", and the goodness (§4.4.3).
- **The council speech.** The divine speech of 1:26 matches the Demiurge's speech to the younger gods (§4.4.4).
- **Platonic classification.** Creatures are divided "after their kind" and "likeness". The divisions are into water, air and land creatures, and land animals into tame, wild and creeping (Statesman 263d–266b; Timaeus 40a, 92a). They are Plato's divisions, not Aristotle's (§4.4.5).
- **Names and etymology.** Genesis plays on names: Adam from earth, Eve as "mother of all living". Compare Cratylus 414a, which links γυνή (woman) with γονή (birth) (§4.4.6).

### 13. Genesis 1 as Science: Plato Corrected by Zeno

Genesis did not copy Plato's physics wholesale. Gmirkin argues that where Plato's science was thin, the authors went to Zeno the Stoic and others (§5.2–5.5).

- **No *ex nihilo*.** Genesis 1:1 is a prologue, like Timaeus 27d–29d. It is not a first act of creation. The heavens and earth are made only in verses 6–10. On Parmenides' rule nothing comes from nothing, and "Plato's notion of a creator God thus did not envision creation ex nihilo" (§5.1, §5.3). Gmirkin follows May in dating an explicit doctrine of creation *ex nihilo* to the Church Fathers. He treats 2 Maccabees 7:28 as ambiguous (§5.1.1).
- **The chaos of 1:2.** The Septuagint's "invisible and unformed" comes from Plato's Receptacle. The picture of formless earth under water, dark air above and a stirring wind, with fire not yet lit, matches a stage in Zeno's cosmogony more closely than Plato's. Zeno gives wind and fire an active role (§5.2.1–5.2.3). God is present in the chaos in the form of wind. That fits Zeno, for whom God is everywhere. It conflicts with Timaeus 53b, where the chaos is the universe "in the absence of God" (§5.2.4).
- **A fiery God.** Gmirkin links "God is a devouring fire" (Deut 4:24), and the bush that burned and "was not consumed" (Ex 3:2), to Zeno's creative fire, which burns without consuming. He thinks the Bible's fiery God may owe something to Stoic physics (§5.2.4).
- **Day before the sun.** Day and night come before the sun because Hesiod and Empedocles held that daylight comes from the bright upper air, not the sun (§5.4.1). Plants spring up before the sun appears because Empedocles said trees grew from the earth "before the sun was unfolded around it" (Aetius 5.26.4; §5.4.3).
- **Life from water and earth.** The waters and the earth "bring forth" life by spontaneous generation. Yet God is also said to make the same creatures. Gmirkin reads these as two strands, one scientific and one theological (§5.4.5–5.4.6). The Septuagint calls animals living souls but not plants. That is Zeno's view against Plato's (§5.4.3).
- **The rest on the seventh day.** "There is no biblical tradition in which God resumed his labors on the eighth day" (§5.4.7). The Creator retires, as the Demiurge does at Timaeus 42e, and hands mortal life to his offspring.

The authors also departed from Zeno at three points. They kept a transcendent, bodiless God. They kept the lesser gods. They kept Plato's humanlike Creator who speaks, makes and rejoices (§5.2.5).

### 14. Genesis 2–3: Yahweh Elohim Among the Younger Gods

This is the boldest part of the book. In the *Timaeus*, the Demiurge makes the cosmos and then says to his offspring: "Gods of gods, those works whereof I am framer and father are indissoluble save by my will" (41a). If he made mortals himself they would be equal to gods. So the younger gods must fashion them, so that he may be "blameless in respect of the future wickedness of any one of them" (41c, 42d; §6.2). Gmirkin argues that Genesis 2–3 is the biblical form of this second account:

- **Two gods, not two sources.** The narrator is a different deity, Yahweh Elohim, not the same God under another name (§6.3–6.4). The Septuagint has plain "God" (ὁ θεός) in 2:4–7 and "the Lord God" (κύριος ὁ θεός) only from 2:8.
- **A theogony title.** The heading "the generations of the heavens and the earth" (2:4) is a title for a theogony. Heaven and Earth head the gods in Hesiod and at Timaeus 40e.
- **Two verbs.** Hebrew *bara* (create) is kept for the Creator of Genesis 1. *Yatsar* (fashion), rendered ἔπλασεν, is the work of Yahweh Elohim in 2:7–8, 19 (§6.4).
- **A terrestrial god.** Yahweh Elohim lives in Eden, walks in the evening breeze, and asks "Where are you?". He "possessed only conditional immortality sustained by his eating from the tree of life" (3:22; §6.5).
- **El and his sons.** Gmirkin ties this to the Canaanite pattern. In Deuteronomy 32:8–9, in the Qumran reading, Elyon divides the nations "according to the number of the sons of God", and Yahweh's portion is Jacob. The mapping of the Demiurge and his offspring onto El and his seventy sons let the authors introduce "the novel god of Platonic philosophy in the familiar guise of the supreme Canaanite god El" (§6.3.5).

Gmirkin finds the details of Eden and the Fall drawn from Plato and Hesiod:

- **Material things.** Man is made of earth-bones, flesh and breath (Tim 73e–79e). Fruit trees are "planted ... for the express purpose of serving as food" (Tim 80e). The serpent is the lowest, legless animal (Tim 92a).
- **The golden age.** Nakedness and talk with animals come from Plato's Age of Kronos (Statesman 272a–d).
- **The first woman.** Woman is made second (Tim 91a, Hesiod). Her creation by surgery and "one flesh" echo Aristophanes in the *Symposium* (§6.5).
- **The fall itself.** It comes from the story of Pandora. Its psychology is Platonic, drawn from the *Protagoras*, where a man "knowing the evil to be evil, nevertheless commits it" because pleasure overcomes him, and from the three-part soul (§6.6–6.7). The command to Cain to "master" sin echoes Timaeus 42a–b, where those who master their passions live justly (§6.7).
- **Clothing.** The garments of skins come from the *Protagoras*, where Prometheus gives naked men clothes (§6.6).
- **No immortal soul.** Gmirkin denies that Genesis 2:7 contains the Platonic immortal soul. The "breath of life" fits Zeno's view of perishable breath (§6.4).

Genesis 1 and Genesis 2–3 contradict each other at well-known points. Gmirkin explains this by two teams writing at the same time from the *Timaeus*' outline: philosophers for chapter 1, storytellers for chapters 2–3 (§6.8).

### 15. Eden as Atlantis, the Flood as Critias

Chapter 7 is the most original. Gmirkin argues that the antediluvian world of Genesis follows the *Critias*:

- **Eden as Atlantis.** Poseidon lives with the first earth-born people in his allotted land. There is a temple on a hill with springs, and "the outflowing water they conducted to the sacred grove of Poseidon, which contained trees of all kinds that were of marvelous beauty". There are tame and wild animals, gold and the fiery metal orichalcum (Critias 113c–117b). Gmirkin finds the Mesopotamian parallels to Eden weak by comparison (§7.1–7.2). Genesis keeps the paradise of the Age of Kronos and holds technology back until Genesis 4, the Age of Zeus (§7.3–7.4).
- **The sons of God.** Poseidon "smitten with desire" weds the mortal Cleito (Critias 113d), as the sons of God take the "fair" daughters of men (Gen 6:2). Their offspring are heroes, "warriors of renown" (Gen 6:4), like Atlas and his brothers. Nothing in Genesis 6:1–4 condemns them. The fallen Watchers of Enoch are reception history (§7.6.2–7.6.3).
- **The decline.** The demigods of Atlantis stayed righteous only "so long as the inherited nature of the God remained strong in them". As the divine part thinned through intermarriage, "lawless ambition and power" took over (Critias 120e–121b). The earth was "filled with violence" (Gen 6:11). Only Genesis and the *Critias* make a flood the punishment for ethical decline (§7.6.5).
- **The judgment.** The *Critias* breaks off as Zeus, "the God of gods, who reigns by Law", assembles the gods to pass sentence (121b–c). Gmirkin sees Genesis 6:5–7 as directly dependent on it (§7.6.6). The flood itself reverts to Berossus and Gilgamesh (§7.6.7).
- **Covenant and history.** The kings of Atlantis cut a bull's throat over a pillar inscribed with the laws, mix its blood in a bowl and swear an oath with curses on themselves and their descendants (Critias 119e–120b). Gmirkin calls this the only real parallel anywhere to the blood covenant of Exodus 24. Yahweh as a "god of history" who punishes a broken covenant is, he argues, finally Zeus punishing Atlantis (§7.8).

Gmirkin notes one place where Genesis departs from Plato. In the *Timaeus* the gods, being good, can never will the destruction of what they made. Yet the Lord is "sorry that he had made humankind" and plans to wipe it out (Gen 6:6–7). Gmirkin's explanation is that "The storyteller who authored Gen 6:5–7 apparently did not fully grasp the profoundly philosophical divine ethics laid out in Timaeus". The ark then saves the life God made (§7.6.8).

### 16. Cosmic Monotheism, Terrestrial Polytheism and the Collapse

Gmirkin's last chapter tells a history. Varro distinguished three theologies: civic, mythic and natural (Augustine, *City of God* 6.5). "The Greek natural philosophers were the inventors of monotheism" (§8.3). Xenophanes had one greatest god among other gods. Anaxagoras, "the first scientific creationist", was convicted of atheism. Socrates died partly for Anaxagoras' views (§8.3.1–8.3.4). Plato protected philosophy in two ways. He made the civic gods the children of the Demiurge, and he kept the worship of the civic gods compulsory. "Plato's Timaeus was the first text to combine a cosmic monotheism based on philosophy with a terrestrial polytheism based on civic religion and popular myth" (§8.3.5). Even the Demiurge stands under the Form of the Good. Goodness is a standard the gods meet, not whatever they command (§8.4.3.2).

Genesis carries out this program. Abraham worships El Elyon, "creator of heaven and earth", at Melchizedek's temple alongside a Canaanite king. Joseph marries the daughter of an Egyptian priest. No foreign god is condemned, and violence is always human (§8.6). Exodus–Joshua reverses it:

- **Monolatry.** Exclusive worship of Yahweh becomes the centre of the covenant.
- **War on other gods.** The other gods are wicked, and "Yahweh is a warrior" (Ex 15:3).
- **Command ethics.** Good is whatever the jealous god commands (§8.7).
- **Yahweh as Creator.** The fourth commandment is the first text to make Yahweh the Creator of Genesis 1 (Ex 20:11). The ban on images borrows the Demiurge's invisibility for a local god (§8.8.2–8.8.3).

Gmirkin's account of what followed is political:

- **Rule by priests.** A hereditary high priest took the place of Plato's elected philosophers at the head of a council modelled on the Nocturnal Council.
- **The Torah replaced the Laws.** The *Laws* was never adopted as the rulers' textbook (§8.9).
- **Monotheism in the full sense** arrived only with Second Isaiah and second-century texts. That monotheism denied that the other gods existed (§8.1.6).
- **Philosophy lost.** The philosophers' Creator "was effectively subjugated and overthrown by the authors of Exodus–Joshua to become the terrestrial voice of petty nationalistic and cultic interests", and "Philosophical rule was effectively decapitated" (§8.10).

### 17. The Two Books Together

The books never mention each other, but their subjects overlap at nearly every point.

| Question | O'Brien | Gmirkin |
|---|---|---|
| First text to read Genesis through the *Timaeus* | Philo (*De Opificio Mundi*) | Genesis 1 itself, about 270 BCE |
| A model or pattern in creation | "clearly not a Jewish or Christian concept"; it enters through Philo and Origen (pp. 302, 305) | already in Genesis 1:26 ("image and likeness") and the Septuagint's ὁμοιότης |
| Creation from nothing | Greeks never mentioned it (p. 24); O'Neill holds Philo taught it and the New Testament assumed it (p. 57) | absent from Genesis and Plato; a later patristic doctrine (§5.1.1) |
| Who makes mortal man | Plato's Young Gods; Philo's helpers of "Let us make man"; the Gnostic Archons | Yahweh Elohim as one of the younger gods (Gen 2:7) |
| Demiurge and highest god | separated from Numenius on; ends in antagonism with the Gnostics | Genesis keeps them distinct; Exodus merges Yahweh with the Creator |
| Source of evil | matter (Plutarch, Numenius), a fallen Aeon (Valentinus), free will (Origen) | Plato's delegation to the lesser gods, retold in Genesis 2–3 |

Read together, each book sheds light on the other.

- **The "Let us make man" problem has a long history.** O'Brien shows Philo struggling with the plural, and rabbis, Gnostics and Christians all assigning parts of man to helpers (pp. 68–9, 206–7). Gmirkin's answer is that the plural is exactly what a reader of Timaeus 41 would expect (§5.4.6). If so, Philo was not imposing Plato on Moses. He was recovering what the authors put there.
- **The Demiurge's demotion runs in parallel.** O'Brien traces it in Greek thought, from Plato's honoured craftsman to Ialdabaoth. Gmirkin argues that the reverse happened in Israel: a lesser terrestrial god was promoted to supreme Creator. The Gnostic myth, which makes the God of the Old Testament an ignorant lower demiurge, then reads as a claim that Genesis 2 is about a different god from Genesis 1. That is Gmirkin's claim as well, made for opposite reasons. O'Brien notes that Marcion used Yahweh's ignorance in Eden this way, and Gmirkin cites the same point (§6.6).
- **The two authors weigh the evidence differently.** O'Brien is cautious about attributions. He doubts Philo invented the noetic cosmos and gives Calcidius and Proclus less weight than Eusebius. Gmirkin builds a long chain on a contested date. His argument holds only if the Pentateuch was written about 270 BCE. Several of his parallels, such as Eden and Atlantis or the Fall and the *Protagoras*, are only general resemblances, not word-for-word borrowings. The Septuagint vocabulary is his firmest evidence. The case for the Hebrew depends on the text-critical view that the Greek is literal.
- **Both books ask why the craftsman god was useful.** For O'Brien, the "likely story" of the *Timaeus* was flexible enough for Jews, Christians, Gnostics and pagans alike (pp. 307–8). For Gmirkin it was useful to rulers. Plato meant the story of the Demiurge as one of the myths that shape citizens' beliefs, and his rules for acceptable myths fit it exactly (§8.4.2).

### Conclusion

O'Brien's book is a history of a philosophical figure. It follows the Demiurge through every system that used him: the craftsman of the *Timaeus*, Philo's architect with his cutting Logos, Plutarch's passive Osiris, Maximus' nodding Zeus, Numenius' divided Second God, the Hermetic second Mind, the ignorant Ialdabaoth, Origen's Son and the lower intellect of Proclus. In each case O'Brien asks the same five questions about the first principle, matter, the Forms, evil and limits. His main result is that the Demiurge lost rank as later thinkers sought to keep the highest god further from matter. Plotinus ended this by giving up deliberate making altogether.

Gmirkin's book is an argument about Genesis. He claims that Genesis 1 is a Platonic cosmogony with Stoic physics. Genesis 2–3 is the *Timaeus*' second creation, in which a lesser god makes mortals, and Genesis 4–11 follows the *Critias* from paradise to flood. The God of Genesis 1 is the Demiurge under the name of El. If Gmirkin is right, the separation O'Brien traces in Greek thought appears in the Bible from its opening pages. Exodus then reverses it by making the local god the Creator, and later Jewish and Christian readers take the merged figure as the original one.
