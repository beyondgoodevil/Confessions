**Divine illumination**: the view that the human mind needs special help from God to complete some part of *ordinary* knowing. Not mystical experience or revelation, but everyday knowledge, like seeing that 7 + 3 = 10 must be so. Augustine is the main source. Its decline around 1300 is a turning point toward naturalism in theories of mind.

The classical cousin of [[TAG]] and of [[The Augustinian Proof]].

## 1. Background

- **Plato**:
    - *Republic*: the Good is like the sun; it gives both being and being-known. See [[The Good in Plato]].
    - *Meno*: a slave boy "sees" a geometric proof for himself, which Plato explains as recollection.
    - *Phaedo*: no two sticks are perfectly equal, yet we know Equality. So the knowledge didn't come from the senses.
- **Socrates' sign** (*Apology* 31d): a divine voice that held him back. Divine guidance, but only moral and only negative.
- **Aristotle** (*De Anima* III.5): a separate, eternal **active intellect** that makes things intelligible, like light makes colours visible. Divine (Alexander, Avicenna, Averroes) or part of each soul (Themistius, Aquinas)? Disputed ever since.

## 2. Augustine

Key texts:

- *Confessions* IV.15: the mind needs enlightening from outside, because it is not itself the nature of truth.
- *Confessions* XII.25: when you and I both see that something is true, I don't see it in your mind or you in mine. We both see it in the **unchangeable truth above our minds**.
- *De Magistro* 12.40: a teacher doesn't really teach. His words prompt; the student is taught by the things themselves, shown by the inner Teacher (Christ).
- *83 Questions* q. 9: the senses can't give pure truth, because what is always changing can't be known.

The argument underneath, as I reconstruct it:

1. We know some truths that are necessary, unchanging and common to all minds.
2. Nothing we sense is necessary or unchanging. Our minds are changeable too.
3. So the source of these truths is above both: unchangeable Truth.
4. That is God.

## 3. What Illumination Explains

Roughly sorted:

| Explains | How |
|---|---|
| **Certainty** | turns true belief into knowledge: you *see* that it must be so |
| **Necessary truths** | logic, maths, ethics: grasped in the divine light, not abstracted from senses |
| **Concepts / essences** | the senses give appearances; grasping what a thing really is needs more |

- Best reading: illumination is mainly about **justification**, not delivering information. The mind forms its own beliefs; the light lets it see they're true.
- It's **ongoing**, not a one-time gift at birth: "truth walks with me".
- In special cases (Scripture) it does give content. But that's revelation, a different thing.

## 4. The Medieval Debate

| Who | Position |
|---|---|
| **Bonaventure** | certainty needs unchangeability, found only in the eternal ideas in God's mind. We reach them only by illumination |
| **Roger Marston** | God is a separate active intellect *and* each of us has one too |
| **Peter John Olivi** | raises objections that seem fatal, then accepts the theory out of respect (a sign it was weakening) |
| **Aquinas** | we know all things "in the eternal reasons" (*ST* I.84.5), but only in the sense that our natural intellectual light is a **created likeness** of the uncreated light. No further special illumination needed. First principles are self-evident to that natural light |
| **Henry of Ghent** | knowing *that* something is true needs no help; knowing *what* a thing essentially is does. Proposed God is the active intellect |
| **Duns Scotus** | rejects it. If any part of the process is fallible, divine help can't make the whole certain. And we don't need it: self-evident truths, once we have the concepts, are known infallibly by nature |

One image: for Aquinas the light is a deep well inside us; for the Franciscans it falls like rain, drop by drop.

**After Scotus** it was rarely taken seriously. But Descartes's innate "seeds of truth" are close to Aquinas, and Malebranche ("we see all things in God") revived it outright.

## 5. Why It Declined, and Whether That Was Progress

- Scotus's argument is the key move: the mind can do it on its own.
- Arguably the first great victory for naturalism in the theory of mind.
- But the question it answered didn't go away. *How* can a changeable brain grasp necessary truths? Every account since (rationalism, Kant's categories, conventionalism, evolution) is an answer to the problem illumination addressed. See [[Philosophy of Logic: What Grounds the Laws of Logic]] sec. 3, where most of the naturalistic answers turn out circular.

## 6. Comparisons

*Mine.*

**With the Orthodox *nous***

- Similar: true knowledge involves a light from God, and the mind sees in it.
- Different emphasis:
    - Augustine's illumination is for *ordinary* knowledge: maths, logic. The *nous* tradition is about knowing *God*, by a purified heart.
    - In the East the light is **uncreated** energy, God Himself. In Aquinas it becomes created (a likeness). Augustine is ambiguous. That's the essence–energies question in another form.
    - The East ties illumination to **purification**: the light is seen by the pure in heart (Matt 5:8). Augustine's is given to everyone who knows anything.
- See [[Nous]], [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].

**With the Logos**

- "The true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world" (John 1:9). The Fathers read this as the Logos giving reason to every human mind. That's the common root.
- Maximus's logoi: we know things because their logoi are in the Logos, and our reason is an image of His. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].

**With TAG**

- Same structure: necessary, universal truths can't be grounded in changeable minds or matter, so they point to God.
- Augustine argues it as a direct inference. TAG says the unbeliever already relies on it. See [[TAG]], [[Why Only the Orthodox Paradigm Can Account for Knowledge]].
