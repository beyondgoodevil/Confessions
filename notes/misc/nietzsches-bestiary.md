> "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know, my people doth not consider." (Isaiah 1:3)

> "The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid; and the calf and the young lion and the fatling together; and a little child shall lead them." (Isaiah 11:6)

> "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Juda... hath prevailed... And I beheld, and, lo, in the midst of the throne... stood a Lamb as it had been slain." (Revelation 5:5–6)

Nietzsche's books are a zoo: camels, lions, apes, asses, cows, spiders, snakes, eagles, moles, polyps, lambs and the notorious "blond beast". Philosophers usually treat images like these as the wrapping paper around the real arguments. The editors of *A Nietzschean Bestiary* (2004), following Michèle Le Dœuff, argue the opposite: N.'s **"animal imaginary"** *is* part of the argument. It's how he does what he set out to do in *The Gay Science*: "de-deify" nature and "naturalise" the human being. And the animals cut both ways. They are where we came from, what we've been tamed out of, and what we may need to become again. Here's how it works:

N. = Nietzsche, Z = *Zarathustra*, GM = *Genealogy*, TI = *Twilight*, A = *Antichrist*, UM = *Untimely Meditations*, GS = *Gay Science*.

## 1. Man Placed Back Among the Animals

- **A 14**: "We no longer derive man from 'the spirit' or 'the deity'; we have placed him back among the animals." He's the strongest animal because the most cunning. But "man is by no means the crown of creation". Relatively speaking he is "the most bungled of all the animals, the sickliest", the one that has strayed most dangerously from its instincts. "But for all that, he is of course the most *interesting*."
- **GS 224, "Animals as critics"**: "I fear that the animals consider man as a being like themselves that has lost in a most dangerous way its sound animal common sense", "the insane animal, the laughing animal, the weeping animal, the miserable animal".
- **UM II 1, the cattle**: "Consider the cattle, grazing as they pass you by: they do not know what is meant by yesterday or today." Man envies their happiness. He asks the animal why it doesn't tell him about its happiness, and the animal would like to answer, "The reason is I always forget what I was going to say", but forgets that too. **Forgetting** is the animal's gift. Man is the animal that can't forget, and history, memory and the bad conscience all start there.
- **GM II 1**: "To breed an animal that is allowed to promise: is this not precisely the paradoxical task that nature has set for itself with respect to human beings?" A promise needs memory, so the forgetful animal has to have a memory *burned* into it.

**Lemm's thesis.** Vanessa Lemm (*Nietzsche's Animal Philosophy*, 2009) builds a whole reading on this:
- **Continuity.** Human life is not an island. A form of life cut off from other life declines. N.'s picture is closer to Darwin's than to the tradition's.
- **Culture vs civilisation.** For N. these are opposites (a late note: "Civilization and culture: an antagonism"). *Civilisation* tames and breeds the animal: it is moral "improvement" that weakens. *Culture* cultivates the animal and draws on its dreams, passions and forgetfulness.
- **The overhuman is not less animal but more.** Becoming overhuman depends on openness to our animality, which "resists" in us. Man is "a rope, tied between the animal and the overhuman", and the rope doesn't run one way.
- **Forgetting and forgiving.** Lemm argues that N. sets animal forgetfulness and gift-giving against Christian forgiveness, which in his view fails to break the cycle of revenge because it gives animal forgetting no role. (I come back to this.)

## 2. The Three Metamorphoses (Z I)

"Of three metamorphoses of the spirit I tell you: how the spirit becomes a **camel**; and the camel, a **lion**; and the lion, finally, a **child**."

1. **Camel.** The "strong reverent spirit that would bear much" kneels to be loaded. What's most difficult? Humbling yourself to wound your pride; feeding on the "acorns and grass of knowledge"; "loving those who despise us". Loaded, it hurries into the desert.
2. **Lion.** In the loneliest desert the spirit becomes a lion who wants to be master in his own desert. He fights the **great dragon**, whose name is **"Thou shalt"**, covered in golden scales, each one shining with a "thou shalt". The dragon says: "All value has long been created, and I am all created value." The lion says **"I will"**. He can't create new values, but he can create the freedom for it: "a sacred 'No' even to duty". That takes a beast of **prey**.
3. **Child.** "The child is innocence and forgetting, a new beginning, a game, a self-propelled wheel, a first movement, a sacred 'Yes'."

**Readings**
- **Charles Taylor** defends the **camel**. The usual reading looks down on its reverence and admires the lion's boldness. But the camel is the "first thing" every "last thing" grows out of. Without its long obedience there's nothing for the lion to be free *of*. (Compare BGE 188: all freedom grew under the "tyranny" of rules.)
- **Higgins** adds a **fourth** stage from Part IV: the **ass**. The child's innocence is informed by folly.
- The sources for the scheme include Hesiod's and Ovid's ages of man (Richard Perkins's appendix in the *Bestiary*).

## 3. The Ape

- Prologue 3: "What is the ape to man? A laughingstock or a painful embarrassment. And man shall be just that for the overman." And "even now, too, man is more ape than any ape."
- In "On Passing By" (Z III), a foaming fool who mimics Zarathustra's speech is called **"Zarathustra's ape"**.
- **Peter Groff**: the ape is the animal of **mimesis**, imitation. That's what N. despises in it, and in us. The danger after Darwin isn't that we came from apes but that we stay apes: copying, aping our gods (a late note: "Do you merely want to be the ape of your god?"). The cure is the **child**, who doesn't imitate but plays. Groff notes an odd 1882 fragment in which an ideal "ape" could one day stand before humanity as a goal.

## 4. Zarathustra's Animals

**Eagle and serpent** (Prologue 10)
- At noon an eagle soars in wide circles, "and on him there hung a serpent, not like prey but like a friend: for she kept herself wound around his neck." "These are my animals": "the proudest animal under the sun and the wisest animal under the sun".
- **Pride and wisdom**, height and depth, sky and earth, **reconciled**. The natural predator and prey are friends. Later they speak the doctrine of eternal return for him ("The Convalescent").

**The dog** ("On the Vision and the Riddle")
- **Gary Shapiro**: the dog howling at the gateway is the real actor in that scene. Dogs are what we've domesticated, and domesticating them domesticated us. To take in eternal return we must give up "the dog we call 'ego'".

**The tarantula and the spider**
- "On the Tarantulas" (Z II): preachers of equality are tarantulas whose bite is revenge. **Alan Schrift**: N. is more afraid of spiders than admiring. The spider is both artist and metaphysician, spinning webs of concepts that trap and poison.

**The snake**
- The black snake hanging from the shepherd's mouth, the serpent on the eagle's neck, the serpent on Zarathustra's staff around the sun. **Nickolas Pappas**: the snake doesn't "slither straight to an index card" marked enemy, knowledge or temptation. It keeps its biblical and Greek meanings and turns them round, but it stays **masculine**: an eternity without maternity.

**The cow**
- In Part IV the **voluntary beggar**, a rich man who gave everything away, preaches to cows on a hill: "Except we turn back and become as cows, we shall not enter the kingdom of heaven" (a parody of Matt 18:3). We must learn one thing from them, **chewing the cud**, or we won't get rid of our great melancholy, "but today that is called nausea". (Note the other Gospel echo: what would it profit a man to gain the whole world and not learn this?) **Tracey Stark** links it to Cadmus, led by a cow to the site of his city.

**The ass**
- The ass carries the higher men's burdens through Part IV, and in "The Awakening" they worship it. The ugliest man's litany begins "Amen! And praise and honor and wisdom and thanks and glory and strength be to our god", and the refrain is always "But the ass brayed: Yea-Yuh." The ass "took upon himself the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7), "never says No", and "always says Yea to the world he created".
- **Kathleen Higgins**: N. is drawing on the medieval **Feast of the Ass** (late 11th to 16th c., the weeks after Christmas). Sub-deacons played bishops, the clergy brayed at Mass and the "Prose of the Ass" was sung. At Beauvais a girl with a child on an ass re-enacted the **Flight into Egypt**, and the priest brayed three times instead of *Ite, missa est*. BGE 8 quotes its first line: *Adventavit asinus, pulcher et fortissimus*, "the ass arrived, beautiful and most brave".
- The German bray is *I-A*, which sounds like ***Ja***, yes, the ass's "Amen". So the ass is a parody of affirmation: the yes that can't say no. Higgins reads the episode as **comic self-overcoming** rather than plain contempt.

## 5. The Beasts of Prey

**The blond beast** (GM I 11)
- At the base of all noble races "is the predator, the magnificent **blond beast** roaming about lustily after prey and victory". From time to time this hidden ground needs discharging: "the animal must emerge once more, must return to the wilderness: Roman, Arabic, Teutonic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Scandinavian Vikings". They come back from murder, arson, rape and torture "as if they had merely pulled some student prank" (Del Caro).
- **Gerd Schank** (for the *Nietzsche-Wörterbuch*): "blond" and "blond beast" occur only **thirteen times**, all from 1885–88. "Blond beast" mainly means the **lion** (the "blond-haired lion-monster" of Z IV), plus the "achievers" of agonal culture and the state-founding "pack of blond beasts of prey" (GM II 17). The racial use is in GM I 5 ("blond, namely Aryan conqueror race"), and the list in I 11 includes Arabs and Japanese. Schank concludes it isn't meant to lionise Germans.
- **Daniel Conway**: don't soften it. These are humans who "act like wild animals toward other human beings". But they're also N.'s **missing link**: by domesticating their captives they domesticated themselves almost to extinction.

**Lambs and birds of prey** (GM I 13)
- "It does not seem strange that lambs bear a grudge against the great birds of prey: only this is no reason to hold it against the great birds of prey that they snatch themselves little lambs." The lambs call the birds evil and themselves good. The birds might say: "we do not bear a grudge against them, these good lambs, in fact we love them: nothing is tastier than a tender lamb."
- To demand that strength not express itself as strength is as absurd as demanding that weakness express itself as strength. The "subject" who could *choose* to be a lamb is a fiction.

**Sea animals forced onto land** (GM II 16)
- The origin of the **bad conscience**: when "semi-animals" adapted to wilderness, war and roaming were suddenly locked inside society and peace, it was like "aquatic animals" forced "either to become land animals or to perish". Their instincts were "disconnected". They were "reduced to thinking, inferring, calculating", to "their most feeble and most mistake-prone organ", consciousness. So the instincts turned inward.

**The Church's menagerie** (TI "The Improvers of Mankind" 2)
- "To call the taming of an animal its 'improvement' sounds almost like a joke." Beasts in menageries aren't improved but weakened, made sick by fear, pain and hunger. In the early Middle Ages "the church was indeed, above all, a menagerie": it hunted the finest "blond beasts", and the "improved" Teuton came out of the monastery "like a caricature of man", a "sinner" in a cage, "malevolent against himself". "In short, a 'Christian.'"

**The laughing lion** (Z IV "The Sign")
- The book ends with a lion that laughs, surrounded by doves. "The lion came, my children are near."
- **Paul Loeb** argues this ending isn't gentle. The lions are Zarathustra's returning disciples, in leonine form, come to drive off the higher men. Their laughter isn't the child's innocent gaiety but delight in destruction. Loeb also argues that the events of Part IV fit chronologically *before* the end of Part III. A footnote notes a suggestion from Jung's seminar: the image may come from the winged lion and pigeons of St Mark's in Venice.

## 7. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What N. sees**
- **GS 224 is biblical.** "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know" (Isa 1:3). The prophets got there first: the animals keep their nature, and only man has lost his "common sense". And Ecclesiastes was "placing man back among the animals" long before A 14: "that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts... so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast" (Eccl 3:19). The Bible doesn't flatter us either.
- **Forgetting.** UM II 1 is beautiful and true. We are the animal tortured by "it was". But the answer isn't to become cattle. It's the **forgiveness** Lemm sets against animal forgetting. On her reading N. thinks forgiving can't break the cycle of revenge. The Gospel claims something stranger: God "will remember their sin no more" (Jer 31:34), and that kind of "forgetting" is not animal oblivion but **re-creation**. Animal forgetting can't heal a wound. It only stops feeling it.
- **The ass festival.** Higgins is right that it's funny and that the medieval Church *let it happen*. That tells you something. A faith secure enough to bray at its own Mass for a week a year is not the brittle "menagerie" of TI. And the Church already knew that the ass is the animal of God's surprises: **Balaam's ass** sees the angel the prophet can't (Num 22:23–33), and Christ enters Jerusalem "meek, and sitting upon an ass" (Matt 21:5). The higher men worship the ass. The Church honours the ass because **it carried Christ**.

**Where he goes wrong**
- **Lambs and birds of prey, and the Lamb.** GM I 13 is N. at his sharpest: the lambs' morality is their revenge on the hawks. But Revelation 5 has already staged the scene and reversed it. John is told to look at "the **Lion** of the tribe of Juda", turns, and sees "a **Lamb** as it had been slain". The Lion *is* the Lamb. Strength in the Gospel is not denied but **redefined** as self-giving. And the Lamb doesn't resent the birds of prey. He dies for them. That breaks N.'s dichotomy: here is a strength that doesn't "express itself" as overpowering and isn't weakness either.
- **The menagerie and the desert.** TI's caged Teuton is a real type: the sick, self-hating "convert". But look at how the Church actually told its animal stories. **St Gerasimos of the Jordan** pulls a thorn from a lion's paw. The lion stays to serve the monastery, and when the old man dies it lies down on his grave and dies there. A lion also helped St Zosimas dig the grave of **St Mary of Egypt**. **St Seraphim of Sarov** fed a bear from his hand. This isn't taming by fear, pain and hunger (TI's menagerie). It's what Isaiah 11 promised: "the wolf also shall dwell with the lamb... and a **little child** shall lead them." Isaiah ends with the child too, but the child *leads the lion* instead of replacing it. The saint doesn't kill the animal in himself or let it loose. He **restores Eden's peace**.
- **The blond beast.** Schank and Conway are both right: the image isn't a racial programme, and it's still a celebration of murder, arson and rape as "student pranks". The Bible's version of a king becoming a beast is **Nebuchadnezzar**, who in his pride "did eat grass as oxen... till his hairs were grown like eagles' feathers, and his nails like birds' claws" (Dan 4:33). The beast is not the noble ground under culture. It is what pride *becomes*. He's healed when he "lifted up mine eyes unto heaven, and mine understanding returned unto me" (Dan 4:34).

**Man as microcosm**
- The Fathers have a better middle term than N.'s "rope". For St Gregory of Nyssa and St Maximus, man is a **microcosm** and **mediator**: animal by body, spiritual by soul, set between the creatures and God to gather the whole creation and offer it back. That's why Adam **names** the animals (Gen 2:19), and why "the whole creation groaneth" waiting for the sons of God (Rom 8:19–22). The animals don't need us to become them. They need us to become fully human, so that they can be set free too. See [[Image and Likeness]] and [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
- The Church even had its own bestiary: the **Physiologus** (Alexandria, early centuries AD), the ancestor of all medieval bestiaries. Its lion's cubs are born dead and wake on the third day when their father breathes on them. N. wrote a bestiary of nature without its Creator. The older one read every animal as a sign of Him. "Be ye therefore wise as **serpents**, and harmless as **doves**" (Matt 10:16): Zarathustra's eagle and serpent, his lion and doves, are all already in that one verse.
