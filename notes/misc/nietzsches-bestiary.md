> "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know, my people doth not consider." (Isaiah 1:3)

> "The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid; and the calf and the young lion and the fatling together; and a little child shall lead them." (Isaiah 11:6)

> "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Juda... hath prevailed... And I beheld, and, lo, in the midst of the throne... stood a Lamb as it had been slain." (Revelation 5:5–6)

Nietzsche's books are a zoo: camels, lions, apes, asses, cows, spiders, snakes, eagles, moles, polyps, cats, lizards, lambs and the notorious "blond beast". Philosophers usually treat images like these as the wrapping paper around the real arguments. The editors of *A Nietzschean Bestiary* (2004) start from Michèle Le Dœuff's question: why does nobody write the history of a philosopher's "image-album"? They argue that N.'s **"animal imaginary"** *is* part of the argument. It's how he does what he set out to do in *The Gay Science*: "de-deify" nature and "naturalise" the human being. The animals cut both ways. They are where we came from, what we've been tamed out of, and what we may need to become again. Here's how it works:

N. = Nietzsche, Z = *Zarathustra*, GM = *Genealogy*, TI = *Twilight*, A = *Antichrist*, UM = *Untimely Meditations*, GS = *Gay Science*, BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, HH = *Human, All Too Human* (AOM and WS are its second volume), D = *Daybreak*, EH = *Ecce Homo*.

The *Bestiary* is laid out like a zoo, and so is this note:
1. **"Lowly" origins**: ape, camel, polyp.
2. **Zarathustra's animals**: dog, spider, snake, bird, cow, ass.
3. **Beasts of prey**: lion, blond beast, beasts of prey.
4. **Human animals**: woman, satyr, overhuman.
5. **Animal Nietzsche**: mole, cat, lizard. The animals he identified *himself* with.

## 1. Man Placed Back Among the Animals

- **A 14**: "We no longer derive man from 'the spirit' or 'the deity'; we have placed him back among the animals." He's the strongest animal because the most cunning. But "man is by no means the crown of creation". Relatively speaking he is "the most bungled of all the animals, the sickliest", the one that has strayed most dangerously from its instincts. "But for all that, he is of course the most *interesting*."
- **GS 224, "Animals as critics"**: "I fear that the animals consider man as a being like themselves that has lost in a most dangerous way its sound animal common sense", "the insane animal, the laughing animal, the weeping animal, the miserable animal".
- **UM II 1, the cattle**: "Consider the cattle, grazing as they pass you by: they do not know what is meant by yesterday or today." Man envies their happiness. He asks the animal why it doesn't tell him about its happiness, and the animal would like to answer, "The reason is I always forget what I was going to say", but forgets that too. **Forgetting** is the animal's gift. Man is the animal that can't forget, and history, memory and the bad conscience all start there. (Lemm notes the image comes from a poet, Leopardi.)
- **GM II 1**: "To breed an animal that is allowed to promise: is this not precisely the paradoxical task that nature has set for itself with respect to human beings?" A promise needs memory, so the forgetful animal has to have a memory *burned* into it.
- **BGE 230**: the task is "to translate humankind back into nature", to read again "the terrible basic text of *homo natura*" under the paint, deaf "to the luring songs of old metaphysical bird-catchers" fluting "you are more! you are higher!" (Del Caro).

### Feral philosophy (Ralph Acampora)

The usual reading gives N. only two kinds of animal: the **tame** herd animal (*Sklaventiere*) and the **wild** beast of prey (*Raubtiere*). Kaufmann concluded N. must want to transcend animality altogether, since he wants neither "animal conformity" nor "super-brutes". Acampora says this is a false dilemma. There is a third, excluded middle: the **feral**, the tame animal gone wild, like a stray dog or an alley cat, at home both in the house and the wilderness.

- **Two projects.** Following T. J. Reed: a *diagnostic* one (reveal the animal under the theological and humanist paint) and a *therapeutic* one (restore wildness to the domesticated human).
- **The philosopher is feral.** He is neither herdsman nor savage but a "deserter" from his society who comes back to educate it. TI "Maxims" 3: "To live alone one must be a beast or a god, says Aristotle. Leaving out the third case: one must be both—a philosopher."
- **Breeding is not taming.** N. distinguishes them (WP 398). Taming roots out the drives; breeding or training ("training without taming", Graham Parkes's phrase) directs them. So the **beast of burden outranks the herd animal**: the camel's discipline builds culture, the "last man's" comfort doesn't.
- **The rope is never cut.** "Man is a rope, tied between beast and overman" (Z Prologue 4). Acampora's formula: animality without humanity may be blind, but humanity without animality is empty.

### Lemm's animal philosophy

Vanessa Lemm (*Nietzsche's Animal Philosophy*, 2009) builds the most systematic reading. Schematically: **forgetting belongs to the animal, memory to the human, the promise to the overhuman**, and the three aren't stages but forces always at war inside us.

1. **Culture against civilisation.** A late note: "Civilization and culture: an antagonism." *Civilisation* is **taming and breeding** (*Zähmung*, *Züchtung*): moral "improvement" by imposing memory on the forgetful animal. *Culture* is **cultivation** (*Bildung*): it remembers the animal and draws on its dreams, passions and forgetting. "The great moments of culture have always been, morally speaking, times of corruption" (note of 1888). Greece is culture's exemplar, Rome civilisation's.
2. **Truth as Circe.** HH 519: "Error has made animals into men; might truth then be capable of making man back into an animal?" Civilisation is the error that made us human; culture is the truth that can turn us back.
3. **The "over-animal" is the opposite of the overhuman.** HH 40, "The *Ueber-Thier*": "The beast in us wants to be deceived; morality is an unavoidable lie to keep it from tearing us to pieces." Morality makes man think himself "something higher", and so he hates whatever is closer to the animal (Lemm adds: that's why slaves were treated as things). Lemm's point is about the little word *über*. In "over-animal" it is **vertical**: man set above the animals, which ends the contest. In "overhuman" it is **horizontal**, like a bridge or a wind blowing over (*Über-wind-ung*): just enough distance for a face-to-face contest with the animal. Hence GS 314, "New domestic animals": "I want to have my lion and eagle near me so that I always have hints and omens that help me to know how great or small my strength is." And a note: the great human being "is strong enough to make of these beasts his domestic animals" (KSA 13:16[7]).
4. **Life is dreaming.** A notebook defines a living being as "a being with the habit of ordering in dreaming" (KSA 11:34[247]). In dreams and passion the animal comes back (D 312), "with its savage grimaces".
5. **The genius of culture is a centaur.** HH 241: he uses lies, force and self-interest so skilfully he looks demonic, "but his goals, shining through here and there, are great and good. He is a centaur, half beast, half human, and has angelic wings on his head besides."
6. **Two promises.** Civilisation's promise is the "memory of the will", made with blood and torture ("How much blood and horror lies at the basis of all 'good things'!", GM II 3). The **sovereign individual's** promise is different: "The proud knowledge of the extraordinary privilege of responsibility... has settled in him to his uttermost depths and has become instinct, the dominating instinct" (GM II 2, Del Caro). Responsibility has become *animal*, unconscious. Lemm reads it as a counter-promise, agonistic and anti-institutional, and compares it to Sartre's Marathon messenger who arrives dead.
7. **Gift-giving against forgiving.** Lemm's ch. 4 argues that N. rejects Christian forgiveness because it works through judgment, guilt and debt, and so keeps revenge alive. The noble type forgets instead: Mirabeau "had no memory for insults... and was not able to forgive only because he—forgot" (GM I 10). The **gift-giving virtue** of Z I is, she argues, an *animal* virtue, because it needs forgetting: "I love the one whose soul is overfull so that he forgets himself" (Prologue 4). Zarathustra's honey comes from his animals. She sets it beside Derrida (a pure gift must be forgotten) against Arendt (forgiveness as memory).
8. **Counterhistory.** In UM II forgetting is *prior* to memory: "the unhistorical and the historical are necessary in equal measure". Monumental, antiquarian and critical history all forget something. History in the service of life is an **art**, not a science, and N.'s 1886 prefaces are examples: he rewrites his own past.
9. **Animal silence and "intuited metaphors".** In "On Truth and Lies" N. distinguishes *Anschauungsmetapher* (first impressions, pictures, shared with animals) from concepts (pictures dissolved into schemes). Animals don't lack truth, they have a **silent** truth; the gazing cow forgets what it wanted to say and leaves man "wondering". And "insects or birds perceive a quite different world", with no criterion of "correct perception".
10. **Affirmative biopolitics.** Her conclusion turns this against Foucault's "biopolitics" (power over life as a species). Continuity with the animals undermines the division of life into species and races; it doesn't feed it.

## 2. The Three Metamorphoses (Z I)

"Of three metamorphoses of the spirit I tell you: how the spirit becomes a **camel**; and the camel, a **lion**; and the lion, finally, a **child**."

1. **Camel.** The "strong reverent spirit that would bear much" kneels to be loaded. What's most difficult? Humbling yourself to wound your pride; feeding on the "acorns and grass of knowledge"; "stepping into filthy waters when they are the waters of truth, and not repulsing cold frogs and hot toads"; "loving those who despise us". Loaded, it hurries into the desert.
2. **Lion.** In the loneliest desert the spirit becomes a lion who wants to be master in his own desert. He fights the **great dragon**, whose name is **"Thou shalt"**, covered in golden scales, each one shining with a "thou shalt". The dragon says: "All value has long been created, and I am all created value." The lion says **"I will"**. He can't create new values, but he can create the freedom for it: "a sacred 'No' even to duty". That takes a beast of **prey**.
3. **Child.** "The child is innocence and forgetting, a new beginning, a game, a self-propelled wheel, a first movement, a sacred 'Yes'."

**Readings**
- **Charles Taylor** defends the **camel**. The key word is *Ehrfurcht*, "reverence", literally **honour + fear**. The camel isn't a drudge doing daily chores; it *chooses* the heaviest load so that "its strength becomes joyful". Taylor traces this back to HH: N. "freezes" the myth of instant genius (Beethoven's notebooks show his music pieced together slowly; the great artist's real gift is ruthless **judgment** of his own work). And WS 140, "**Dancing in chains**": every Greek artist imposed a new constraint on himself, "making things hard for oneself, and then spreading an illusion of facility over this". The camel's joy comes *while* carrying, not after. Without its obedience there's nothing for the lion to be free *of* (compare BGE 188).
- **Higgins** adds a **fourth** stage from Part IV: the **ass**. The child's innocence is informed by folly.
- **Richard Perkins** finds the **source**. A notebook entry of 1880: "'At 40 years one is a camel, at 70 an ape'—Spaniard." That's Baltasar Gracián's seven ages: "At twenty peacocks, at thirty lions, at forty camels, at fifty serpents, at sixty dogs, at seventy apes, and at eighty nothing at all." Behind Gracián is a fable by **Jaime Juan Falcón** (16th c.): God gave the ass, the dog and the ape thirty years each, and they begged to have fewer; man thought his thirty too few and asked for their leftover years. So after thirty we live as an overworked **ass**, a snarling **dog** guarding its things, and a doting **ape**. (Grimm has a version; it goes back to Aesop and the Midrash.) N. **inverts** the fable: the ass becomes the noblest beast of burden, the camel; the dog the noblest predator, the lion; the senile ape the **child**. The degenerating ages of man become the regenerating ascent to the overman. Perkins thinks this answers Erich Heller, who called the passage an "allegorical disaster".

## 3. "Lowly" Origins

### The ape

- Prologue 3: "What is the ape to man? A laughingstock or a painful embarrassment. And man shall be just that for the overman." And "even now, too, man is more ape than any ape."
- **Peter Groff**: the ape is the animal of **mimesis**. Oddly, N. keeps the old "speciesist" ranking (ape below man) that his own naturalism should forbid, probably to exploit his readers' prejudices. The real target is imitation:
  - **"Zarathustra's ape"** (Z III "On Passing By") is a foaming fool who copies Zarathustra's phrasing. His words are even partly right about the great city, but they come from resentment, not love. Zarathustra's parting gift: "where one can no longer love, there one should pass by."
  - **Actors** are "ideal apes" (D 324); children are "born apes" (D 34); "No animal is as much ape as the human being", and even pity may be "an instinctive, inner imitation" (note of 1880).
  - The earliest source is Greek: Heraclitus' fragment that the wisest man is an ape before god (via Plato's *Hippias Major*), and "Heracles' ape", who "merely knew how to deck himself out in the ancient pomp" (BT 10). That's probably the Cercopes, tricksters Zeus turned into monkeys.
- **But imitation isn't simply bad.** Every apprenticeship starts with copying; that's the camel stage. The danger is staying there: "To have joy in originality without becoming the ape of it will perhaps one day be the sign of a new culture." At the end N. sounds resigned: "I searched for great human beings; I always found only the apes of their ideals" (TI "Maxims" 39).

### The polyp

N. uses it once in print, D 119, but **Brian Domino** shows it carries a whole psychology.
- **History.** In 1740 Abraham Trembley showed the polyp was an animal, and that cut into pieces it becomes as many polyps. Materialists like La Mettrie (*Machine Man*) took this as proof that the soul is divisible and material. N. knew the story from Lange's *History of Materialism*.
- **Polyp psychology.** "Every moment of our lives sees some of the polyp-arms of our being grow and others wither, all according to the nutriment that the moment does or does not bear with it" (D 119). Each arm is a **drive**. Laughed at in the street, one person picks a quarrel, another is glad to have cheered the world: different arms happened to get fed.
- **The intellect isn't the captain.** D 109 lists six ways to fight a drive, then adds that the choice isn't ours: "our intellect is only the blind instrument of another drive which is a rival of the drive whose vehemence is tormenting us." The "self" is a shifting configuration of drives; the intellect is a **barometer** of how well they get along, not a ruler.
- **A test case.** Domino describes getting furious at drivers who nearly hit him on his walk to the library. Seeing his anger as a hungry "combativeness" arm calmed him, and he chose one of N.'s methods (attaching a painful thought to the drive's pleasure) until he realised it was really his "tenure drive" fighting his "anger drive". The question isn't whether we *are* polyps but whether thinking so helps us flourish.
- In EH N. remembers D not as a polyp but a "sea monster". Domino: what is a hydra but a mythologised polyp?

## 4. Zarathustra's Animals

**Eagle and serpent** (Prologue 10)
- At noon an eagle soars in wide circles, "and on him there hung a serpent, not like prey but like a friend: for she kept herself wound around his neck." "These are my animals": "the proudest animal under the sun and the wisest animal under the sun".
- **Pride and wisdom**, height and depth, sky and earth, **reconciled**. The natural predator and prey are friends. Later they speak the doctrine of eternal return for him ("The Convalescent").
- **Pappas** notes the pair comes from BT 9: the Semitic myth of sin has a **serpent** (Eden), the Aryan myth of sacrilege an **eagle** (the one that eats Prometheus's liver). The two animals of punishment become Zarathustra's encouragers.

**The dog** ("On the Vision and the Riddle")
- **Gary Shapiro**: the dog howling at the gateway is the real actor in that scene. A notebook: "wherever I climb, my dog follows me everywhere; he is called 'ego'."
- **Training trains the trainer.** We think we domesticate dogs; in fact dog-training trains us to be owners. The ego is likewise bred. Z I "On the Thousand and One Goals": "The delight in the herd is more ancient than the delight in the ego." A note: "Once the ego was hidden in the herd; now the herd is still hidden in the ego." Hence "the herd of independent minds".
- **N. on dogs.** GS 312, "My dog": "I have given a name to my pain and call it 'dog'. It is just as faithful, just as obtrusive and shameless". The Wanderer won't keep one: "those lazy, tail-wagging parasites that first became 'doglike' as the slaves of humans" (WS Epilogue). Plato's joke that the dog is the philosophical animal (*Republic* 376a) because it tells friend from foe by knowledge; Diogenes the "Cynic" (canine).
- **The point for eternal return.** Taking in recurrence means giving all moments equal weight, which dissolves the story of "my" life. When the shepherd bites off the snake's head, the dog falls silent: the domesticated ego drops away, and the shepherd is "no longer shepherd".

**The tarantula and the spider**
- **Alan Schrift** lists every spider in the corpus and finds a **progression from positive to negative**.
  - *Early*: in "On Truth and Lies" the spider's web is the last of N.'s images for science, admirable because the spider spins it **out of itself** (unlike the bee). This is N. at his most Kantian.
  - *Middle*: AOM 194, "Three thinkers like a single spider": in every sect the first produces the sap, the second spins the net, "the third watches in the net for victims... and tries to live off the philosophy".
  - *Late*: the *Kreuzspinne* (garden spider, literally "cross-spider", which really has a white cross on its back) becomes the priest (Z III "On Apostates": "under crosses one can spin well"). Kant is a "fatal spider" (A 11), Spinoza a cunning one. God himself, "hypnotised" by the metaphysicians, "became a spider" (A 17–18).
- "On the Tarantulas" (Z II): preachers of equality are tarantulas whose bite is revenge.
- Schrift's verdict: N. is more afraid of spiders than admiring. They are trappers, not true predators; he fears being caught in webs of concepts more than being killed. Unlike Spinoza, who (says his biographer) amused himself setting spiders to fight.

**The snake**
- **Nickolas Pappas** starts with "On the Adder's Bite" (Z I). Zarathustra sleeps under a fig tree; an adder bites him; he tells it his poison is stronger, and it licks the wound. The scene is stitched from Scripture: the **fig tree** of Eden (Gen 3:7) and of Nathanael (John 1:48), and above all **Acts 28**, where a viper bites Paul on Malta and the islanders conclude first that he's a murderer, then a god. N. inverts it: Paul's immunity proves he's *no* murderer; Zarathustra calls himself "annihilator of morals".
- The snake doesn't "slither straight to an index card" filed under enemy, knowledge or temptation. In GS's "Prelude in Rhymes" N. says "the snake in me" craves earth, an image of skin-shedding, renewal and digging into low places.
- **The Bible already had good snakes.** Moses' rod turned serpent, the brazen serpent that heals (Num 21:6–9), which Jesus applies to himself (John 3:14–15). So N. isn't simply restoring a pagan snake against a Christian one.
- **Loeb's reading** of the snake in the shepherd's mouth: it's Wagner's dragon Fafner. Its head is the present, its tail the past, and biting it off means ending humanity as it now is.
- **Masculine.** N. keeps the biblical snake male, an image of eternity "without maternity". He loves eternity but never says where the philosophers of the future are to be born.

**The bird: the halcyon tone**
- **Gary Shapiro** again. N. says *Zarathustra* must be heard in the right tone, "the **halcyon** tone" (EH Preface 4). He reintroduced the word into German. In Ovid, Alcyone loses her husband at sea and both become kingfishers; the female builds a **floating nest** in the calm "halcyon days" around the winter solstice. Her cry was famously sad, her days famously calm.
- Bird-wisdom closes Part III ("The Seven Seals"): "Behold, there is no above, no below! Throw yourself around, out, back, you who are light! Sing! Speak no more!" And: "all that is heavy and grave should become light; all that is body, dancer; all that is spirit, bird."
- Z III "On Old and New Tablets" 22: "man is the best beast of prey. Man has already robbed all the beasts of their virtues... Only the birds are still over and above him."
- Shapiro reads it as *becoming-bird* (Deleuze and Guattari): desire not as lack but as becoming other, which also recasts the debate over N. and the feminine (Derrida, Irigaray).

**The cow**
- In Part IV the **voluntary beggar**, a rich man who gave everything away, preaches to cows on a hill: "Except we turn back and become as cows, we shall not enter the kingdom of heaven" (a parody of Matt 18:3). We must learn one thing from them, **chewing the cud**, or we won't get rid of our great melancholy, "but today that is called nausea". "The kingdom of heaven is among the cows." He ends: "You yourself are good, and even better than a cow, O Zarathustra."
- **Tracey Stark**: GM Preface 8 asks for reading as "**rumination**", for which "one has almost to be a cow". In EH N. says he once sensed a herd of cows before he saw it, "because milder and more philanthropic thoughts came back to me: they had warmth".
- **Cadmus.** Zarathustra's town is "The **Motley Cow**". Stark identifies it with **Thebes**: Cadmus was led by a spotted heifer to found it, killed Ares' serpent (compare the shepherd), married Harmony and brought the **alphabet** to Greece. Dionysus' mountain Cithaeron is nearby.

**The ass**
- The ass carries the higher men's burdens through Part IV, and in "The Awakening" they worship it. The ugliest man's litany begins "Amen! And praise and honor and wisdom and thanks and glory and strength be to our god", and the refrain is always "But the ass brayed: Yea-Yuh." The ass "took upon himself the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7), "never says No", and "always says Yea to the world he created".
- **The Feast of the Ass.** **Kathleen Higgins** traces it to the medieval feast (late 11th to 16th c., the weeks after Christmas). Sub-deacons played bishops, clergy diced and ate blood pudding at the altar, brayed at Mass, and sang the "Prose of the Ass". At Beauvais a girl with a child on an ass re-enacted the **Flight into Egypt**, and the priest brayed three times instead of *Ite, missa est*. In 1445 the Paris theology faculty condemned it. BGE 8 quotes its first line: *adventavit asinus, pulcher et fortissimus*, "the ass arrived, beautiful and most strong" (Del Caro). The German bray is *I-A*, which sounds like ***Ja***: the ass's "Amen".
- **Why "The Awakening"?** *Die Erweckung* was the Lutheran revival movement N.'s parents belonged to, who called themselves "children". The retired pope defends the worship: "Better to adore God in this form than in no form at all!" Zarathustra brays "even louder than the ass", then praises the festival as something "only convalescents" invent: "do it for my sake. And in remembrance of me."
- **Apuleius.** Higgins argues Part IV systematically alludes to *The Golden Ass*: Lucius, turned into an ass by the wrong ointment, learns through his humiliations, is given wine, dances, and is turned back by eating **roses** at Isis's procession. Zarathustra throws the higher men a "rose-wreath crown" of laughter. So the ass is a **stage**: folly that teaches you to laugh at yourself, and laughing at yourself is already moving past it. Compare TI "Maxims" 11: "Can an ass be tragic?... The case of the philosopher."

## 5. The Beasts of Prey

**The blond beast** (GM I 11)
- At the base of all noble races "is the predator, the magnificent **blond beast** roaming about lustily after prey and victory". From time to time this hidden ground needs discharging: "the animal must emerge once more, must return to the wilderness: Roman, Arabic, Teutonic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Scandinavian Vikings". They come back from murder, arson, rape and torture "as if they had merely pulled some student prank" (Del Caro).
- **Gerd Schank** (for the *Nietzsche-Wörterbuch*):
  1. *"Beast"* occurs only about 27 times. It has two contexts: the **Greeks' success** in integrating "the wildest beasts of nature" into culture through Apollo (BT 2), and the beast made **sick** by morality since then (HH 40; GM II 22; TI). N. likes the beast, never "bestiality". The menagerie image may answer Paul Rée, who called the state "a great menagerie" where fear stops the beasts devouring one another.
  2. *"Blond"* and "blond beast" occur only **thirteen times**, all from 1885–88. When "blond" stands alone it is mostly negative: "blond saints" in Wagner, the "too blond" stupid sensuality of the Viennese waltz, and a note mocking "flaxen" heads and "blue" eyes: "What a blessing a Jew is among Germans!"
  3. "Blond beast" means the **lion** (the "blond-maned lion-monster" of Z IV), the agonal "achievers", and the state-founding "pack of blond beasts of prey" (GM II 17). The racial use is GM I 5 ("blond, namely Aryan conqueror race"), but the list in I 11 includes Arabs and Japanese, and N. says there's hardly a conceptual, "let alone one of blood" relationship between the old Germanic tribes and modern Germans.
  4. The **reception**: a slogan by 1895, bred into the Nazi elite schools; Heydrich's party nickname was "the blond beast". Thomas Mann distanced himself from it in 1930.
- **Daniel Conway**: don't soften it. These are humans who "act like wild animals toward other human beings". But they're also N.'s **missing link** (next section).

**Beasts of prey as unconscious artists** (Conway)
1. **The founding of the state** (GM II 16–17). Some semi-animals were shut into "the walls of society and peace" like sea animals forced onto land; their instincts turned inward and became the "soul". Who shut them in? "Some pack of blond beasts of prey, a conqueror and master race", who arrive "like fate, without reason". N. never explains where these predators came from.
2. **Predators are artists.** "Their work is an instinctive creating of form, impressing of form; they are the most involuntary, unconscious artists in existence" (GM II 17, Del Caro). They don't just kill their captives, they **organise** them. Conway's point: predation in nature also renews the whole environment.
3. **They domesticate themselves.** Shaping a populace in their image shaped them back. Like dog-trainers trained by dogs, they ended up merging with the people they ruled. (He mentions the theory that wolves domesticated themselves to eat human scraps.)
4. **The priest as new predator.** Their go-betweens were the priests, who learned their art and turned it inward. To fight the beasts of prey the priest must "develop in himself almost a new type of predator... in which the polar bear, the supple cold patient tiger cat, and not least the fox seem to be bound together" (GM III 15, Del Caro). The ascetic ideal is animal husbandry turned on the predators themselves.
5. **What next?** N. hopes a "winged creature" might emerge from the priest's "caterpillar" (GM III 10): the philosopher.

**Lambs and birds of prey** (GM I 13)
- "It does not seem strange that lambs bear a grudge against the great birds of prey: only this is no reason to hold it against the great birds of prey that they snatch themselves little lambs." The lambs call the birds evil and themselves good. The birds might say: "we do not bear a grudge against them, these good lambs, in fact we love them: nothing is tastier than a tender lamb."
- To demand that strength not express itself as strength is as absurd as demanding that weakness express itself as strength. The "subject" who could *choose* to be a lamb is a fiction.

**The Church's menagerie** (TI "The Improvers of Mankind" 2)
- "To call the taming of an animal its 'improvement' sounds almost like a joke." Beasts in menageries aren't improved but weakened, made sick by fear, pain and hunger. In the early Middle Ages "the church was indeed, above all, a menagerie": it hunted the finest "blond beasts", and the "improved" Teuton came out of the monastery "like a caricature of man", a "sinner" in a cage, "malevolent against himself". "In short, a 'Christian.'"

**The laughing lion** (Z IV "The Sign")
- The book ends with a mighty yellow lion that lies at Zarathustra's feet "and acted like a dog that finds its old master again". Doves flutter round it, and it laughs. Zarathustra weeps; the lion licks his tears; then it roars the higher men back into the cave. "The lion came, my children are near... this is my morning, my day is breaking: rise now, rise, thou great noon!"
- **Paul Loeb** argues this ending isn't gentle, and his argument is a whole reading of the book:
  1. **Part IV is a satyr play** that happens chronologically *inside* Part III, between the start of "On Old and New Tablets" (Zarathustra waiting for his sign of "the laughing lion with the flock of doves") and "The Convalescent".
  2. So the "Tablets" chapter isn't a monologue to imaginary future disciples. From section 6 Zarathustra speaks in the **present tense** to brothers who are *there*: "Now, however, we are first-borns." He notices their horror: "You flee from me? You are horrified?"
  3. The climax is his command: "Shatter, shatter the good and the just for me!" Not just the old tablets, the people. N. later called this the "decisive" chapter.
  4. **Why laughing?** Because the nobles in GM I 11 come back "rejoicing" from atrocity, and the magician's "Song of Melancholy" praises the panther's bliss in "rending laughing". Loeb concludes the lions' laughter is delight in destruction, and that pointing from the blond beast to the "spiritual" lion of Z I gives no comfort at all.
- A footnote reports a suggestion from Jung's seminar: the image may come from the winged lion and pigeons of St Mark's in Venice. Loeb also notes the allusion to Revelation, where the **Lion of Judah** defeats the dragon.

## 6. Human Animals: Woman, Satyr, Overhuman

**Woman as predator** (Thomas Brobjer)
- **Herd and predator.** N. read Otto Caspari's *Prehistory of Mankind* (1877) around 1881, which argued from zoology that man descends from both social, sympathetic animals and proud, egoistic beasts of prey. Only after reading it does N. start calling humans "herd animals". Civilisation and Christianity fed the herd side; the "last man" is the end point.
- **Woman is the more predatory.** You'd expect N. to put women with the herd. He doesn't: woman is cat, tiger, "the tiger's claw beneath the glove" (BGE 239), "essentially unpeaceful, like a cat" (BGE 131). She is also "more animal" than man, closer to nature.
- **The whip.** "You are going to women? Do not forget the whip!" (Z I "On Little Old and Young Women") is said by an old woman. Brobjer argues it's a **circus** whip that cracks, not a horse whip that strikes: in "The Other Dancing Song" Life says "O Zarathustra, don't crack your whip so frightfully! After all, you know that noise murders thought". Sources: the 1882 photograph N. staged of Lou Salomé in a cart with a whip and N. and Rée as draft animals; Turgenev's *First Love* (a father strikes the woman, she loves him the more), which Elisabeth claims she read to him (and she exclaimed on reading Z, "I am the old woman"); and *Carmen*: "love as fatum... the deadly hatred of the sexes" (CW 2). Resa von Schirnhofer says N. told her the line meant one particular person.
- Brobjer doesn't excuse it. N. wanted woman "kept under control", and he admits this sits badly with his attack on taming.

**Woman as liberated animal** (Jennifer Ham)
- Ham reads the same passages the other way: grouping women with animals was an argument "for their liberation within the context of a new, posthuman order".
- **Animal trainers.** In N.'s day the famous trainer Carl Hagenbeck campaigned against "whips, clubs and hot irons" for "love, patience, energy". N. condemns the "horrific species" of animal tamers "who know how to handle red-hot iron", the priests (WP 397), and Zarathustra: "Cursed I call all who have only one choice: to become evil beasts or evil tamers of beasts" (Z III "On the Spirit of Gravity").
- **Ensphinxed.** In "Among Daughters of the Wilderness" (Z IV) the wanderer sits "enveloped by you, silent and foreboding girl-cats, Dudu and Suleika—ensphinxed". Unlike Oedipus, he doesn't answer the sphinx's riddle (whose answer is "man") and kill her; he stays enchanted.
- **Truth as woman, truth as Circe.** HH 519 again: Circe turned Odysseus' men into swine. N. "philosophizes as Circe".

**The satyr** (Lawrence Hatab)
- "I am a disciple of the philosopher Dionysus; I should prefer to be even a satyr to being a saint" (EH Preface 2). The satyr is half animal, half human: Greek nature and culture without a seam.
- In BT tragedy grows from the satyr chorus. Hatab adds what scholarship has since found: satyrs were horse-tailed (goat features came later), they **discover** wine, music, fire and the first woman in satyr plays, and every tragic trilogy ended with a satyr play by the same actors. So the satyr's comedy was part of tragedy's life-affirmation. Loeb and Hatab both read *Zarathustra* IV as N.'s satyr play.

**The overhuman animal** (Lemm's chapter): covered in §1. The overhuman is not *less* animal but more; overcoming "takes the form of a return to the beginning, to the animal".

## 7. Animal Nietzsche: Mole, Cat, Lizard

**The mole** (Debra Bergoffen)
- D Preface: the book is the work of "a subterranean man", a solitary mole who "tunnels and mines and undermines". But unlike a mole he needs eyes, and wants his own daybreak. The mole is the perfect totem for a genealogist (and a "mole" is also a double agent).
- Then N. turns on it. In HH 18 a mole-like squinting is the organic root of the metaphysical belief in **substance**; in GS 86 the mole is the passionless modern man; in Z III "On the Spirit of Gravity" Zarathustra calls himself an eagle and the mole (with the dwarf) his archenemy.
- Bergoffen's question: did N. kill his own double? He is the philosopher of the body but not of the **dark**: no caves, no wombs, all heights and light. She contrasts the Kogi of Colombia, whose holy men are raised for nine years in near darkness so that daylight astonishes them for life.

**The cat** (Martha Kendal Woodruff)
- The cat is feral by nature: never a herd animal, never a "master's" pet. It stands between the big cats (Dionysus' panthers, "your wildcats must first turn into tigers", Z II) and the child at **play**.
- N. accuses cats of deceit and lust: the moon creeps "catlike and without honesty" (Z II "On Immaculate Perception"); women are "still cats and birds. Or at best, cows" (Z I "On the Friend").
- But he writes like one: "All good things approach their goal crookedly. Like cats, they arch their backs, they purr inwardly over their approaching happiness: all good things laugh" (Z IV "On the Higher Man" 17). Woodruff reads that as a description of N.'s own **style**: indirect, ironic, playful. Montaigne: "When I play with my cat, who knows if I am not a pastime to her more than she is to me?"

**The lizard** (Babette Babich)
- The convalescent in HH Preface 5 sits still in the sun, grateful for "the flecks of sunlight upon the wall": "the most grateful animals in the world and also the most modest, these convalescents and lizards who have once again turned halfway toward life".
- EH on *Daybreak*: the art of making things that slip by "stay still for a little", "moments which I call **divine lizards**", not by impaling them like a young Greek god (Apollo the lizard-slayer), "but nonetheless still with something sharp, with the pen".
- At noon in Z IV: "Precisely the least, the softest, lightest, a lizard's rustling, a breath, a breeze, a moment's glance—it is little that makes the best happiness." A cold-blooded animal that warms itself in the sun and regrows a lost tail: an image of thought, convalescence and alchemical renewal. *Eidechse* also sounds like *eidos*, idea.

## 8. Paws, Claws and Jaws

Christa Acampora's afterword looks at the **parts** of animals.
- **Claws.** "To some people you may not give your hand, only a paw: and I desire that your paw should also have claws" (Z I "On the Way of the Creator"). Weaklings think themselves good "because they had no claws" (Z II).
- **Fingers** cut both ways: dialectic "shows all five fingers" (TI "Socrates" 5), but good readers need "delicate eyes and fingers" (D Preface 5).
- **Zarathustra's body as a bestiary.** In "On the Spirit of Gravity" his mouth is rough to "silky rabbits", his foot is cloven, his stomach is an eagle's that likes lamb's flesh, his arms are wings. "The ostrich runs faster than the fastest horse, but even he buries his head gravely in the grave earth." To become light one must love oneself.
- **Metaphor changes what we are.** Following Kofman, all concepts are worn-out metaphors. Acampora goes further: metaphors aren't decoration but **therapy**. A species is held together by its habitual interpretations, so changing the images could change the animal. She compares the Iroquois Eagle Dance, where the dancers don't imitate the eagle but become it. The human is "the animal that explores and ranges across taxonomic borders".

## 10. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What N. sees**
- **GS 224 is biblical.** "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know" (Isa 1:3). The prophets got there first: the animals keep their nature, and only man has lost his "common sense". And Ecclesiastes was "placing man back among the animals" long before A 14: "that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts... so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast" (Eccl 3:19). The Bible doesn't flatter us either.
- **The feral middle is right.** Acampora's point that the alternatives aren't only "tame" and "wild" is the best thing in the volume, and it fits the Christian tradition better than N. thinks. The monk is a feral creature: he deserts the city for the desert and comes back to teach it. Taylor's camel, who *chooses* the heaviest load and dances in his chains, is a fair portrait of an ascetic.
- **Forgetting.** UM II 1 is beautiful and true. We are the animal tortured by "it was". Lemm makes the strongest case that N. sets animal forgetting and gift-giving *against* Christian forgiveness, which on her reading can't break the cycle of revenge because it runs on judgment and debt. But the Gospel claims something stranger: God "will remember their sin no more" (Jer 31:34), and that kind of "forgetting" isn't animal oblivion but **re-creation**. Animal forgetting can't heal a wound; it only stops feeling it. Mirabeau "was not able to forgive only because he forgot"; the saints forgive *and* remember, which is harder.
- **N.'s own demand is Christian.** In "On the Adder's Bite": "Would that you might invent for me the love that bears not only all punishment but also all guilt! Would that you might invent for me the justice that acquits everyone, except him that judges!" That love has been invented. Charity "beareth all things" (1 Cor 13:7), and the Servant "bare the sin of many" (Isa 53:12). "The Father judgeth no man" (John 5:22), and the Son says "I judge no man" (John 8:15): that is the closest thing on record to the justice N. asks for.
- **The ass festival.** Higgins is right that it's funny and that the medieval Church *let it happen*. That tells you something. A faith secure enough to bray at its own Mass for a week a year is not the brittle "menagerie" of TI. And the Church already knew that the ass is the animal of God's surprises: **Balaam's ass** sees the angel the prophet can't (Num 22:23–33), and Christ enters Jerusalem "meek, and sitting upon an ass" (Matt 21:5). The higher men worship the ass. The Church honours the ass because **it carried Christ**. Even the retired pope's line, "better to adore God in this form than in no form at all", is a backhanded tribute to the Incarnation: God *did* take a form.

**Where he goes wrong**
- **Lambs and birds of prey, and the Lamb.** GM I 13 is N. at his sharpest: the lambs' morality is their revenge on the hawks. But Revelation 5 has already staged the scene and reversed it. John is told to look at "the **Lion** of the tribe of Juda", turns, and sees "a **Lamb** as it had been slain". The Lion *is* the Lamb. Strength in the Gospel is not denied but **redefined** as self-giving. And the Lamb doesn't resent the birds of prey. He dies for them. That breaks N.'s dichotomy: here is a strength that doesn't "express itself" as overpowering and isn't weakness either.
- **Loeb's lion.** If Loeb is right, the end of *Zarathustra* is a call to "shatter the good and the just", and the lion that "acted like a dog that finds its old master again" is a war-hound. Loeb points out the allusion to the Lion of Judah. The difference is exactly the one above: Revelation's Lion conquers by being slain; Zarathustra's by slaying.
- **The menagerie and the desert.** TI's caged Teuton is a real type: the sick, self-hating "convert". But look at how the Church actually told its animal stories. **St Gerasimos of the Jordan** pulls a thorn from a lion's paw. The lion stays to serve the monastery, and when the old man dies it lies down on his grave and dies there. A lion also helped St Zosimas dig the grave of **St Mary of Egypt**. **St Seraphim of Sarov** fed a bear from his hand. This isn't taming by fear, pain and hunger (TI's menagerie). Nor is it Hagenbeck's kinder circus. It's what Isaiah 11 promised: "the wolf also shall dwell with the lamb... and a **little child** shall lead them." Isaiah ends with the child too, but the child *leads the lion* instead of replacing it. The saint doesn't kill the animal in himself or let it loose. He **restores Eden's peace**.
- **The blond beast.** Schank and Conway are both right: the image isn't a racial programme, and it's still a celebration of murder, arson and rape as "student pranks". Conway shows N. even admires the predators as *artists* of the state. The Bible's version of a king becoming a beast is **Nebuchadnezzar**, who in his pride "did eat grass as oxen... till his hairs were grown like eagles' feathers, and his nails like birds' claws" (Dan 4:33). The beast is not the noble ground under culture. It is what pride *becomes*. He's healed when he "lifted up mine eyes unto heaven, and mine understanding returned unto me" (Dan 4:34).
- **Women and the whip.** Brobjer and Ham argue in opposite directions from the same texts, which tells you the texts are unstable. Ham's "liberation" reading is generous; Brobjer's "control" reading fits N.'s letters better. Either way the woman is made a cat so that she can be feared or tamed, never a neighbour.

**Man as microcosm**
- The Fathers have a better middle term than N.'s "rope". For St Gregory of Nyssa and St Maximus, man is a **microcosm** and **mediator**: animal by body, spiritual by soul, set between the creatures and God to gather the whole creation and offer it back. That's why Adam **names** the animals (Gen 2:19), and why "the whole creation groaneth" waiting for the sons of God (Rom 8:19–22). The animals don't need us to become them. They need us to become fully human, so that they can be set free too. See [[Image and Likeness]] and [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
- Lemm's "over-animal" and "overhuman" point at something real. The over-animal, who sets himself *above* the beasts and despises them, is the man the Fathers call fallen: he has lost the kingship by grasping at it. But the answer isn't a horizontal contest with the animals either. It's the **priestly** relation: man between earth and heaven, not over the animals and not merely beside them.
- The Church even had its own bestiary: the **Physiologus** (Alexandria, early centuries AD), the ancestor of all medieval bestiaries; the *Bestiary*'s own editors mention it. Its lion's cubs are born dead and wake on the third day when their father breathes on them. N. wrote a bestiary of nature without its Creator. The older one read every animal as a sign of Him. "Be ye therefore wise as **serpents**, and harmless as **doves**" (Matt 10:16): Zarathustra's eagle and serpent, his lion and doves, are all already in that one verse.
