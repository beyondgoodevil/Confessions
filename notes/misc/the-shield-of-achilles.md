In *Iliad* 18 Achilles has lost his armour. Patroclus wore it into battle, Hector killed him and stripped it, and now Hector is wearing it. Thetis goes up to Olympus and asks Hephaistos for new armour, and Homer spends about 130 lines on **the shield alone**. It isn't decorated with Gorgons or monsters. It shows **the whole world**: sky, two cities, farms, vineyards, herds, a dance, and Ocean around the edge. Most of it is **at peace**. So the question every reader eventually asks: **why does the deadliest warrior in the poem carry a picture of ordinary peaceful life into his last battle?** Here's how it works:

### 1. The Making

- Thetis finds Hephaistos sweating at his bellows. He is making **twenty tripods on wheels** that roll into the gods' assembly by themselves and roll home again (18.373–7).
- He comes out leaning on **maids made of gold**, "in appearance like living young women", with mind, voice and strength. Lane Fox calls this "the first attestation of robots and the first examples of artificial intelligence".
- Hephaistos owes Thetis. When he was thrown out of heaven for being lame, she took him in under the sea for nine years, and he made her jewellery. So he's glad to help, though he says he can't save her son from death.
- **Brann**: Hephaistos is the only god with "human weight" in Homer. He's crippled, a buffoon on Olympus who makes the gods laugh, and the husband Aphrodite cheats on. But he's also **the only god who makes anything**. He's "lame as Homer is blind": the god's version of the poet. He makes a world in metal, Homer makes one in words, and Homer gets the last word because he is the author of Hephaistos' masterpiece.
- **Edwards**: formally the whole scene is Achilles' **arming scene** moved forward and blown up. Agamemnon gets a corslet and Gorgon shield in eleven lines (11.20–40); Achilles gets a book. The scale fits the size of his return. Edwards calls it "like an immense simile": it stops the action while we look.
- **Construction**: five layers (*ptuches*), a triple rim, a silver strap (18.479–82). At 20.268–72 we're told the layers are two of bronze, two of tin and one of gold inside, which makes no sense (why is the gold hidden?). Aristarchus rejected the lines and Edwards thinks they're probably an interpolation.

### 2. Circle by Circle

Homer doesn't actually say how the scenes are laid out, but the usual reconstruction (followed by Taplin) is five concentric bands from the centre to the rim.

1. **The cosmos** (483–9). "He made the earth upon it, and the sky, and the sea's water", then the sun, moon, Pleiades, Hyades, Orion and the Bear, "who turns about in a fixed place and looks at Orion" and alone "is never plunged in the wash of the Ocean". It's the only place in the *Iliad* where constellations appear.
   - Lane Fox: the Bear is also the "Wagon" because the Babylonian name was Akkadian *eriqqu*, "waggon", which sounded to Greeks like *arktos*, "bear". So both names stuck.
2. **Two cities** (490–540).
   - **The city at peace**:
     - **A wedding.** Brides are led through the streets by torchlight, young men dance to flutes and lyres, and the women stand in their doorways admiring.
     - **A lawsuit.** Two men dispute "the blood price for a man who had been killed". Lattimore has one man promising "full restitution" and the other refusing, but the Greek is notoriously unclear (Edwards lists the readings: "claimed to have paid it all" against "promised to pay"; is the other man refusing payment, or denying he received it?). The elders sit "on benches of polished stone in the sacred circle", and "two talents of gold" lie in the middle "to be given to that judge who in this case spoke the straightest opinion". Nobody is killed: the feud goes to court.
   - **The city at war**:
     - Two armies besiege it and argue whether to sack it or take half its wealth. The townspeople refuse, leave wives, children and old men on the walls, and go out to set **an ambush** at a river, led by Ares and Athena, who are drawn bigger than everyone else and in gold.
     - Two herdsmen come along "playing happily on pipes" and "took no thought of the treachery". They're killed and the herds taken.
     - Then a pitched battle by the river, with Strife, Confusion and Death, who drags a corpse by the feet and whose clothes are "strong red with the men's blood".
3. **The countryside through the year** (541–72).
   - **Ploughing**: at each turn a man hands the ploughmen a cup of honey-sweet wine. "The earth darkened behind them and looked like earth that has been ploughed though it was gold. Such was the wonder of the shield's forging." This is the one place the poet steps back and marvels at the trick of the art (Edwards).
   - **Harvest on a king's estate** (*temenos*): reapers, sheaf-binders, children gathering, and "the king in silence and holding his staff stood near the line of the reapers, happily". A feast is cooked under a tree.
   - **The vintage**: gold vines with dark grapes on silver poles, a ditch of dark metal and a tin fence. Young people carry the grapes "in all their light-hearted innocence", and a boy plays the lyre and sings the **Linos song** (a lament for a dying youth, sung at harvest).
4. **Animals** (573–89).
   - Golden and tin cattle head to pasture with four herdsmen and nine dogs. **Two lions** seize a bull and gulp "the black blood and the inward guts"; the dogs bark but won't go near.
   - Then a quiet sheep pasture in a valley, with huts and folds.
5. **The dance and Ocean** (590–607).
   - "A dancing floor, like that which once in the wide spaces of Knosos Daidalos built for Ariadne." Young men and girls dance holding wrists, the girls in garlands, the young men with golden knives on silver belts.
   - They run in a circle "as when a potter crouching makes trial of his wheel", then in crossing rows. A crowd watches "happily" and two acrobats lead.
   - Around the rim runs "the great strength of the Ocean River".

Then the reaction (19.12–19): the armour clashes when Thetis sets it down, and the Myrmidons tremble and can't look at it. "Only Achilleus looked, and as he looked the anger came harder upon him." He "was glad, holding in his hands the shining gifts of Hephaistos". The shield doesn't soften him at all.

### 3. Why Peace on a War Shield?

The normal war shield is meant to **terrify**. Agamemnon's has the Gorgon with Fear and Terror (11.36–7); Heracles' baldric in the *Odyssey* (11.609–14) has bears, boars and lions, battles and murders. Achilles' shield has weddings and grape-picking. Edwards: Homer "has other designs than to frighten us".

**Taplin** (1980) rejects three easy answers:

1. **It's a real heirloom shield**, remembered by the oral tradition. No: nothing like it has ever been found, any more than golden robots have. Like the "gynaikoids", the shield is "a wonder of divine craftsmanship". It is "poetic invention not history".
2. **It's relief from the fighting.** But the rest of Books 18–19 is already relief, and that doesn't explain why *this* kind of relief.
3. **The oral poet just rambled** from one stock scene to the next. Taplin goes through the circles to show that's false. Almost every scene echoes something in the rest of the poem:
   - **The besieged city is Troy.** Old men, women and children stand on the walls, as Hector orders at 8.518–22, and as Helen and the elders sit over the Scaean gates and Andromache waits on the Great Tower (Book 6). "The city on the shield stands for every threatened homeland." Brann adds that offering half the wealth is what Hector considers in his last monologue (22.111–21), and that the corpse dragged by the feet foreshadows Hector: "This is Troy, to a tee."
   - **The ambushed herdsmen** recall Achilles killing Andromache's seven brothers while they were tending sheep (6.423–4).
   - **The vintagers' "light-hearted innocence"** (*atala phroneontes*) is a phrase used elsewhere only of the baby **Astyanax**.
   - **The king happily watching his harvest** on his *temenos* is "the kind which Achilles might have had if he had chosen long life instead of glorious death". It's also the life Odysseus is fighting to get back to (Od. 19.109–14, the good king whose land prospers). Taplin sides with Leaf against Kirk, who thought the king would eat the beef and the workers get barley mash: the women are sprinkling the meat with barley, as at Od. 14.77, so everyone eats.
   - **The lawsuit** is "the stable justice of a civilized city": no vendetta or exile, as in the age of heroes, but arbitrators and speeches. The elders' "polished stones" are the epithet for Priam's palace and Nestor's council seats at Pylos (Od. 3.406–8). (I'd add that it sends you back to Book 9, where Ajax tells Achilles that even a man whose brother was killed accepts the blood-price, 9.632–6. Achilles won't.)
   - **The rural scenes are the four seasons**: ploughing is spring, the harvest summer, the vintage autumn, and the cattle kept in the dungyard winter. Taplin notes that all four of Alcman's season-words already occur in Homer.
   - **The lions** are the lions of the similes, where heroes are compared to beasts tearing cattle. On the shield, "in the peacetime agricultural world man's worst enemy is the lion, not other men".
   - **The lions** "break in on this as though to prevent the world of the shield from being too perfect".
   - **The dance** goes with peaceful Troy and with Phaeacia ("always the feast is dear to us, the lyre and the dance", Od. 8.248–9). The *Odyssey* is full of these settled scenes. Taplin follows Kakridis in making the dance **the climax** of the shield (Gärtner preferred the king's estate). It forms a ring with the wedding at the start, and if you borrow a line from the identical passage at Od. 4.17–19 there is a bard among the dancers, the only *aoidos* in the *Iliad*.
   - Fine clothing appears on the shield, and in the *Iliad* Troy's robes end up as ransom and as Hector's shroud. The washing troughs where Trojan women used to wash clothes "in the time of peace" are the very place Achilles chases Hector past (22.153–6).

So Taplin's conclusion: **"The shield is a microcosm, not a utopia."** It shows the world, with war and lions included, but in **inverse proportion** to the rest of the poem. "On the shield the Iliad takes up, so to speak, one half of one of the five circles." Homer lets us step back and see the war as one detail in a large landscape that the narrative usually hides. He quotes E. T. Owen: the scene deepens the tragedy "by seeing it thus against the large indifferent background of the wider life of the world". And Edwards: the shield shows "the everyday human life which he has given up".

But Taplin is careful: this doesn't make the *Iliad* "an anti-war epic, a pacifist tract". The poem doesn't condemn war or sweeten it. "It presents both sides, victory and defeat, the destroyer and the destroyed; and it does not judge between them." That even-handedness is part of its greatness.

**Lane Fox** quotes Richard Rutherford's commentary: these "unheroic and unmemorable lives" are ones that "offer more in terms of happiness and fulfilment than the path of heroic warfare brings for Achilles". Nowhere else in the poem does anyone feel joy like the grape-pickers or the dancers.

### 4. Real Metalwork?

Not a real shield, but **real techniques** behind it (Edwards, citing Borchhardt and Dorothea Gray):

- **Inlay of different metals** in bronze, as in the Mycenaean inlaid daggers. The darkened gold earth and dark grapes may reflect niello or coloured inlay.
- **Bronze shields from Crete** (the Idaean cave), with figured decoration in rings.
- **Phoenician silver and bronze bowls** of about Homer's date, from Cyprus and Italy. They have concentric friezes with cities under siege, farming, hunting and processions. One, from Praeneste, tells a little **story** in sequence around the bowl, and it has a **snake around the rim**, rather like Ocean.
- Lane Fox adds that the scenes fit **Homer's own time** (8th century), not the heroic age. The lawsuit belongs to a community without a polis and written law, and two talents of gold is modest for heroes (it's a fourth prize in Book 23) but a lot in Homer's world. He also notes that the smithing is imagined as ironworking.

### 5. Moving Pictures: Ekphrasis

- *Ekphrasis* is the description in words of a work of visual art. This is the founding example.
- **Edwards** points out that Homer always describes objects **through action**: Pandarus' bow is told through its making, Odysseus' raft through its building. Here the verbs keep returning to Hephaistos making ("he made on it...", 478, 483, 490, 541...). Every scene **moves and makes sound**: the lawsuit litigants speak in turn, ploughmen turn and turn again, cattle low, a river babbles, the dancers form a circle and then lines. Becker: "the predominant way of appropriating visual images is to translate them into stories".
- **Lane Fox**: Geometric vase painting showed sequences in separate panels like a strip cartoon, but Homer narrates continuously. So these are "moving pictures, the earliest known examples in the world". The word *cinema* comes from Greek *kinēma*, "movement".
- **Brann**: Hephaistos, the maker of self-moving tripods, "knows how to make moving pictures within his round frame", and Homer can do in words what the god does in metal. She sets it beside Keats' *Ode on a Grecian Urn*, where the figures are frozen forever.
- But **Edwards** notes the difference from Keats: Homer never stops to reflect that the lawsuit and the siege will never be decided. That's what Keats does, and so does the *Aspis*, whose chariot-racers toil on forever with no winner. Homer isn't showing particular events at all but "paradigms of ever-continuing human social activities".
- **Marg** (followed by Edwards): the shield is described on Olympus, among gods, and Achilles only glares at it with anger. He can't really see what's on it until Book 24, with Priam.

### 6. Imitations and Answers

- **The Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* (Aspis)**: a later poem that imitates and inverts Homer. It opens with **Fear, Strife, Panic and Slaughter**, the Fates and the Death-mist, with Perseus and the Gorgons. It's a shield meant to terrify. Four of the melee lines with Strife and Death on Achilles' shield (18.535–8) also occur in the *Aspis* (156–9), and Taplin follows Solmsen in thinking 535–40 were imported from it. The same thing nearly happened again: a first-century BC papyrus (P.Berol. 9774) adds four lines about a harbour full of fish after 608, almost word for word from the *Aspis* (207–13).
- **Euripides, *Electra* 442–86**: the chorus sings of the shield, but in their version the sun and stars are there to panic Hector, and Perseus with the Gorgon's head skims around the rim. It's designed to terrify the Trojans, and Taplin reads it as Euripides' reaction to a brutal war, in a play about the aftermath of Agamemnon's inhumanity.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 8**: Vulcan makes Aeneas a shield showing **the future of Rome** down to Augustus at Actium. Aeneas lifts it "on his shoulders, the fame and destiny of his descendants". Edwards' contrast: Achilles doesn't shoulder responsibility for the people on his shield; Aeneas carries a nation.
- **W. H. Auden, "The Shield of Achilles"** (1952). Thetis looks over the god's shoulder for vines, cities and ships and finds instead "An artificial wilderness / And a sky like lead": faceless crowds, a voice "proved by statistics that some cause was just", an execution, and a ragged urchin who has never heard of a world where promises were kept. It ends with "Iron-hearted man-slaying Achilles / Who would not live long".
  - Taplin thinks Auden made Achilles a prototype of the Aryan superman. He says that "Simone Weil understood the Iliad more fully than W. H. Auden". Auden wanted war shown with no loopholes, but "the Iliad is not only a poem of war, it is also a poem of peace", and Priam's "I put my lips to the hands of the man who has killed my children" is what the whole poem leads up to.
  - Brann says Auden is a good reader of Homer "by opposition". His shield is the exact opposite of Homer's world: there, the land is green, troops get to eat, nobody knows statistics, faces are expressive, gods come to help, and everything is debated. The one thing that still holds is that Achilles is a man who dies before his body dies, "but not for shame".
- **Later artists** (Wikipedia): people have tried to reconstruct the shield on paper and in metal at least since the early 18th century. There's Angelo Monticelli's drawing (c. 1820), and van Dyck's *Thetis Receiving the Weapons of Achilles from Hephaestus* (1630–32).

### 7. The Cosmic Readings

- Because the shield starts with the sky and ends with Ocean, ancient readers took it as a **model of the universe**. The Stoic critic **Crates of Mallos** read Homer's shields cosmologically, and **Heraclitus the Allegorist** (1st century AD) treated the shield of Achilles as an image of the cosmos and Hephaistos as the fire that shapes it (see [[How Philosophers Saved Homer: Allegory from the Stoics to the Byzantines]]).
- **Philip Hardie**'s article "Imago Mundi" (*JHS* 1985) takes this tradition seriously. He looks at how the shield came to be read as an image of the world, and at the political ("ideological") meaning that cosmic picture could carry, which matters for Virgil's shield of Aeneas and its Roman world-rule.
- Germaine Aujac (cited on Wikipedia) calls it the first example of cosmological mapping in Greece.
- **Brann** notices what's missing: the shield has earth, heaven and sea, the realms of Zeus and Poseidon, but **no Hades**, the realm of the third brother. Hades' name was understood as *A-ides*, "the unseen", and he lives beyond Ocean, outside the visible world. The shield is the **visible** world only.
- Taplin also lists what the shield leaves out: poverty, trade and seafaring, religion and cult, myths and named heroes. It's a selected world, not an inventory.

### 8. The Shield in Battle (Brann)

- Brann's best point is that we have to remember **what the shield is for**. Achilles carries it into the battles of Books 20–22, half berserk. Aeneas' spear nearly goes through it; "one cannot help wondering what he hits — perhaps the golden dancing youths and maidens?" The river Scamander nearly drowns it.
- When Hector's spear bounces off it, Homer calls it "the fair and artfully made shield". It's the last thing Hector sees before he dies: "a world that has repelled his thrust".
- The adjective for the shield's strong construction (*puka*) can also mean "wisely". So it's at once good defensive equipment and "a work of Hephaestean wisdom".
- In Homer's world, the dancers and the devouring lions, the ploughmen and the ambushes, "are all equally golden". That's the truth about the *Iliad* too: "a blood-and-guts poem of unfailing beauty".
- Her tentative guess: Achilles carries the world "as if he were the power behind the cosmos". The shield gets punctured and dented but never pierced, like a world that can be repaired after war. Maybe Homer thinks of Achilles as the being who makes possible the poetry that makes the world visible. She admits "it's not the clearest thought".

### 9. Assessment

- **The best reading is Taplin's.** The shield isn't a digression or an escape. It's the poem looking at itself from outside: the war in proportion, against everything it destroys. That fits the way the similes work throughout the *Iliad* (Brann: they lay the memory of peaceful labour over the scenes of war). It also explains why it comes exactly here, just after Achilles has chosen death.
- **The irony is in who carries it.** The man holding the image of the good life is the one who has turned his back on it, and the sight of it makes him angrier. It takes Priam in Book 24 for Achilles to weep for his own father and so for the world on his shield.
- **Is it a utopia?** Not quite, as Taplin says: lions, ambush and siege are all there, and Death drags a corpse. But the peaceful scenes are clearly the norm, and conflict is either settled by law (the lawsuit) or kept in a small place. Reinhardt thought the shield too aristocratic and too bright. Lane Fox replies that hired workers and brides of no great rank are there too.
- **The real-artefact question is a dead end**, but the Phoenician bowls matter: they show Homer knew the visual idea of a world in concentric bands, and then did something no craftsman could, by making it move.
- **Orthodox angle.** A few things stand out:
  - **Craft and creation.** Hephaistos is the only Homeric god who makes anything, and even he makes only an *image* of a world he didn't create; the gods in Homer are not creators. Christianity starts at the opposite end: God makes the world from nothing, and human makers (Bezalel filled with the Spirit to make the tabernacle, Exod. 31:1–5) imitate Him.
  - **The church as microcosm.** St Maximus the Confessor (*Mystagogy* 2–4) reads the church building as an image of the whole cosmos, the sanctuary as heaven and the nave as earth, and also as an image of man. The Byzantine dome has Christ the Pantocrator at the centre, then prophets and saints, and the feasts of the year below. That's a world in circles too, but its centre is the face of God, and it is ordered around **worship**. Homer's shield deliberately leaves out cult (Taplin) and leaves out Hades (Brann).
  - **The missing Hades.** On the icon of the Resurrection, Christ stands on the broken gates of Hades and pulls Adam and Eve out. The unseen world isn't left beyond the rim; it's been broken into.
  - **Another kind of shield.** St Paul's armour (Eph. 6:16) has "the shield of faith" against "the fiery darts of the wicked". Its protection doesn't come from a beautiful surface, and the man carrying it isn't fighting "against flesh and blood".
  - **Taplin's last word fits.** The scene the whole poem leads to is Priam kissing Achilles' hands. The fathers who read Homer (Basil's *Address to Young Men* says to take the honey and leave the poison) would have recognized that as the best thing in it.

### Conclusion

The shield of Achilles is Homer's picture of the world, with sky, law, marriage, work, music, wild animals, and war in its small corner, put on the arm of the man whose anger is destroying a city. Taplin's "microcosm, not a utopia" is the key: the shield shows us what the *Iliad* costs. The other readers fill in the rest. Edwards shows how it works as an immense simile in motion, Lane Fox finds the first robots and the first moving pictures, Brann sees Hephaistos as Homer's double and the shield taking spears in battle, and Auden turns it inside out for the twentieth century. It all still looks golden, and that may be the most unsettling thing about it.
