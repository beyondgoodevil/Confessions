The *Odyssey* names its hero only in line 21. Its first line calls him *andra polytropon*, "the man of many turns", which McCrorie renders as "The man, my Muse, resourceful, driven a long way" (1.1). The word can mean that he was turned about, driven off course across the sea, or that he turns things about himself — versatile, devious, many-sided. Both senses run through the poem. Odysseus lies to nearly everyone he meets, changes his name, is disguised as a beggar by his goddess, and tells the Phaeacians the story of his wanderings only after a large dinner. Athena greets him on Ithaca with delight at exactly these qualities:

> "A man must be sneaky and smart to beat you at all your
> wiles — not even a God could, chancing to meet you —
> so richly thoughtful and stubborn! It seems you will never
> stop your lying, not even here in your own land.
> You love such guile and fakery, right from your feet up!"
> (*Odyssey* 13.291–95, McCrorie)

This note sets out five readings of the many-turned man. Benardete's Platonic reading of the poem is covered in [[The Bow and the Lyre: A Platonic Reading of the Odyssey]], and the guest-host rituals Odysseus exploits in [[Xenia: The Homeric Hospitality Scene]].

### 1. Stanford: The Grandson of Autolycus

W. B. Stanford's *The Ulysses Theme* traces Odysseus through three thousand years of literature, but its first chapters on Homer set the terms for later discussion.

**Origins.** Odysseus was not Homer's invention: his name has twelve variant forms in Greek, and over 220 versions of the Cyclops story have been collected "from India to Ireland" (pp. 8–9). Stanford thinks Homer developed a folk-tale "Wily Lad", like Tyl Eulenspiegel, into a more complex figure, and left the clue in the story of the scar (pp. 9–10). Odysseus' maternal grandfather Autolycus, "the very wolf", was in Homer's words better than all men "at swearing and stealing", a gift from Hermes. When asked to name the baby, Autolycus said:

> "Because I came here 'at odds' myself with so many
> on richly nourishing ground, with women and men both,
> let the boy's name be 'Odysseus.'"
> (19.407–09, McCrorie)

Stanford renders the name "Man of Odium". Odysseus was "something of a half-breed… the grandson of a nobleman, on the one side, and of a professional trickster on the other", and so "he was doomed to cleverness at his birth" (p. 12).

**The honest Odysseus of the Iliad.** Stanford's most original observation is that in the *Iliad*, apart from the night raid in Book 10, Odysseus "never exploits a single ruse or deceit… his conduct is scrupulously honest and his words are studiously candid". Yet his reputation is the same as in the *Odyssey*: Helen calls him "adept in all kinds of devices". "Odysseus's wiliness is admitted, though not illustrated" (p. 13). Stanford suggests that Homer meant "the spectacle of a wily, sensitive, and self-controlled man disciplining his personality to fit into a rigid code of heroic conduct", like "an Irish chieftain at the court of Elizabeth I", conscious that any cleverness will confirm suspicion of him (p. 14). Antenor's description of him standing with eyes down, gripping the staff "like any man who knows nothing" (3.216–19) fits this; so does his refusal of Diomedes' praise in Book 10, and his entering only two events at the funeral games (pp. 15–17). The suspicion surfaces twice. Agamemnon insults him in Book 4, and Achilles opens his reply to the embassy with

> As I detest the doorways of Death, I detest that man, who hides one thing in the
> depths of his heart, and speaks forth another.
> (*Iliad* 9.312–13, Lattimore)

Stanford reads this as an oblique strike at Odysseus, who in fact had spoken candidly (p. 18).

**Three kinds of lie.** Stanford borrows the medieval schoolmen's division of lies: fictions told for pleasure, lies told to avoid a greater evil, and malicious lies. Odysseus' Cretan tales belong mainly to the first: "anyone who expects to hear gospel truth from a beggar on his rounds is innocent indeed". Most of his other lies belong to the second, and some are courtesies, like his claim to Alcinous that he himself chose not to follow Nausicaa into town (pp. 20–21). "Nowhere in the *Iliad* or in the *Odyssey* does he tell one of these unforgivable lies against a friend or colleague"; his harmful deceptions are aimed at enemies such as Dolon, the Cyclops and the suitors (p. 22). Homer does not moralise, but he shows the cost. Odysseus distrusts Calypso, Ino and Athena; he tests Penelope beyond caution and lies needlessly to his father. His crew suspect him of hiding loot in Aeolus' bag "because he so rarely told them the whole truth" — and that suspicion cost them their homecoming. Homer, "without condemning them on moral grounds… plainly revealed their insidious effects" (pp. 23–24).

**Athena's favourite.** Athena explains her loyalty in Book 13: "I cannot abandon you now in your sadness, / because your wits are sharp, you're kindly and thoughtful" (13.331–32, McCrorie). Stanford analyses her three Greek words: *epētēs*, "civilized", gentle and attentive to others; *ankhinoos*, quick in hitting the mark; and *ekhephrōn*, "self-possessed", an epithet otherwise used almost only of Penelope (pp. 31–33). That self-possession appears most strikingly when Odysseus forbids Eurycleia to exult over the dead suitors (22.411–12) (p. 34). Stanford's conclusion is that Homer used the relationship to show a man who could rise "above both the selfish trickeries of an Autolycus and the insensate pride of the average Achaean hero": "the only kind of man that one can trust to bring a complex crisis to a safe conclusion is a man like Odysseus" (pp. 40–42).

### 2. Brann: Real Facts Falsely Related

Eva Brann, a philosopher who taught the *Odyssey* for decades at St John's College, writes in short chapters on single moments. Three concern the lies.

**The liar's goddess.** On Ithaca Athena speaks to Odysseus "not as a propitious deity but as a soul mate" (p. 230). After hearing his first Cretan tale she smiles, touches him, takes a woman's form, and says words "such as surely no deity has ever said to a man" (p. 233). Brann insists that "low is what Homer's Odysseus is not". He does not lie to himself, "as does noble Achilles", nor "for himself in a mean and narrow sense. He lies royally, as a king sometimes must who is returning a people in part run wild to its old and just constitution. But there is no denying that he also lies exuberantly, like a natural of the imagination" (p. 236).

**The Cretan liar.** Brann counts seven lying tales: to Athena, Eumaeus, Antinous, Amphinomus, Melantho, Penelope and Laertes. In all but one he is a Cretan (pp. 237–46). She compares the paradox of Epimenides the Cretan, who said all Cretans are liars. Odysseus' tales are reflexive in the same way: he keeps meeting Odysseus in them. "They are facts really lived become lies in the telling — real facts falsely related" (p. 237). The long tale to Eumaeus turns each adventure into a mundane counterpart — "deities into mortals, women into men, kindness into danger, generosity into stinginess, north into south" — a raid in Egypt for the Cicones and Laestrygonians, seven years with an Egyptian king for Calypso. "One might say these lies are a tissue of fact" (pp. 242, 246). The tale to Penelope carries covert messages, and Brann thinks she has seen through his disguise already (p. 244).

**Lying tales against fairy tales.** Brann draws a large conclusion. The lying tales are "collages of fact", built from the ordinary experiences of returning warriors who traded, raided and were shipwrecked; this is probably what Odysseus was in fact doing for ten years. The fabulous adventures in Books 9–12 are "truths told in figures". "The Odyssey is the story of a man in his world; the odyssey is the tale of his soul in its realm… the lying tales relate the — cleverly scrambled — facts of his reasonably expeditious Return in their brute reality; the fairy tales tell the circumstances of his delayed homecoming in their psychic actuality" (pp. 247–48). It is therefore "love's labor lost" to map the wanderings.

### 3. Nagy: Mētis, Noos and the Name No-One

Gregory Nagy reads the many-turned hero through three words. *Mētis*, "craft", is Odysseus' defining quality, as *biē*, "force", is Achilles'. Both poems allude to a dispute over whether Troy would fall by force or craft, and craft won through the Wooden Horse (Hour 10 §17). *Noos*, "mind" or "consciousness", appears in the proem: Odysseus saw many cities "and the men's minds" (1.3). Nagy connects it with *nostos*, "homecoming", through a common root meaning "return to light and life". The lotus-eaters lose their *noos* of home and so cannot go home; the names Antinoos and Alkinoos mean the one who opposes return and the one who brings it about (§§7–10).

The central episode is the Cyclops. Odysseus calls himself *Outis*, "No-one": "My name is No-one: No-one's the name they have / called me" (9.366–67, McCrorie). When the other Cyclopes ask *mē tis*, "surely no one is harming you?", the phrase sounds like *mētis*, "craft" (§16). The trick saves his life but "erases any previous claim to any kleos that the hero would have had". The Cyclops reviles him as *outidanos*, "good-for-nothing", an Iliadic insult. "So Odysseus has suffered a mental erasure. The name that the hero had heretofore achieved for himself has been reduced to nothing and must hereafter be rebuilt from nothing" (§22). He must also refuse the Sirens, who offer an endless song of Troy, "a false Iliad": the Odyssey's hero "must get over the Iliad and get on with the Odyssey" (§§18–20). When he reaches Circe's island, he despairs that there is any *mētis* left in him: "I do not know which place is west and which place is east" (10.190–93; §§38–40).

He rebuilds himself by telling his story among the Phaeacians and then, back on Ithaca, by the *ainoi*, the coded tales, of the beggar. "For this song to succeed, Odysseus must keep adapting his identity by making his noos fit the noos of the many different characters he encounters"; this is why the Sirens call him *poluainos*, "of many tales" (12.184; §42). "He is the ultimate multiform. That is why he is called *polutropos*… 'the one who could change in many different ways who he was'" (§46). Nagy translates the opening differently from McCrorie for this reason.

### 4. Newton: The Rebirth of Odysseus

Rick Newton's article starts from a puzzle. Nausicaa and Athena both tell Odysseus to pass by the king and supplicate Queen Arete, yet after he does so Arete says nothing and Alcinous takes charge. Newton notes three irregularities in the supplication: Odysseus appears suddenly at the queen's knees out of Athena's mist; he lets go of her at once instead of clinging until answered; and he sits a long time "in the ashes beside the fire" on the ground (7.140–69; pp. 6–7).

He finds the parallel in a ritual of rebirth. Diodorus describes Hera adopting Heracles by drawing him through her clothing to the ground, "in imitation of an actual birth". Plutarch tells of a man presumed dead abroad who returned, was barred from the temples, and was told by Delphi to be washed, swaddled and nursed by women "as if he were being born for the first time". Hesychius defines a *deuteropotmos*, "man of second fate", as one thought dead who returns, or one who "for a second time, tumbles through a woman's lap" (pp. 8–9). Odysseus fits this exactly. Telemachus, Penelope, Eurycleia and the suitors all speak of him as dead (p. 10). Calypso's cave, whose name means "concealer", is ringed with cypress, poplar, alder, owls and carrion-crows (p. 11). The storm of Book 5 reads as a birth: stripped naked, girdled with Ino's veil like a caul, then let through the calm river mouth, he lies swollen and speechless with seawater pouring from his mouth, before dropping the veil behind him (pp. 12–13). Nausicaa asks for a wrapper to cover him and her maids clothe and feed him, though he insists on washing himself; Arete's lap completes the ritual; and Alcinous seats him in his own son's chair and offers him Nausicaa (pp. 14–18). Newton connects the naming in Book 9 — "I am Odysseus, son of Laertes" — to the naming that followed a Greek child's acceptance into the family (p. 18).

The psychological point is that "as Odysseus buries the past, he buries that part of his psyche which longs for the carefree existence of the gods". The diffident man weeping on Calypso's shore is not the Odysseus Homer's audience knew; once reborn into the human world, "he proceeds to establish that dauntless identity and sense of self which enables him to declare his name to the Phaeacians with pride" (p. 19). Newton also notes, as Pucci does, that Athena misleads Odysseus about Arete's power, and that she smiles about it later (p. 17).

### 5. Pucci: Disguise, Fiction and the Belly

Pietro Pucci reads the *Odyssey* as a text constantly in dialogue with the *Iliad*, and finds Odysseus' many turns in the poem's own writing.

**Fiction.** After Odysseus' false tale to Penelope, the narrator comments, in Pucci's translation, "While telling many lies he was making them similar to real events" (19.203). The line suggests that the rest of the poem is true, and so "deludes" its readers "into believing that they are privileged and granted the truth" (pp. 98–99). On Ithaca Athena disguises the island so that Odysseus cannot recognise it, then disguises herself to him. She "represents in this scene the aggrandized, divine image of the hero's force and destiny, of his *mētis*", mocking his own need to disguise himself. But "he is shrewd, but he is not a god. Nor is he a poet" (p. 103). Even the goddess's final "true" form is described only with "seemed like", so that "nothing distinguishes identity from semblance of identity", as Hesiod's Muses had said (p. 107). Pucci concludes that *mētis* "obtains the effect of displacing the situation rather than resolving it" (p. 106).

**The belly.** As set out in [[Did Homeric Heroes Have Minds? Decisions, the Gods and the Soul]], Pucci notes that the *Odyssey* reuses the *Iliad*'s lion simile but replaces "his bold heart commands him" with "his belly commands him" (6.133), and that the only Iliadic use of *gastēr* for hunger is Odysseus' insistence that the army eat before battle (*Iliad* 19.225) (pp. 157–69). In the *Odyssey* the belly becomes the force behind all action. Odysseus tells Eumaeus that "no one can hide the squeeze of his belly, / a curse that hands out plenty of evil to mankind. / Because of hunger our strong-planked vessels are well-rigged / for tireless seas and bringing their enemies great harm" (17.286–89, McCrorie). Pucci shows that the lines echo the opening of the *Iliad*, where the accursed thing that "caused countless griefs" was Achilles' wrath (pp. 175–76). Odysseus fighting Irus says the belly drives him; Telemachus replies as if he had said "your spirit and bold heart" (18.53–62), translating the beggar back into the hero (pp. 161–64).

Pucci ties this to Odysseus' storytelling. He names himself to the Phaeacians and tells of his sufferings only after praising the banquet: "as if it were literally true that he could not think of his pains and griefs until his gaster was filled". He becomes their poet, pleasing them for gifts and so deferring his return, like the Sirens charming listeners with tales of death (pp. 185–86). Calling himself No-one, he "dies symbolically as 'Odysseus,' hero of a specific kleos, only to be reborn again" as the man Poseidon hates (p. 183). The *Odyssey* uses the blunt word *gastēr* cautiously, only when Odysseus speaks of human needs in general or plays the beggar, but it "presents it with enormous emphasis… as the instinctual source of all that happens in its narrative" (p. 181).

### 6. Five Readings Compared

| Reader | What "many turns" means | Key episode | Is Odysseus diminished by his lies? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stanford | Inherited wiliness (Autolycus) disciplined by intelligence and gentleness (Athena) | Athena in Book 13; the honest Odysseus of the *Iliad* | No, but distrust costs him and his men |
| Brann | Controlled, exuberant lying that tells the truth in disguise | The seven Cretan tales | No: "he lies royally" |
| Nagy | Adaptability of *noos* and mastery of many *ainoi* | The name No-one; the Sirens | He must lose his Iliadic identity to build an Odyssean one |
| Newton | Death and rebirth into human life | Storm, Nausicaa, Arete's lap | He gains a new self |
| Pucci | Disguise as the poem's own method; the belly as the motive force | Athena's disguise; the lion simile; Irus | His *mētis* displaces rather than resolves; he is not a god or a poet |

### 7. Assessment

The readings fit together better than their methods might suggest.

Stanford's observation about the *Iliad* is the most useful starting point: a man famous for cunning who behaves with scrupulous candour among peers who already suspect him. Achilles' line about the man who hides one thing in his heart shows what the heroic world thought of craft, and the *Odyssey* is in part an answer to that judgement. Brann's account of the lies explains their artistry. They are plausible because they are built from the probable facts of a raider's return, and they let Odysseus test, warn and signal to his listeners. Her suggestion that the Cretan tales may be closer to the brute facts than the fabulous Books 9–12 is speculative, but it is a fruitful way of reading the poem's two kinds of storytelling.

Nagy and Newton describe the same arc in different terms: the hero reduced to nothing — No-one in the Cyclops' cave, "dead" in Calypso's, despairing on Circe's island, naked on the shore — and rebuilt by telling his own story. Nagy's etymologies linking *noos* and *nostos* are bolder than the text strictly requires, but the narrative pattern he and Newton identify is well grounded. Odysseus does name himself with pride only after Arete's hearth and Alcinous' dinner, and the name he gives is "famous for my wiles".

Pucci adds the necessary qualification. The poem admires *mētis* but shows it running into its limits: Athena out-tricks Odysseus repeatedly, his name trick earns Poseidon's hatred, and his lies to friends cost him trust. His thesis about the belly is the most original contribution. It identifies something the *Odyssey* says openly and the *Iliad* suppresses: that hunger, gain and survival drive men to sea and to war as much as honour does. Odysseus is the hero who admits this.

The common answer to what *polytropos* means is that Odysseus' versatility is a form of intelligence, used for survival and homecoming rather than glory, and that the *Odyssey* takes it seriously without endorsing it uncritically. As Stanford puts it, Homer shows the "insidious effects" of deceit without moralising, and makes its deceiver also the man best fitted to restore order at home.

### Conclusion

Stanford traces Odysseus' wiliness to his trickster grandfather and shows Athena valuing it only together with gentleness and self-possession; his Odysseus never lies maliciously to a friend, but pays for his habit of distrust. Brann reads the Cretan tales as "real facts falsely related" and the fabulous wanderings as the soul's version of a mundane return. Nagy sees the hero erase his Iliadic fame by becoming No-one and rebuild himself through *noos* and storytelling into the ultimate multiform. Newton shows Homer staging his return to the human world as a ritual rebirth, completed at Arete's knees. Pucci shows the poem's own disguises and its substitution of the belly for the heroic heart as the motive of human action. The many-turned man is a liar, beggar and survivor, and the *Odyssey* presents him as the hero best fitted for a world where force has failed and homecoming requires craft.
