title: The Question of Christ's Two Wills

summary: Dyothelitism — why two natures mean two wills, and why this does not divide Christ's person.
---

The doctrine of Christ's two wills (dyothelitism) is directly connected to the Chalcedonian Definition, which affirms that Christ has two natures—divine and human—united in one person. Since **will is a property of nature, not person**, Christ must have two wills: a divine will, proper to His divine nature, and a human will, proper to His human nature. These two wills are distinct yet inseparably united in the single divine Person of Christ, working in perfect harmony.

The question of whether two wills imply two subjects is resolved by recognizing that the unity of Christ's person (hypostasis) does not depend on the number of wills or energies but on the fact that both wills belong to the same divine person. The human will of Christ submits freely and entirely to His divine will, as seen in His prayer in Gethsemane: "Not My will, but Yours be done" (Luke 22:42). This submission does not imply division but rather perfect cooperation between the two wills.

The Church Fathers, particularly St. Maximus the Confessor, defended this teaching against monothelitism, which claimed Christ had only one will. St. Maximus argued that denying Christ's human will undermines the fullness of His humanity and the reality of His salvific work. As St. Gregory Nazianzus stated, “What is not assumed is not healed”—if Christ did not assume a human will, then human nature, including the human will, would remain unredeemed.

Thus, dyothelitism preserves the integrity of Christ's incarnation and His work of salvation. The two wills in Christ act in complete unity, demonstrating the fullness of His divine and human natures without dividing His person. This teaching is firmly rooted in the writings of the Cappadocian Fathers, St. Cyril of Alexandria, and the decisions of the Third Council of Constantinople (681), which affirmed dyothelitism as orthodox doctrine.
