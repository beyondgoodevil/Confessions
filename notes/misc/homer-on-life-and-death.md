> "Like the generations of leaves are the generations of men." (Glaucus, *Iliad* 6.146)

Homer's poems have no heaven and no hope of resurrection. Death is the edge of everything, and the gods who watch it never die. Jasper Griffin's *Homer on Life and Death* (1980) is the classic study. The copy in my folder is a scan without text, so these notes build on Clarke, Kearns and the commentaries, which draw heavily on Griffin.

## 1. The Two Planes: Carefree Gods, Wretched Men

- Book 1 of the *Iliad* ends with the gods **laughing** at a feast, while Achilles' anger is about to send "many sinewy ghosts to Hades". Thetis: "Alas my child, why did I bear you... your portion of life is short" (1.414–18).
- The gods drink **nectar**, a name which may originally have meant "that which overcomes death". In the *Hymn to Apollo* the Muses sing of "those pains that humans endure... [who] can find no cure for death or defence against old age".
- **The gods "live at ease"** (*rheia zōontes*). Human suffering is "a trivial matter, a foil to the cheerful ease" of the gods (Clarke).
- **The common Near Eastern wisdom.** *Gilgamesh*: "When the gods created mankind, death they dispensed to mankind, life they kept for themselves." Hesiod: "the gods keep life hidden from mankind."
- **Achilles to Priam** (24.525–6): "So have the gods spun the thread for wretched mortals, that they should live in sorrow, while they themselves are free from care."
- **Double motivation** (Kearns, following Dodds): the same event has a divine and a human cause. "Which God caused them to quarrel?" is the poem's first question, yet the men are still responsible.
- Kearns: Homer took "a way of speaking about the Gods which is properly metaphorical" and made it literal, "pushed to its limits". That is why "belief in the Gods of Homer could never be fully given and yet could not exactly be withheld either".

## 2. Glory as Substitute Immortality

**Sarpedon's speech** (12.310–28) is the classic statement:

> "Dear friend, if by escaping this war we could go on to live ageless and immortal forever, I would not fight myself in the front rank... but since in truth the demons of death stand around us innumerable, whom no man can flee or elude, let us go forth, whether we give some other man cause to boast, or he give it to us."

- Fame (***kleos***) is "a kind of surrogate immortality" (Clarke). The poem itself is what keeps it alive. Helen: Zeus gave us an evil fate "so that hereafter we may be a song for men to come" (6.357–8).
- **Mortality is the foundation of the ethic.** If we were gods, there would be no need for courage. *Because* we die, glory matters.
- Sarpedon is **Zeus's own son**. Zeus wants to save him; Hera says he must die like the rest. Zeus's only concession: Sleep and Death carry his body home (16.431–61), and Zeus weeps "tears of blood" on the earth.
- **No reward beyond the grave in the *Iliad***: "Hades is a place of darkness and decay." No heaven, no Elysium, no hero-cult (Clarke). Menelaus' Elysium in the *Odyssey* (4.561–9) isn't an afterlife but an *alternative* to death, granted only because he is Zeus's son-in-law.

## 3. The Hero: Half-God, Half-Beast

- **"Hero"** (*hērōs*) in Homer doesn't mean a brave person. It means a member of a **race**: the "race of half-gods" (*hēmitheōn genos andrōn*, 12.23).
  - In Hesiod's five ages (gold, silver, bronze, heroes, iron), the heroes are "an interlude of virtue and strength" just before our degenerate Iron Age.
  - They can lift stones "that two men of today could not lift".
- ***Menos*** is "the force of onrushing energy" in body and mind, carried by fluids like blood. The heroic virtue is ***agēnoriē***, "excessive manhood".
- **The danger.** Excess *menos* pushes a man toward **madness**: "this man is raging (*mainetai*) too much" (6.100–1). *Menos* and *mainomai* share a root. The raging hero is "poised between two extremes", reaching for divine glory or descending into a beast's fury (Clarke).
- **Epithets** ("blameless" is used even of Aegisthus) give a fixed, simple heroic image, against which the story shows the complications.
- **Benardete**: the poem is called the *Iliad*, not the *Achilleid*, because Homer "wished to show us not only Achilles but also, in a way, all heroes" (Porphyry). Achilles "holds within himself all the heroic virtues that are given singly to others". The repeated epithets aren't fillers: some stay neutral, others become charged in context, and both are needed.

## 4. Achilles

- His anger is ***mēnis***, a word otherwise used almost only of the **anger of gods**. He is "agonisingly close to divinity": son of a goddess, great-grandson of Zeus.
- **The choice** (9.410–16): long life without fame at home, or short life with "imperishable glory" (*kleos aphthiton*) at Troy. In Book 9 he rejects the heroic life altogether and says he will go home. A soul can't be bought back once it has passed the barrier of the teeth.
- **After Patroclus dies**, revenge replaces honour. He knows his death follows Hector's and accepts it (18.79–126). Athena sets a flame on his head; his shout kills twelve men. He is "transfigured, superhuman, blazing".
- **Then the beast**:
  - he refuses suppliants, "no oaths between lions and men" (22.262);
  - he drags Hector's body;
  - Apollo: "Achilles has destroyed pity, and there is no shame (*aidōs*) in him... like a lion" (24.39–45).
- **Plato's Socrates** cites Achilles as his model in the *Apology* (28b–d). Not for his virtue, but for "the unflinching determination with which he accepts that death will be the price".
- **Benardete's epilogue**:
  - "Achilles is his wrath, and his wrath is his fate... He only becomes visible when he is about to die."
  - **The tragic hero vs the comic hero.** Achilles can't lie, can't hide, "is forever trapped in the Cyclops' cave". Odysseus "talked his way out of" death; "Achilles made his way into his death".
  - "There is no non-Homeric Achilles; but there would be an Odysseus without Homer."

## 5. Hector

- A more ordinary man, "remote from divine ancestry", driven by duty to city and family.
- **Andromache on the wall** (6.407): "your own *menos* will destroy you". He knows Troy will fall (6.448) and goes back anyway, for shame. He prays that his son will be "far better than his father". The baby is frightened of the helmet's plume, and he laughs and takes it off.
- **His madness too**: "He raged like Ares... foam appeared round his mouth" (15.605–10), wolf-madness. He also dreams of being a god: "If only I were immortal and ageless... honoured as Athena and Apollo" (8.538–41).
- **His end** (22.297–305). Tricked by Athena, alone, he knows the gods have called him to death: "Let me not die without an effort, without fame, but after doing some great deed that future men will still hear of."

## 6. Achilles and Priam: The End of the *Iliad*

- **Book 24.** Priam, guided by Hermes, comes by night to Achilles' hut and **kisses the hands** "that killed so many of his sons". "I have endured what no man on earth has yet endured."
- **Both weep**: Priam for Hector, Achilles for his father Peleus and for Patroclus.
- **The jars of Zeus** (24.525–33): two jars stand on Zeus's floor, one of evils, one of blessings. To some he gives a mixture, to others only evils, and they wander the earth honoured by neither gods nor men.
  - Richardson: a "gnomic" consolation which "foreshadows the themes of later *consolationes*": suffering is common to all, weeping does no good, others have endured worse.
  - Ancient readers argued whether there were two jars or three (Pindar three, Plato two).
  - The scholia think Zeus's opening speech in the *Odyssey*, that men blame the gods for suffering they bring on themselves (*Od.* 1.32–43), deliberately answers Achilles.
- **Niobe** (24.602–17): even Niobe, whose twelve children were killed, remembered to eat. They eat together. Then a truce for Hector's funeral.
- The poem ends not with Achilles' death but with Hector's burial: "Such were the funeral rites of Hector, tamer of horses."

## 7. *Odyssey* 11: The Shades

- **The *Nekyia***, the summoning of the dead (Heubeck): "no other adventure is so dangerous or comes so near to the limits of human experience". Odysseus "approaches the borderline between life and death itself".
- **Sources** combined: necromancy (a trench, sheep's blood, the dead drinking to speak), the hero's descent (like Heracles), folk-tale, and the Anthesteria belief that the dead mingle with the living. Meuli saw shamanism in it. Heubeck thinks the poet unified it all.
- **The structure**:
  - Elpenor, Tiresias, Odysseus' mother Anticleia;
  - a catalogue of heroines;
  - an interlude;
  - Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax;
  - the great sinners of the past (Tityos, Tantalus, Sisyphus) and Heracles' phantom.
- **What a shade is.** A *psychē* is an image (*eidōlon*) without strength, unable to speak until it drinks blood. Anticleia slips three times through her son's arms "like a shadow or a dream". This is what happens to all: "the sinews no longer hold flesh and bone, but the fire's force destroys them... and the soul, like a dream, flutters away."
- **Achilles' reply** (11.488–91), the bleakest line in Homer. Odysseus says he rules among the dead. Achilles: "Do not speak soothingly to me of death... I would rather be a serf to some landless man... than be king over all the dead." He asks only about his father and son. Told that Neoptolemus fought bravely, he strides away "with great steps over the meadow of asphodel, rejoicing".
- **Ajax** refuses to speak to Odysseus even in death: anger outlasts life.
- **Punishments** (Tityos, Tantalus, Sisyphus) exist for a few great offenders against the gods. Dodds and others note this anticipates a later moralized afterlife. See [[Dionysus, Orpheus and the Mysteries: Dodds and the Irrational]].
- Its influence: *Aeneid* 6 and Dante's *Inferno*.

## 8. The *Odyssey*'s Answer

Clarke: "Where the *Iliad* problematises heroic excellence... the *Odyssey* moves below and beyond the glamour of heroism to a more fundamental level of the human condition, where the hero succeeds only by accepting the inevitability of his lowliness."

- **Odysseus refuses immortality.** Calypso offers to make him "immortal and ageless" (5.135–6). He chooses mortal Penelope and home. The epic of human limits chooses mortal life over divine ease.
- **He is reduced to nothing**: shipwrecked, naked, a beggar in his own house, hearing a bard sing his own deeds while he weeps.
- **His warning to the suitor Amphinomus** (18.130–7): "Of all that breathes and creeps on the earth, nothing is feebler than man... the mind of men is such as the day the father of gods and men brings upon them."
- **After the slaughter**, when Eurycleia begins to crow: "It is unholy to boast over slain men" (22.412). The suitors fell by their own folly and the gods' justice. Clarke: "It is in this moment, more than any other, that the depth of Odysseus' humanity is revealed."
- **Eumaeus**, born a king's son and made a slave, has learned piety through suffering.

## 9. After Homer

How later Greeks and Christians answered him:
- **Mysteries and Orphics**: a better lot for the initiated; the soul as divine and immortal; the body as prison. See [[Dionysus, Orpheus and the Mysteries: Dodds and the Irrational]].
- **Plato**: in *Republic* 3 he wants Achilles' "I would rather be a serf" and the gibbering ghosts **struck out** of education, because they make men fear death. The *Phaedo* and the myth of Er give the soul a real afterlife. See [[The Good in Plato]].
- **The allegorists** read Odysseus' voyage as the soul's journey home. See [[How Philosophers Saved Homer: Allegory from the Stoics to the Byzantines]].
- **The Old Testament** has its own Homeric-sounding Sheol: "the dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence" (Ps 115:17); "in death there is no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?" (Ps 6:5); "a living dog is better than a dead lion" (Eccl 9:4). But it also has hope: "I know that my redeemer liveth... yet in my flesh shall I see God" (Job 19:25–26); "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life" (Dan 12:2).

## 10. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What Homer sees truly**
- **Death as enemy.** Homer doesn't prettify it. Achilles' line is more honest than much later consolation, and it agrees with Scripture's "last enemy" (1 Cor 15:26) against every philosophy that makes death natural, friendly or nothing. See [[1 Corinthians 15:26: The Last Enemy That Will Be Destroyed Is Death]].
- **The body matters.** A Homeric man without a body is a shadow, not a freed soul. That is closer to the biblical view of the human person, a body–soul unity, than Orphic and Platonic dualism. The Christian answer to Achilles isn't "your soul is happy without your body" but "the dead shall be raised incorruptible" (1 Cor 15:52). See [[On the Soul and the Resurrection]].
- **Achilles and Priam.** Two enemies weep together over their dead and eat together. It's a moment of shared mortality, the closest the *Iliad* comes to love of enemies. The Gospel commands what Homer only glimpses in one night.
- **Hector's prayer** for his son, and his taking off the helmet, show the tenderness that makes death tragic.

**Where Homer leaves us**
- **Glory as substitute immortality** is what most people still live by: legacy, reputation, being remembered. Sarpedon is honest that it's second best ("if we could live ageless and immortal..."). Christianity offers the first best: "This is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God" (John 17:3). Real immortality is not fame but union with God, who is "not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Matt 22:32). See [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- **Carefree gods** watching wretched men are the opposite of the God who "was made flesh" and wept at Lazarus' tomb (John 11:35). Homer's gods have nectar, "that which overcomes death"; the Christian receives the Body and Blood, "the medicine of immortality" (Ignatius, *Ephesians* 20).
- **The descent into Hades.** Odysseus visits the dead and can't hold his mother. Christ descends into Hades and **raises** Adam and Eve by the hand: the icon of the Resurrection, *Anastasis*. "By death He trampled down death, and upon those in the tombs bestowing life."
