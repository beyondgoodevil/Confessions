Three ways to do ethics:

| | Asks | Right action is |
|---|---|---|
| **Deontology** (Kant) | what is my duty? | following the right rule |
| **Consequentialism** (Mill) | what will turn out best? | producing the best outcome |
| **Virtue ethics** (Aristotle) | what kind of person should I be? how should I live? | what a virtuous person would characteristically do |

Virtue ethics looks at a whole life, not isolated acts. The Fathers' ethics is much closer to this than to the other two, which is why it's worth understanding.

## 1. Aristotle's Terms

| Term | Means |
|---|---|
| ***arete*** | excellence. Of a knife: cutting. Of a human: living well as a rational being |
| ***eudaimonia*** | flourishing, the best kind of life. Not "happiness" as a feeling. Wanted for itself, and everything else for it |
| ***phronesis*** | practical wisdom: seeing what matters in *this* situation and how to act. Comes mostly with experience |
| **the mean** | virtue lies between excess and deficiency, relative to the person and situation. Courage between rashness and cowardice |
| **habituation** | we become just by doing just acts, like builders by building. Then it becomes second nature |

- **The function argument**: everything has a function; man's is reason; so the good life is activity of soul in accordance with reason, i.e. with virtue.
- The mean isn't "moderation in all things". Sometimes the right amount of anger is a lot.
- **Continence** (*enkrateia*): doing the right thing while wanting the wrong one. Better than vice, short of virtue. The virtuous person *wants* the good.
- Virtue is necessary for flourishing but (Aristotle) not sufficient: you also need some luck, health, friends. Plato and the Stoics: virtue alone is enough.

## 2. The Revival

- **Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958)**: "moral obligation" only makes sense if there's a divine lawgiver. Modern philosophers dropped the lawgiver and kept the "ought", which now hangs in mid-air. Either go back to God, or go back to Aristotle and talk about virtue and flourishing.
- **Philippa Foot**, **Rosalind Hursthouse**: neo-Aristotelian. Virtues are grounded in what human beings, as a kind of living thing, need to flourish.
- **Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981)**: modern moral argument is interminable because it uses fragments of an older scheme (man as he is → man as he could be if he realised his *telos*) with the *telos* removed. Virtues live in **practices** and **traditions**.
- **Michael Slote** (agent-based), **Christine Swanton** (target-centred), **Linda Zagzebski** (exemplarist: we learn goodness from admirable people).
- **Robert Adams**: goodness is resemblance to God; virtue is being for the good. A theistic version.

Anscombe's point is the same as [[Is Ought]] from the other side.

## 3. Objections and Replies

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| **No guidance**: "be virtuous" doesn't tell me what to do | virtue terms do guide: "don't do what's dishonest, cruel, cowardly". And rules have never settled hard cases either |
| **Circular**: right = what the virtuous do; virtuous = does what's right | the virtues are defined by flourishing and human nature, not by "right" |
| **Relativism**: virtues differ by culture | Nussbaum: the virtues are the same (how to handle fear, desire, others' needs); local *understandings* differ. And the other theories face the same problem |
| **Conflict**: honesty vs kindness | practical wisdom; and Kant has conflicts of duty too |
| **Egoism**: it's about *my* flourishing | the virtues are mostly about others; the good person's good and others' good coincide |
| **Situationism**: psychology experiments show traits are unstable | the experiments show most people *aren't* virtuous, which Aristotle said. Virtue is rare and hard |
| **Moral luck**: upbringing decides your character | yes. The good life is fragile. That's the human situation |

## 4. Cardinal and Theological Virtues

- **Four cardinal** ("hinge") virtues, from Plato's *Republic* via the Stoics and Ambrose: **prudence** (practical wisdom), **justice**, **courage** (fortitude), **temperance**. Wisdom 8:7 has the same four.
- **Three theological** (1 Cor 13:13): **faith**, **hope**, **love**. Aquinas: the cardinal virtues can be acquired by practice; the theological ones only *infused* by God.
- The Eastern sources don't use this 4 + 3 grid much. They organise by passions and their opposites. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].

## 5. The Fathers

*Mine.*

**Where they agree with Aristotle**

- Ethics is about becoming a certain kind of person, not ticking off acts.
- Virtue is a habit, formed by practice. "Train thyself rather unto godliness" (1 Tim 4:7). The Greek is *gymnaze*.
- The passions aren't wiped out but trained and redirected.
- Practical wisdom: the Fathers' **discernment** (*diakrisis*), which St. Anthony calls the greatest virtue, because it keeps all the others from excess. Close to *phronesis*, and close to the mean.
- Learning by example: the Lives of the Saints are an exemplarist ethics.

**Where they change it**

| Aristotle | The Fathers |
|---|---|
| goal: *eudaimonia*, flourishing as a rational animal | goal: **theosis**, union with God. Flourishing is fulfilled *beyond* nature |
| virtue acquired by our effort | virtue is **synergy**: grace and effort together. Without Christ "ye can do nothing" (John 15:5) |
| top virtue: contemplation, or magnanimity (the great-souled man, who knows his worth) | top virtues: **love** and **humility**. Aristotle's great-souled man would look to the Fathers like vainglory |
| the passions are natural, to be moderated | many passions are *diseases* of the soul from the Fall, to be healed |
| a self-sufficient citizen of the *polis* | a member of the Body of Christ; the Church as the community of practice |
| tragedy and luck can ruin a good life | suffering borne in Christ becomes part of salvation |

- **The body**: Aristotle has no fasting. For the Fathers ascesis of the body is part of virtue, since the whole person is being trained. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]] sec. 7.
- **Image and likeness**: the virtues are God's own qualities. Growing in virtue = becoming like God. That gives virtue ethics the grounding Anscombe said was missing. See [[Image and Likeness]].
- **Answer to "why be moral?"**: not duty (Kant), not utility (Mill), not only my flourishing (Aristotle). Because we were made to share God's life, and virtue is what sharing it looks like.

**And Aquinas**

- Aquinas is the great synthesis of Aristotle and Christian ethics in the West: acquired cardinal virtues + infused theological ones, natural law as the framework.
- The Eastern route is less systematic and more ascetic and therapeutic: the passions as illnesses, the spiritual father as physician.
