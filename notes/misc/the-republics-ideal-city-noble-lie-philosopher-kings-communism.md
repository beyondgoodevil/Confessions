The city Socrates builds in the *Republic*, which he later calls **Callipolis** ("beautiful city"), is the most famous and most attacked political design in Western thought. It is founded on a **noble lie** about the citizens' birth. It is guarded by soldiers and rulers who own no property, have no private families and may not touch gold. In Book V Socrates proposes three "**waves**" of paradox: women should be guardians alongside men; the guardians should share wives and children; and the city will never be well governed until **philosophers rule as kings**. Readers have called the result a blueprint for totalitarianism, an ironic warning against utopias, a feminist manifesto, and a picture of the just soul written large. This note follows the main passages in Bloom's translation, then sets out the debates using Malcolm Schofield, Julia Annas and Donald Morrison.

### 1. Where the City Comes From

In Book II Socrates proposes to look for justice in a city before looking for it in a soul, because there it will be written larger. He first sketches a simple city of farmers and craftsmen, which he calls "true" and "healthy". Glaucon dismisses it as a "city of sows" (in other translations, a "city of pigs") and asks for couches, relishes and luxuries. Socrates agrees to describe a "feverish" city instead. Luxury breeds war, war needs professional soldiers, and the soldiers or **guardians** need careful education (Books II–III). Out of the guardians Socrates then selects **rulers**, who must be wise, capable, "and above all people who care for the city" (412c, as Schofield summarizes it). Everything in this note comes from the attempt to make sure that people with power will care for the city and not exploit it.

### 2. The Noble Lie (414b–415d)

Socrates has already said that lies may be useful as a "remedy" or drug. Only rulers may use them, "for the benefit of the city in cases involving enemies or citizens", just as only doctors may give drugs. For a private man to lie to the rulers is worse than for a patient to lie to his doctor (389b–c). Now he proposes one big lie:

> Could we ... somehow contrive one of those lies that come into being in case of need, of which we were just now speaking, some one noble lie to persuade, in the best case, even the rulers, but if not them, the rest of the city? (414b–c)

He calls it "a Phoenician thing", an old tale "that requires a great deal of persuasion". Glaucon says, "It wasn't for nothing that you were for so long ashamed to tell the lie" (414e). The tale has two parts.
1. **Born from the earth.** The citizens are to be told that their upbringing and education "were like dreams". They were really being formed underground, and the earth, "their mother", sent them up. So they must defend the land as a mother and nurse, and "think of the other citizens as brothers and born of the earth" (414d–e).
2. **The metals.** The god "mixed gold" into those fit to rule, silver into the auxiliaries (soldiers), and "iron and bronze" into the farmers and craftsmen. Children usually resemble their parents, but not always. The rulers must watch the children above all. A bronze child of golden parents must be "thrust out among the craftsmen or the farmers" without pity, and a golden child of bronze parents raised up. There is an oracle "that the city will be destroyed when an iron or bronze man is its guardian" (415a–c).

Can anyone be made to believe this? "None at all," says Glaucon, "for these men themselves; however for their sons and their successors" (415c–d). Socrates says that even so, "that would be good for making them care more for the city and one another" (415d).

### 3. Guardians Who Own Nothing (415d–417b)

Socrates then houses the guardians like a military camp. The worst danger is that the city's "dogs" become "wolves", "savage masters instead of well-meaning allies" (416a–b). So:
- "No one will possess any private property except for what's entirely necessary."
- No one will have "any house or storeroom into which everyone who wishes cannot come".
- They will receive food from the other citizens "as a wage for their guarding", with no surplus, and eat together "like soldiers in a camp".
- They are told they have divine gold and silver in their souls, so "it is not lawful to handle and to touch gold and silver, nor to go under the same roof with it ... nor to drink from silver or gold" (416d–417a).

The reason is stated clearly: "Whenever they'll possess private land, houses, and currency, they'll be householders and farmers instead of guardians, and they'll become masters and enemies instead of allies of the other citizens" (417a–b). They would end up "far more afraid of the enemies within than those without".

### 4. Are the Guardians Happy? (419a–421c)

Book IV opens with Adeimantus' objection. The city really belongs to the guardians, yet they have no lands, no fine houses, no money. They "look exactly like mercenary auxiliaries who sit in the city and do nothing but keep watch" (419a). Socrates answers with one of the Republic's key principles:

> We are not looking to the exceptional happiness of any one group among us but, as far as possible, that of the city as a whole. (420b)

He compares the city to a painted statue. You do not paint the eyes purple just because eyes are the most beautiful part; you give each part what suits it "and make the whole fair". You could dress farmers in robes and let potters recline at banquets, but then "the farmer won't be a farmer, nor the potter a potter" (420c–421a). The guardians must be the best possible guardians, and "nature" will assign each group its share of happiness (421c). He adds that both wealth and poverty corrupt craftsmen, so the guardians must keep both out of the city (421d–422a).

### 5. The First Wave: Women Guardians (451c–457b)

In Book V Polemarchus again tugs someone's cloak, echoing the first scene of Book I, and the whole company refuses to let Socrates skip the subject of women and children (449b–450a). Socrates is reluctant because the argument "might seem to be a prayer". He says it is "a lesser fault to prove to be an unwilling murderer of someone than a deceiver about fine, good, and just things in laws" (450d–451a). Having "completely finished the male drama", he turns to "the female" (451c).

The argument starts from dogs.
- Female watchdogs guard and hunt with the males. So if women are to do the same work as men, they need the same education in music, gymnastics and war (451d–452a).
- People will laugh at the thought of women, even old ones, exercising naked in the gymnasium. But not long ago the Greeks laughed at naked men too, and "he is empty who believes anything is ridiculous other than the bad" (452c–e).
- The serious objection is that the city was built on "one nature, one job", and women's nature is different. Socrates calls this "eristic, not dialectic": it fixes on the word "nature" without asking *which* difference is relevant. Bald men and long-haired men also have different natures, but nobody thinks that bars one group from shoemaking (454a–c).
- The only difference cited, "that the female bears and the male mounts", has nothing to do with guarding a city (454d–e).

So "there is no practice of a city's governors which belongs to woman because she's woman, or to man because he's man; but the natures are scattered alike among both" (455d). Socrates adds at once: "but in all of them woman is weaker than man". Glaucon concedes that "many women are better than many men in many things" (455d). The conclusion is that the proposal is "according to nature. Rather, the way things are nowadays proves to be, as it seems, against nature" (456c). The women guardians "must strip, since they'll clothe themselves in virtue instead of robes" (457a).

### 6. The Second Wave: No Private Families (457c–466d)

The second wave is "far bigger":

> All these women are to belong to all these men in common, and no woman is to live privately with any man. And the children, in their turn, will be in common, and neither will a parent know his own offspring, nor a child his parent. (457c–d)

Socrates postpones the question of possibility, like "idle men" who daydream about what they want before asking whether it can happen (458a). The details are startling.
- **Breeding.** The guardians will be drawn together by "not geometrical but erotic necessities", as Glaucon puts it (458d). The city will breed people as Glaucon breeds his hunting dogs and fighting cocks, from the best and from those in their prime (459a–b).
- **Lies about the lottery.** "It's likely that our rulers will have to use a throng of lies and deceptions for the benefit of the ruled", as a kind of drug (459c–d). Marriages will be arranged at festivals by "subtle lots", rigged so that "the ordinary man will blame chance rather than the rulers" (460a). Brave young men will be rewarded with more frequent intercourse (460b).
- **Children.** Babies will be taken to a common nursery. Mothers will nurse without knowing which child is theirs. "Those of the worse, and any of the others born deformed, they will hide away in an unspeakable and unseen place, as is seemly" (460c).
- **Ages.** Women bear children for the city from twenty to forty, and men father them from the time "he passes his swiftest prime at running" up to fifty-five. Children conceived outside these rules are not to be raised (460e–461c).
- **Kinship.** Everyone born seven to ten months after a man's "wedding" counts as his child, so that each guardian has hundreds of "fathers", "brothers" and "sons" (461d).

The justification is **unity**:

> Have we any greater evil for a city than what splits it and makes it many instead of one? Or a greater good than what binds it together and makes it one? (462a–b)

The best city is one where everyone says "my own" and "not my own" about the same things. It is "most like a single human being": when one finger is hurt, the whole person feels the pain (462c–d). In other cities the people call the rulers "masters". In this one they call them "saviors and auxiliaries", and the rulers call the people "wage givers and supporters" (463a–b). Without private property or families there will be no lawsuits over money or relatives, no flattering of the rich, and no worry about debts. The guardians will live "a life more blessed than that most blessed one the Olympic victors live" (465d). So the answer to Adeimantus comes back: a guardian who wants more learns that Hesiod was right that "the half is more than the whole" (466c).

Socrates adds rules for war. Children watch battles on fast horses. Brave soldiers may kiss whomever they like during a campaign. Greeks must not enslave Greeks or ravage Greek land. A war between Greeks is not really war but "faction", a sign that "Greece is sick" (467a–471c).

### 7. The Third Wave: Philosopher-Kings (471c–480a)

Glaucon finally demands to know whether this city is possible. Socrates says he is bringing "the biggest and most difficult, the third wave" (472a). First he lowers the bar. They were looking for a "pattern" (*paradeigma*) of justice. A painter who draws the most beautiful possible man is no worse a painter because he cannot prove such a man could exist. "Is it the nature of acting to attain to less truth than speaking?" So it is enough to find the smallest change that would bring a real city close to the pattern (472b–473b). Then the wave breaks:

> Unless ... the philosophers rule as kings or those now called kings and chiefs genuinely and adequately philosophize, and political power and philosophy coincide in the same place, while the many natures now making their way to either apart from the other are by necessity excluded, there is no rest from ills for the cities, my dear Glaucon, nor I think for human kind. (473c–d)

He expected it to "drown me in laughter and ill repute". Glaucon warns that many important men will "throw off their clothes" and run at him with whatever weapon is at hand (474a).

Socrates must now say who the philosophers are. A lover of boys loves every boy in bloom, a lover of wine loves every wine, a lover of honour takes honour wherever he can get it. So the philosopher loves all wisdom, not one part of it (474c–475c). The "lovers of sights and sounds" who run to every festival are not philosophers. The true ones are "the lovers of the sight of the truth" (475e). They see the beautiful itself, not just many beautiful things. The man who sees only beautiful things is "living in a dream", taking a likeness for the thing itself (476c). The difference between them is the difference between knowledge and **opinion** (476d–480a), which leads into the Sun, the Line and the Cave in Books VI–VII.

### 8. The Ship of State and the Useless Philosopher (487b–489a)

Adeimantus objects that, in real life, people who stay with philosophy turn out either odd and vicious or, at best, "useless" to their cities. Socrates agrees, and explains with an image (488a–489a).
- **The owner.** A ship's owner is taller and stronger than everyone aboard but "rather deaf", shortsighted and ignorant of navigation. He stands for the people.
- **The sailors.** They quarrel over who should steer, though none has learned how. They insist that steering "isn't even teachable", and drug the owner "with mandrake, drink, or something else" so they can take over and feast.
- **The real pilot.** The man who actually studies the art of steering, the seasons, the sky and the winds, is called "a stargazer, a prater and useless".

The fault, Socrates says, lies not with philosophy but with the people who will not use it. This is the anti-democratic heart of the *Republic*. Ruling is an expertise like navigation, and an assembly cannot judge it.

### 9. Compelling the Philosophers to Go Back Down (519c–521b, 540a–b)

After the allegory of the Cave, Socrates draws the political conclusion. Those who have seen the Good will not want to return to the prisoners. So:

> Then our job as founders ... is to compel the best natures to go to the study which we were saying before is the greatest, to see the good and to go up that ascent; and, when they have gone up and seen sufficiently, not to permit them what is now permitted. ... To remain there. (519c–d)

Glaucon asks, "Are we to do them an injustice, and make them live a worse life when a better is possible for them?" Socrates repeats the principle from Book IV: the law does not aim at the happiness of one class but at "harmonizing the citizens by persuasion and compulsion" (519e–520a). Philosophers who grow up "spontaneously against the will of the regime" in other cities owe those cities nothing. But these philosophers were raised by the city "like leaders and kings in hives", so "you must go down, each in his turn, into the common dwelling of the others". Having seen the truth, they will see in the dark "ten thousand times better" than the prisoners (520b–c).

The paradox of reluctant rule from Book I returns as the city's great advantage:

> That city in which those who are going to rule are least eager to rule is necessarily governed in the way that is best and freest from faction. (520d)

"If you discover a life better than ruling for those who are going to rule, it is possible that your well-governed city will come into being", because then only "the really rich" will rule, "rich not in gold but ... in a good and prudent life" (520e–521a). The only such life is "true philosophy" (521b). Later Socrates adds that the philosophers will rule in turns and spend most of their time on philosophy. They treat ruling not as something fine but as something necessary (540a–b).

### 10. Everyone Over Ten Must Leave (540d–541b)

At the end of Book VII Socrates insists the city is "not wishful thinking": it is "hard for them to come about but not impossible" (540d). Then he gives the quickest way to found it. The philosopher-rulers will send "all those in the city who happen to be older than ten ... out to the country". They will take over the children and raise them, "far away from those dispositions they now have from their parents", in the new laws. Glaucon calls this "by far the quickest and easiest way" (540e–541a). Readers have argued ever since about whether this is meant as a plan or as a warning.

### 11. The Pattern Laid Up in Heaven (592a–b)

The last word on the city comes at the end of Book IX. The just man, Socrates says, will take part in politics "in his own city", but perhaps not in his actual homeland "unless some divine chance" happens. Glaucon understands that he means the city "that has its place in speeches, since I don't suppose it exists anywhere on earth". Socrates replies:

> But in heaven ... perhaps, a pattern is laid up for the man who wants to see and found a city within himself on the basis of what he sees. It doesn't make any difference whether it is or will be somewhere. For he would mind the things of this city alone, and of no other. (592b)

### 12. Is the Noble Lie Really a Lie?

Some readers have tried to soften the noble lie. Cornford translated it "bold flight of invention", and Hannah Arendt argued that the Greek *pseudos* could mean "fiction" and that "under no circumstances" was Plato recommending lying "as we understand it". **Schofield** rejects this: "Arendt was simply wrong." The noble lie is introduced explicitly as one of the useful lies "which we were talking about a little while back". Those lies began with the mad friend's weapons in Book I, and the rulers' lies about the marriage lottery in Book V are the same kind of thing (*Companion*, ch. 6). He quotes Ferrari's point that the Greek *gennaios* can mean "noble" but also, colloquially, a "true-blue" lie, as in "grand larceny". He quotes Nietzsche too, who congratulated Plato on "a real lie, a genuine, resolute, 'honest' lie".

Schofield sets the lie against its context.
- **Athenian democracy.** Democratic Athens prized truth-telling in the assembly and thought lying was what Spartans did. Thucydides' Diodotus complains that in a degraded democracy "a person who has something good to say must tell lies in order to be believed". Plato's Socrates calmly accepts this condition as no worse than lying to a child to get it to take medicine.
- **Plato's distinction.** He draws on the *Republic*'s own distinction in Book II. The "true lie" is ignorance "in the soul of the person who has been deceived". A "lie in words" is only an image of it. On that view the rulers, who are not themselves deceived, are not lying in the worst sense.
- **The real tension.** Philosophers are defined as people who hate falsehood. So "even as they tell politically expedient lies, philosopher rulers will hate doing it".
- **Why the lie is needed.** Rulers must love the city, and Socrates says we love what we think shares our interest. But the only reason Book I gave good men for ruling was the fear of being ruled by worse men. That makes ruling "unavoidable", not good, and cannot produce love. The noble lie fills this gap. It makes citizens "care more about the city and one another" by telling them they are brothers born of one mother. Seen as part of educating children, Schofield thinks, the lie looks less sinister. Every society has founding myths that tell people who they are.

**Annas** is less forgiving. Plato's distinction between lies "in the soul" and "in words" does not save the rulers, "for one could avoid the lie in the soul by lying with bad intent as much as by lying with good intent". Even if the rulers come to believe the myth, they believe it "on a rather different level from the others", so there is "at the least, a double standard" (ch. 4, pp. 107–8).

### 13. Was Plato a Totalitarian?

The fiercest modern attack came from Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945). As Schofield summarizes it, Popper read the noble lie as a "totalitarian theory of morality" that licenses deceiving citizens for the good of "the state". Popper thought the *Republic* was "not so much ... a theoretical treatise, but ... a topical manifesto". Annas quotes this against Guthrie's reply: "These are not so much the machinations of a totalitarian monster as the dreams of an impractical theorist" (ch. 7, p. 185).

Annas takes a middle line.
- **The inequality is real.** The producers have all the property but no power and no freedom to change their way of life. Plato divorces power from wealth: the rulers have power and nothing else. So "Plato does, then, insist on the most extreme inequality of power that there could be".
- **But not tyranny.** It is too quick to call him "a monstrous totalitarian". In his eyes the ruled are "friends" of the rulers, and are ruled by people who know their interests better than they do. The obvious objection is that "it is not in human nature not to resent being in another's power" (ch. 7, pp. 173–4).
- **Education and totalitarianism.** Plato's idea of education as a training of character is not tied to totalitarianism. Annas notes that the modern educational system closest to his ideal is America's (ch. 4).

### 14. Was Plato a Feminist?

The first wave is often praised as feminism "ahead of its time", the first such argument before Mill's *Subjection of Women*. **Annas** says no. Plato's interest "is neither in women's rights nor in their preferences as they see them, but rather with production of the common good", and in not wasting talent (ch. 7, pp. 181–4).
- The reform covers only guardian women. Producer women's lives are left as they were.
- Plato elsewhere sneers at equality of the sexes in actual societies (536b). He does not think subjecting women is wrong in itself, only that under ideal conditions it wastes resources.
- He insists that men are better than women at everything, even weaving and cooking. Annas calls this "an insulting way to claim that there are no specifically female competences".

The proposal was radical for Athens, where women were not legal persons and respectable women rarely left the house. But it is a proposal about the efficient use of guardians, not about women's freedom.

### 15. Was Callipolis Meant to Be Possible?

**Morrison** maps the main answers (*Companion*, ch. 9):

| Reading | Who | What Callipolis is |
|---|---|---|
| A real programme | Barker, Popper | A blueprint Plato hoped to see applied |
| A possible but very difficult ideal | Aristotle (who thought it wrong), Burnyeat, Morrison | A "utopia that is not a mere utopia": the best city, hard but not impossible |
| Ironic | Strauss, Bloom | A city Plato knew to be impossible, written to show the limits of politics |
| A metaphor for the soul | Annas (1999) | Not a political proposal at all, but a picture of justice in the individual |

Morrison sides with the second reading. Socrates explicitly says the city is not "wishful thinking" (540d), and he wants to show its approximate possibility, because otherwise it would not be an ideal for *human beings*. Morrison compares it to calling a Rothko painting the ideal of a beautiful woman. Socrates assumes human nature is very flexible in some ways, enough that a whole class could live without families. In other ways it is not: he never thinks most people could become philosophers. As Morrison puts it, "nature is just too fond of dolts".

The **Straussian** argument centres on the return to the cave. If contemplation is the best life, going back down to rule is bad for the philosopher. A wise man will not choose what is worse for himself, so the city cannot exist. Morrison does not think Plato is being ironic, but he does think Plato "cheats". He slides from the philosopher of Book V, who only *loves* wisdom, to the philosopher of Book VII, who has it. Readers do not even agree on whether a given line is ironic. Christopher Rowe thinks Plato could not have been deaf to the irony of founding a "happy" city by expelling everyone over ten; Morrison sees no irony there at all. "Evidently, on this point either Rowe or I has a tin ear. But there is no proving which of us it is."

Morrison also finds the appeal to the "pattern in heaven" (592b) "not very successful" as a fallback. It is unclear what it would mean to "engage in the politics" of a city that exists nowhere. Most surprisingly, he argues that the simple "city of pigs" of Book II, which Socrates called "true" and "healthy", may be Plato's real best city. It would be possible if all its citizens were like Socrates, moderate without needing to be ruled. In that case Callipolis would be only second best.

**Annas** gives the ethical answer: "an ideal of justice is none the worse for being non-realizable in practice if its purpose is to inspire the individual person to be as just as he or she can be" (ch. 7, p. 185). The pattern in heaven says exactly this: what matters is founding the city "within himself".

### 16. Assessment

The ideal city makes most sense as an answer to one problem: how to give power to people who will not abuse it. Plato's answers are extreme because the problem is extreme.
- **Remove what corrupts.** Take away property, family and money, the three things that make rulers into "wolves".
- **Choose the right people.** Choose rulers who do not want to rule, because they have something better to do.
- **Bind everyone together.** Bind the whole city with a story that makes everyone a brother.

The price is high. It includes deception by rulers, eugenic breeding through rigged lotteries, the disposal of "deformed" infants, the absorption of the individual into the whole, and the permanent subordination of the many to the few.

The defenders and critics agree on more than they seem to. Popper and Morrison both think Plato meant the city seriously. Annas and the Straussians both think the soul matters more than the city. Schofield shows that the noble lie cannot be explained away, and that some version of it, a founding myth that tells citizens who they are, may be impossible for any political society to do without. Whatever Plato intended, the *Republic* forces anyone who thinks about politics to say where they disagree with him and why.

### Conclusion

Socrates founds his city on a noble lie: everyone is born from the same earth, and the god has put gold, silver or bronze into each soul. He houses its guardians without property or gold so they will stay dogs and not become wolves, and he answers Adeimantus by aiming at the happiness of the whole city. In Book V he risks three waves:
- women guardians, justified because nature scatters talents across both sexes;
- common wives and children, justified by the unity of a city that feels pain like a single body;
- philosopher-kings, justified because only those who love the truth can steer the ship.

In Book VII the philosophers must be compelled to go back down into the cave, and the city could be founded quickly by sending everyone over ten to the countryside. By Book IX the city may exist only as "a pattern laid up in heaven" for anyone who wants to found it in himself. The debates over Popper's totalitarian Plato, Strauss's ironic Plato, the feminist Plato and the utopian Plato all start from these pages.
