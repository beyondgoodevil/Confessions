Buddhism was the first great foreign idea to reach China, and by the end of the Tang it had changed Chinese religion, art, philosophy and economic life. It came along the **Silk Road** in the first century CE, was first worshipped as a kind of Daoist god, and was translated into Chinese with Daoist words. During the centuries of division it won over the **literati**, the **barbarian kings** of the north and the **southern emperors**, and its **monasteries** became some of the richest institutions in the empire. It was also attacked four times by the state, in the "**disasters of the Law**" of 446, 574, 845 and 955, and each time it came back. This note follows the story from the Han to the Song, using the Cambridge Histories of China (vols. 1, 2, 3 and 5) and Dieter Kuhn's *Age of Confucian Rule*.

### 1. Arrival on the Silk Road

Demiéville begins with a warning that shapes the whole story: "Chinese Buddhism was not so much Indian Buddhism in China as a new Buddhism peculiar to China" (*CHC* 1, p. 821). Buddhism travelled with merchants. Han armies had pushed into Central Asia in the first century BCE, and the oasis kingdoms between China and India, which Demiéville calls "Serindia", became the meeting point of the two civilizations.

The first reliable mention of the Buddha is almost an accident. An edict of 65 CE pardoned **Liu Ying**, king of Chu and brother of Emperor Ming, who was suspected of plotting. In his favour the emperor noted that he "recited the subtle words of Huang-Lao and held in honor the humane cult of the Buddha", kept a period of "purification and fasting", and gave "plentiful meals" to *upāsaka* and *śramaṇa*, lay believers and monks (pp. 821–22). So by the middle of the first century there was a Buddhist community at Pengcheng, a trading city in the east, and the Buddha was worshipped next to the Yellow Emperor and Laozi.

A century later a local official, **Ze Rong**, built near Pengcheng a temple with a spire of nine discs "in the fashion of the Indian stupa" and a gilt bronze Buddha dressed in brocade. It could hold "three thousand or more people". He exempted his followers from labour service and held feasts on the Buddha's birthday for tens of thousands (pp. 822–23). Demiéville thinks his piety was partly a way of recruiting followers in the chaos of the 190s.

The famous story that Emperor Ming dreamed of a golden flying god and sent for monks, who arrived on a **white horse** with the scriptures, is a later legend (p. 823). Kieschnick points out what it tells us: "This is how Chinese writers thought Buddhism should have come to China", by imperial order from the top down. In fact it "slipped into court life gradually, haphazardly, with little fanfare" (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

The first translators were not Indian. Demiéville counts two Parthians, two Sogdians, other Iranians, three Indo-Scythians (Yuezhi), and "only three Indians", who had themselves come through Serindia (*CHC* 1, p. 824). The earliest named translator, **An Shigao**, "Shigao the Parthian", reached Luoyang in 148.

### 2. The Buddha as a Daoist God

In 166 Emperor Huan sacrificed to Laozi and the Buddha together in the palace at Luoyang, with gold and silver vessels, canopies normally reserved for emperors, and the ritual music used for sacrifices to Heaven. An astrologer named **Xiang Kai** described the new cult in a memorial:

> I have heard tell that sacrifices to Huang-Lao and the Buddha have been instituted in the palace. Now their Way is the Way of purity and the void; it extols quiescence (*wu-wei*), love of life and hatred of murder, the lessening of desires and the suppression of excess. (*CHC* 1, p. 826)

Xiang Kai also repeated the belief that the Buddha was really Laozi, who had gone west to convert the barbarians. Demiéville's verdict is that at this stage the Buddha played "the part of an acolyte-de-luxe of Lao-tzu" (p. 826). Kieschnick adds that Buddhism at the Han court was "conspicuously uncontroversial": it was known mainly for its "gentle offerings" of fruit, incense and flowers instead of animal sacrifice (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

The first translations made the Daoist reading almost unavoidable, because Daoist words were used for Buddhist ideas (*CHC* 1, p. 825):

| Buddhist term | Chinese word used | What the Chinese word normally meant |
|---|---|---|
| *bodhi* (enlightenment), *yoga* | *dao* | The Way |
| *nirvāṇa* | *wuwei* | Non-action, quiescence |
| *tathatā* (suchness) | *benwu* | Original non-being |
| *arhat* (saint) | *zhenren* | Daoist "perfected man" or immortal |

Demiéville calls the result "a clumsy and obscure jargon". Buddhist breathing and meditation looked like Daoist techniques for long life, and the Buddhist "emptiness" looked like Laozi's "non-being". This misunderstanding was also the bridge by which Buddhism crossed into Chinese culture.

### 3. Why Did It Succeed?

Kieschnick admits that the question "is probably too complex to ever answer convincingly". He offers several possibilities:
- The fall of the Han caused "a loss of faith in early ideologies and a new receptiveness to foreign ideas".
- Buddhism was strongest where Chinese religion was weakest. It offered a detailed account of life after death, a professional clergy, and elaborate forms of devotion.
- Or the opposite: it succeeded *despite* the chaos, which should have made monasteries hard to support.
- Or it was simply the result of sustained contact with foreigners devoted to "a fundamentally evangelistic religion", made possible by the Han conquests in Central Asia (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

Dien's introduction to the same volume puts the emotional case: "The violent chaos of the period made people exceedingly aware of the fragility and absurdity of life", and this need for new answers opened the way for Daoism and Buddhism. Buddhism went "from being an alien faith practiced only by foreigners in urban settings to a universal religion that was accepted by every social class" (*CHC* 2, introduction).

### 4. Monks Among the Literati: Emptiness and Dark Learning

Buddhism reached the educated class through the **Dark Learning** (*xuanxue*) of the third and fourth centuries, the fashionable philosophy built on Laozi, Zhuangzi and the *Changes*. After Luoyang fell to the Xiongnu in 311, the émigré aristocrats in the south spent their time in what Demiéville calls "tertulias on the mysteries" (*xuantan*), and cultured monks joined them (*CHC* 1, pp. 838–39). The Buddhist question of emptiness was read as the old Daoist question of being (*you*) and non-being (*wu*). Scholars counted "six schools" and "seven theses".

The most brilliant of these monks was **Zhi Dun** (314–366). He identified matter with the void, and he used Buddhism to reread Zhuangzi. Against the commentators who had turned Zhuangzi's freedom into contentment with one's lot, he protested:

> Would you have us believe that all will be for the best in the best of all possible worlds so long as the phoenix is a phoenix and the cicada a cicada ...? A fig for that social order bred by Confucianism! Let us escape into the infinite, like the phoenix in its prodigious flight, like the Buddhist who frees himself from the world! (*CHC* 1, p. 840)

Demiéville's comment: "a Buddhist monk was needed to retie the thread of the great Taoist tradition of antiquity". The influence ran both ways, and he compares it to the syncretism between late paganism and Hellenized Christianity in the Mediterranean in the same centuries (p. 840; see [[Zhuangzi: Perspectives, the Butterfly Dream and the Useless Tree]]). Lay friends of Zhi Dun such as Sun Chuo already wrote that the Buddha, Laozi and Confucius taught the same thing, the first statement of the "three teachings" idea (p. 840).

### 5. Dao'an, Huiyuan and the Question of Bowing

Two monks turned Chinese Buddhism from fashion into a disciplined religion.

**Dao'an** (312/314–385) came from a northern literati family. He rejected the method of "matching meanings" (*geyi*), translating Sanskrit terms with Daoist words, and he organized translation teams at Chang'an. He compiled the first catalogue of Buddhist scriptures in Chinese and wrote rules for his monasteries. He also gave all monks the surname *Shi*, from Śākyamuni, "symbolically placing all monks in one grand lineage" (*CHC* 1, pp. 841–42; *CHC* 2, ch. 24). Demiéville says he "turned Chinese Buddhism toward a path of indianization".

His disciple **Huiyuan** (334–416) studied the Confucian classics, then Laozi and Zhuangzi, then the Perfection of Wisdom, a path that "reminds us of Saint Augustine". From about 380 he lived on Mount Lu and refused every invitation to court. His community vowed to be reborn in the **Pure Land** of the Buddha Amitābha and practised *nianfo*, concentrating on the Buddha's image (*CHC* 1, pp. 842–43). Huiyuan was thus the founder of the two trends that "survived all the vicissitudes of Buddhism in China": meditation and the Pure Land.

He also fought the issue that defined Buddhism's relation to the Chinese state. In 404 he published *A Monk Does Not Have to Pay Homage to the King*, and the court accepted his case. Demiéville notes that the question "was often taken up again later, with varying results" (p. 844). Kieschnick dates the controversy's first eruption to 340 and calls it one of "the most visible controversies at court" (*CHC* 2, ch. 24). In India, kings found it natural to honour holy men. In China, where the emperor was sacred, it was an insult.

The north found a different answer. Faguo, the first head of the clergy under the Northern Wei, simply declared the emperor to be a Buddha:

> He loves the Way, he is the Tathāgata of our time; it is right that monks should pay him homage. ... I bow down not before the Son of Heaven, but before the Buddha himself. (*CHC* 1, p. 855)

### 6. Kumārajīva and the Great Translation

The decisive figure in translation was **Kumārajīva** (d. 413), son of an Indian Brahmin and a princess of Kucha. A Former Qin general carried him off when Kucha fell in 384. He spent about twenty years held in the Gansu corridor, learning Chinese, until the Later Qin emperor Yao Xing brought him to Chang'an in 402 (*CHC* 1, pp. 850–51). There he led a team of up to three thousand monks and laymen. His versions of the *Lotus Sutra*, the *Vimalakīrti* and the Pure Land sutra outshone all earlier ones and "became part of the Chinese literary heritage". He translated a commentary on the Perfection of Wisdom of nearly a million characters in two years. Demiéville invites the reader to picture the Kuchean master in an imperial park on the Wei River, surrounded by "the flower of the intelligentsia of Ch'ang-an", with their barbarian ruler presiding:

> let us judge whether the Chinese are a race of ethnocentrics who do not know how to manage when they think they have something to learn from foreigners. (p. 852)

At the same time Chinese monks went west to get what was missing. **Faxian** left to find a complete monastic code, travelled through Serindia and India and came back by sea through Ceylon, returning in about 413–14 after fourteen years (*CHC* 1, p. 844; *CHC* 2, ch. 24). Kieschnick calls the whole state-sponsored, team-based process "perhaps the greatest translation project in the history of the world, certainly before the modern era".

New texts set off new arguments. **Zhu Daosheng** (360–434) taught that even the *icchantika*, those thought incapable of salvation, have the Buddha nature, and that enlightenment comes **suddenly**, all at once. He was expelled for heresy, then vindicated when a fuller text of the *Nirvana Sutra* arrived (*CHC* 1, pp. 844–45). Demiéville sees in him "the seeds of the future Ch'an school" (Zen). The poet Xie Lingyun even claimed that Indians believe in gradual enlightenment and Chinese in sudden, which made Confucius a supporter of "instantaneousness" (pp. 845–46).

### 7. Barbarian Kings and a Barbarian God

The rulers of the north were often the warmest patrons. They did not share the Chinese suspicion of foreign religions. The Later Zhao ruler **Shi Hu** (r. 333–349) asked why barbarians should not welcome a barbarian god, even for their Chinese subjects (*CHC* 1, p. 847). The Central Asian monk **Fotudeng**, who reached Luoyang in 310, became his counsellor, prized "as a thaumaturge and divine". He was also Dao'an's teacher. Demiéville stresses that Buddhism was first valued for its magic and prophecy, as later in Japan and Tibet.

The Northern Wei went furthest. After the first persecution (section 11), the monk Tanyao began the cave temples at **Yungang**, where the great Buddhas reproduced the features of the Wei emperors. Demiéville calls this "a theocratic solution to the problem of whether or not monks should pay homage to the secular ruler" (p. 855). When the capital moved to Luoyang in 494, the **Longmen** caves followed. Kieschnick notes that Emperor Xuanwu ordered the Binyang caves at Longmen in honour of his parents (his mother had been murdered when he was twelve). The Buddhas of past, present and future in them were linked to his own position as heir to the throne (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

### 8. The Bodhisattva Emperor: Liang Wudi

In the south the great patron was **Xiao Yan, Emperor Wu of the Liang** (r. 502–549). Kieschnick calls him "perhaps the only ruler in Chinese history deserving the title of 'Buddhist emperor'" (*CHC* 2, ch. 24). He:
- wrote commentaries and lectured on scripture;
- took the bodhisattva precepts and lived, according to the histories, a celibate, vegetarian life, giving up even music;
- required monks to be vegetarian;
- "ransomed" himself to a monastery.

Demiéville describes his "great assemblies from which no one is excluded": more than fifty thousand people in 529 and more than three hundred thousand in 533. At one of them the emperor put on a monk's robe to preach, and his courtiers paid a thousand million cash to buy him back (*CHC* 1, p. 848).

What made him unique, Kieschnick argues, was not his piety but that he let it override other religion. He persecuted Daoism, and "uniquely in Chinese history" he banned **animal sacrifice** from imperial ritual (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

### 9. Monasteries, Land and Money

Before Buddhism, Kieschnick writes, "there was nothing in China resembling the monastic institution". Monasteries had one great economic advantage over families: they did not die out or divide their land among quarrelling sons, so large ones grew steadily for centuries (*CHC* 2, ch. 24). They rented out farmland, ran mills and, from the fifth century, pawnshops. Rent from farmland stayed the main support of Chinese monasticism until the land reforms of the twentieth century.

The Northern Wei even used them as state granaries. The *Weishu* records the monk Tanyao's proposal, around 469: households that delivered sixty *hu* of grain a year would become "sangha-households" and their grain "sangha-grain", "to be used in lean years to relieve the famine-stricken people". Criminals and public slaves became "Buddha-households" to work monastery fields (*CHC* 2, ch. 24; *CHC* 1, p. 855).

The numbers grew fast, especially in the north (*CHC* 1, pp. 856–57):

| Date | Region | Monasteries | Monks and nuns |
|---|---|---|---|
| 477 | Northern Wei | 6,478 | 67,258 |
| End of Northern Wei (534) | Northern Wei | about 30,000 | about 2 million |
| 5th–6th c. | Southern dynasties | 1,768–2,846 | 24,000–82,700 |

By 518 more than a third of the area of Luoyang was occupied by Buddhist buildings. Sixteen years later the Northern Wei fell. With growth came "pseudo-monks" escaping taxes and military service. Between 402 and 517 there were nine peasant rebellions led by Buddhists, often by a monk claiming to be the future Buddha **Maitreya** and promising, like the Yellow Turbans, an age of Great Peace (p. 856).

### 10. Faith of the Many: Merit, Images and Miracles

Most Chinese Buddhists left no philosophy, but they left inscriptions, over 1,600 of them from this period (*CHC* 2, ch. 24). One of the earliest is on a small stone stupa of 377 now in Gansu:

> Cheng Duan'er, recognizing that his own blessings are few, that he was born in the final age [of the dharma], and that he has not read the Buddhist books, has expended his wealth for the sake of his parents and his entire family in order to erect this stone stupa decorated with images. (*CHC* 2, ch. 24)

The inscriptions show the core of popular Buddhism:
- **Merit** made by building images or copying sutras, and **transferred** to dead parents to win them a good rebirth. Donors often gave the merit to "seven generations of ancestors", the same number emperors honoured in the imperial temple. Here Buddhism fitted itself into Chinese ancestor worship.
- **Saviour figures**: Maitreya, the Buddha to come, and Amitābha's Pure Land, often reached through the bodhisattva Guanshiyin (Guanyin).
- **Miracles**: images that wept, sweated and bled; scriptures that survived fire; men saved from prison by calling on Guanshiyin. The miracle tales explained these by the native idea of **resonance** (*ganying*), "like one string on an instrument naturally vibrating when a corresponding string is plucked". This is the same resonance Dong Zhongshu had used for omens (see [[The Mandate of Heaven and the Five Phases: Legitimacy from Qin to Song]]).
- **Lay associations** of dozens of villagers under a monk, which paid for images, bridges and wells.

Chinese **apocryphal** sutras added filial piety, ancestor worship and divination to the Buddha's supposed teachings. A nun named Sengfa, who died at sixteen, recited a whole series of them in trance (*CHC* 1, pp. 849–50). Demiéville notes that even Zhu Xi in the twelfth century criticized Buddhism mostly from apocryphal texts. The sixth-century **Sect of the Three Stages** taught that the age of the "last Law" had begun, built up "inexhaustible treasuries" for Maitreya's coming, and was banned by both the Sui and the Tang (pp. 858–59).

### 11. Why the State Worried

Kieschnick describes the state's attitude as "cautious engagement bordering often on indifference". Emperors and officials could worship as they liked, and monks prayed for rain or the emperor's birthday. But the existence of "a large, coherent group of dedicated religious professionals" was a new problem. From the point of view of "exasperated court ministers":
- Monks paid no tax and did no labour or military service.
- They "produced nothing of value: no crops, no goods, no children".
- Many wandered from place to place, which a state built on registration distrusted.
- Their loyalty was suspect, as the bowing controversy showed.
- They were always associated with "the foreign, the strange, and the potentially disruptive" (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

Demiéville adds the family argument. Leaving home and having no heirs went against ancestor worship, the basis of Chinese society. The conflict "constantly sapped the strength of Buddhism in China, and would finally bring it to ruin" (*CHC* 1, pp. 853–54). Kieschnick's key point is that "the fluctuation between support, suspicion, and persecution" stayed "remarkably consistent through close to 2,000 years". The four great persecutions were exceptions to a normal policy of regulating and patronizing at the same time.

### 12. The First Disaster: Northern Wei Taiwu, 446

The first large-scale persecution came under **Emperor Taiwu** of the Northern Wei (r. 424–452). His chief Chinese adviser **Cui Hao** wanted to remake the Tuoba state on Confucian lines, and had allied with the Daoist reformer **Kou Qianzhi**, whom the emperor honoured with the title "Perfect Ruler of Great Peace" (*CHC* 2, ch. 8). In 446, while putting down a rebellion near Chang'an, Taiwu found weapons in a monastery. He ordered the monks of Chang'an killed and then extended the order to the whole empire: monks executed or defrocked, images destroyed, scriptures burnt (*CHC* 1, pp. 854–55; *CHC* 2, ch. 8, 24). The sources differ slightly on the dates: Kieschnick cites edicts of 444 and 446, and Demiéville places the arms discovery in 445.

Buddhist historians blamed Daoist jealousy and monastic corruption. Kieschnick thinks these explanations "miss the mark". The real motives were mixed: precious-metal images for the treasury, Taiwu's need for conscripts, and fear of links between monks and his enemies. Ironically Kou Qianzhi himself argued against the most violent proposals (*CHC* 2, ch. 24). The decree was not rigorously enforced. Within a few years Taiwu's grandson Wencheng had ended it, and Tanyao began Yungang (*CHC* 1, p. 855). Cui Hao had already been executed in 450.

### 13. The Second Disaster: Northern Zhou Wudi, 574–577

**Emperor Wu of the Northern Zhou** (r. 561–578), who "wished to be more Chinese than the Chinese", held a debate to rank the three teachings. The chief opponents of Buddhism were a defrocked monk, Wei Yuansong, and a Daoist, Zhang Bin. In 573 he put Confucianism first. In 574 Buddhism, and this time Daoism too, was proscribed: clergy returned to lay life, temples, images and books destroyed, property confiscated. When the Zhou conquered the Northern Qi in 577 the ban was extended over all of north China, and **two or three million** monks and nuns were laicized (*CHC* 1, p. 857). The ban lasted only until Wu's death in 578.

### 14. Sui and Tang: Patronage and Control

The man who reunified China had grown up inside Buddhism. **Yang Jian**, Sui Wendi, was born in a monastery and brought up by a nun. He had watched the Zhou suppression and sheltered his childhood mentor during it (*CHC* 1, p. 857; *CHC* 3, p. 75). Wright identifies three roles Buddhism played in his ideology (*CHC* 3, pp. 75–77):
1. **Supplementary legitimation.** Confucian ritual made him Son of Heaven, but Buddhism made him a *cakravartin*, a wheel-turning universal king. A monk's catalogue of 594 calls him "a great Dānapati" (donor) and "indeed a Cakravartin king". Wendi said in 585: "Buddha takes the true dharma and entrusts it to the princes of states. We, being honoured among men, accept Buddha's trust."
2. **A controlled clergy** under a central monastery in the capital, praying for rain, health and the imperial ancestors.
3. **A common faith** to bind north and south after the conquest of 589: "With the armed might of a Cakravartin king We spread the ideals of the ultimately benevolent one."

Like the Indian king Aśoka, he distributed relics to the prefectures in 601, 602 and 604, but built only 111 stupas against Aśoka's legendary 84,000 in one day (*CHC* 1, p. 869). On the same day in 601 that he closed most of the Confucian schools, complaining that their students "simply have their names enrolled and vainly waste their time", he sent out the relics (*CHC* 3, p. 75). The dynasty also banned the Three Stages sect in 600, and in 607, under Yangdi, ordered monks to bow before the emperor and officials (*CHC* 1, p. 869). Kieschnick's conclusion about the Six Dynasties fits Wendi exactly: "it was Buddhism that had helped hold the disparate peoples, families, and regions together in the first place" (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

The Tang founders were cooler.
- **Gaozu.** The court astrologer **Fu Yi** attacked Buddhism in 621 and 624 as foreign and unwholesome. In 626 Gaozu ordered the capital's Buddhist temples cut from over 120 to three and the Daoist ones from about ten to one. The order was reversed three months later, on the day his son Li Shimin seized power (*CHC* 3, p. 180).
- **Taizong** (Li Shimin) was "fundamentally hostile" in private but careful in public (pp. 217–19). He:
  - ordered illegally ordained monks executed (629);
  - forbade monks to receive homage from their own parents (631, withdrawn 633);
  - gave Daoists precedence (637), since the imperial Li family claimed Laozi as ancestor;
  - put the clergy under secular law with the "Regulations concerning the Taoist and Buddhist clergy" (637).
  He wanted the returning pilgrim **Xuanzang** (600–664) for his knowledge of India and Central Asia, and even tried to make him an official.
- **Empress Wu** used the *Great Cloud Sutra*'s prophecy of a female Maitreya to justify her own reign, and founded a Great Cloud temple in every prefecture (*CHC* 3, pp. 305–6).
- **Xuanzong** returned more than thirty thousand illegally ordained monks and nuns to lay life in 714. He also banned new monasteries and the "merit cloisters" that great families used to dodge taxes (pp. 360–61).

The Tang also saw the birth of **Chan** (Zen). Its best-known text, the *Platform Sutra*, tells of a verse contest to choose the Fifth Patriarch's successor. The learned senior monk Shenxiu wrote:

> The body is the bodhi tree; / The mind is like a bright mirror's stand. / Be always diligent in rubbing it— / Do not let it attract any dust.

Huineng, an illiterate "barbarian" from the far south working as a temple menial, answered:

> Bodhi is fundamentally without any tree; / The bright mirror is also not a stand. / Fundamentally there is not a single thing— / Where could any dust be attracted? (*Platform Sutra*, trans. McRae)

Huineng's verse is Zhu Daosheng's sudden enlightenment in its most memorable form. McRae's introduction warns that the story is legend. The two men were never at the monastery at the same time, and the historical Huineng (638–713) "is almost totally unknown". Shenxiu was in fact the most important figure of early Chan, invited to court by Empress Wu in 700, and the text was written later to unite the movement under Huineng's name. Demiéville's judgement on the greatest later Chan master, Linji (d. 866/7), is that his thought is "Chuang-tzu's thought served up with a Buddhist sauce" (*CHC* 1, p. 846).

### 15. The Third Disaster: Huichang, 842–845

The most famous attack had a famous prelude. In 818 Emperor Xianzong had a finger bone of the Buddha, displayed once every thirty years, brought into the palace. The official **Han Yu** protested in his *Memorial on the Bone of the Buddha* (819), describing the Buddha as "a man of the barbarians who did not speak the language of China and wore clothes of a different fashion" (Kuhn, p. 14). In his essay *An Inquiry on the Way* he set out the Confucian case:

> They destroy the natural principles of human relations so that the son does not regard his father as a father, the minister does not regard his ruler as a ruler, and the people do not attend to their work.... Now, they take the ways of barbarism and elevate them above the teachings of our ancient kings. Does this not almost make all of us barbarians? (Chan, p. 455)

Kuhn calls the memorial a milestone whose anti-foreignism "would flourish during the Neo-Confucian revival of the Song". But Dalby warns against treating it as the cause of what followed. Han Yu "represented an extreme opinion", and most late Tang officials found "the more elevated forms of philosophical Buddhist thought and ritual" attractive (*CHC* 3, p. 668).

The persecution began under **Emperor Wuzong** (r. 840–846) in 842 and reached its height in 845. The Japanese monk Ennin, who was in China, recorded it in his diary. The figures (Kuhn, pp. 14–15; *CHC* 3, pp. 666–67):
- More than 4,600 monasteries and over 40,000 smaller shrines and buildings destroyed. Each superior prefecture was allowed one temple; Chang'an and Luoyang two each, with thirty monks per temple.
- About 260,000 monks and nuns returned to lay life: Kuhn gives 265,000, Dalby "perhaps as many as a quarter of a million". Many were killed or injured.
- More than 150,000 monastery slaves registered as taxpayers.
- Gold, silver and bronze images melted down for coin.

Both Kuhn and Dalby stress that the motives were mainly **economic**. The state was short of copper coin. The monasteries held huge reserves of precious metal and great tax-exempt estates. Monks and nuns, having "left the family", escaped the two-tax system. Wuzong was a fanatical Daoist, and his chief minister Li Deyu probably supported the campaign. Li had suppressed **Manichaeism** in 843 after defeating its Uighur patrons, which may have served as a model (*CHC* 3, pp. 667–69). After Wuzong died, his successor allowed rebuilding in 847, but only within limits. Dalby's judgement is that Buddhism as an institution had been "maimed just as it was experiencing one of its greatest periods of growth. Never in later Chinese history was it in a position to challenge, or even to rival, the power of the state" (p. 666).

### 16. The Fourth Disaster: Later Zhou Shizong, 955

The last of the four came during the Five Dynasties. In 955 **Emperor Shizong** of the Later Zhou, needing money, dissolved most monasteries and confiscated their metal. He also banned the self-mutilation and self-burning that some devotees practised. By the time the Song came to power in 960, "only 2,694 monasteries out of 30,336 had survived this purge" (Kuhn, p. 15).

The four persecutions compared:

| | Northern Wei, 446 | Northern Zhou, 574–577 | Tang Huichang, 842–845 | Later Zhou, 955 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emperor | Taiwu | Wu | Wuzong | Shizong |
| Advisers | Cui Hao (Confucian), Kou Qianzhi (Daoist) | Wei Yuansong, Zhang Bin | Li Deyu; Daoist favourites | — |
| Stated reasons | Seditious, corrupt sangha; arms found | Ranking of the three teachings | "Buddhist pest"; foreign religion | Bad conditions, low morale |
| Main real motives | Treasury, conscripts, security | Confucian-state ideology, wealth | Copper, estates, tax losses | Cash shortage |
| Scale | Monks executed or defrocked | 2–3 million laicized; Daoism banned too | About 260,000 laicized; 4,600 monasteries | 27,642 of 30,336 monasteries closed |
| Ended | Within years, under Wencheng | At Wu's death, 578 | Relaxed 847 | Reversed by the early Song |

Three of the four emperors had *Wu*, "martial", in their names. Kieschnick's conclusion about all of them is the key point: they were "relatively short-lived and followed, with the death of the emperor who instigated them, by astonishingly rapid restorations of monks, monasteries, images, and scriptures—testimony to the extent to which Buddhism had become woven into the fabric of Chinese society" (*CHC* 2, ch. 24).

### 17. The Song: Recovery and Laicization

The early Song emperors "halted the attacks of the Later Chou" (Bol, *CHC* 5.2, p. 669).
- Taizu ordained 8,000 monks soon after 960, and in 971 ordered the whole Buddhist canon printed. It was carved on 130,000 woodblocks and finished in 983 (Kuhn, p. 108).
- A translation bureau was set up and students were sent west. By the end of Zhenzong's reign in 1022 more than 397,000 monks were registered (Bol, p. 669).
- Taizong added to Huineng's posthumous title and rebuilt his stupa as the "Stupa of Great Peace and Benefit of the Country" (*Platform Sutra*, trans. McRae).
- In 1221 there were 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns, against 260,000 in 845 (Kuhn, p. 109).

The only Song emperor to turn on Buddhism was the Daoist enthusiast **Huizong**. In 1119 he relabelled monks as lower-ranking Daoist clergy, forcing them to take Daoist titles and dress, "the sternest and oddest anti-Buddhist measure since the Hui-ch'ang persecutions" (Hymes, *CHC* 5.2, p. 607). It did not last.

Hymes argues that the old textbook story of Song Buddhism's "decline" is "a horse too near dead to beat". The real change was **laicization**, "a relative shift in the balance of power and initiative from practitioners toward the laity" (*CHC* 5.2, pp. 595–96).
- **Ordination for sale.** Until the 1070s a monk had to pass a scriptural test at the capital. Then the government, needing money for Wang Anshi's New Policies, began selling ordination certificates (*CHC* 5.2, p. 530). Kuhn dates the first sales to 1067, at 130 strings each, and estimates up to 120,000 a year in the 1160s (p. 109). The certificates circulated "as a particular form of paper money" and paid for warships (Schottenhammer, *CHC* 5.2, p. 457). Rich laymen bought them for poor candidates as an act of charity, or to staff family shrines. Hymes compares this to English gentry controlling church livings after Henry VIII dissolved the monasteries (pp. 599–600).
- **Competition for lay followers.** The great twelfth-century Chan dispute between "kung-an introspection" (Linji) and "silent illumination" (Caodong) grew out of competition for literati patrons once court support declined (p. 597).
- **Lay piety.** Lay societies such as the White Cloud, the White Lotus and the "People of the Way" recited Amitābha's name and kept reduced monastic vows while marrying and working (pp. 601–2).
- **Chinese gods.** Avalokiteśvara became the female **Guanyin**, and Maitreya became the pot-bellied **Laughing Buddha** (Kuhn, ch. 5).

The Neo-Confucians attacked Buddhism and borrowed from it at once. Shi Jie declared that "three things decidedly do not exist between Heaven and Earth: there are no divine immortals, no method to make gold, and no Buddha". Zhang Zai answered Buddhist emptiness with his theory of *qi*, and Zhu Xi wanted Buddhism abolished while knowing it never would be. Kuhn notes that some historians think "without Buddhism in China there would have been no Neo-Confucianism", and that the Confucians absorbed "respect for all life, compassion, philanthropy, meditation" (ch. 5).

To the north the Liao fed 360,000 monks and nuns in 1078, about five per cent of the population, and printed their own canon (Kuhn, p. 115).

### 18. Assessment

The story has a clear shape. Buddhism entered China as a misunderstood foreign cult, attached itself to Daoism, and then, through Dao'an, Kumārajīva and the pilgrims, learned to be itself. It won the north through its magic and its usefulness to non-Chinese kings, the south through philosophy and aristocratic patronage, and everyone through merit, miracles and care for the dead. It became rich enough to threaten the state's finances, and that, more than doctrine, brought on the persecutions.

Three judgements in the sources seem right to me.
- **Kieschnick on continuity.** Policy hardly changed in two thousand years: regulate the clergy, patronize the faith, persecute only rarely. The persecutions were exceptions.
- **Dalby on the Huichang persecution.** It was a turning point not because Buddhism disappeared, but because the monasteries never again rivalled the state.
- **Hymes on the Song.** What looks like decline was really a move from monks and court to laymen and the market.

A note of caution. Most of our sources were written by Confucian officials, who disliked Buddhism and wrote little about popular religion, or by monks, who put eminent monks at the centre of everything. The inscriptions and Dunhuang manuscripts are the main way past both biases.

### Conclusion

Between the edict of 65 and the Song, Buddhism went from a foreign cult worshipped beside Laozi to a religion "woven into the fabric of Chinese society". It changed in the process: translated through Daoist words, fitted to ancestor worship, made sudden and practical in Chan, devotional in the Pure Land, and finally, in the Song, a religion of lay believers. The state never stopped worrying about monks who produced "no crops, no goods, no children" and would not bow. Four times an emperor tried to break the monasteries, in 446, 574, 845 and 955, and four times Buddhism returned within a few years. Huichang broke the monasteries' wealth and power for good. It did not touch the faith, which by the Song had passed from the monasteries into Chinese households.
