Wagner's ***Ring of the Nibelung*** is four music dramas, about **sixteen hours** over four nights, telling one story: a gold ring forged by renouncing love brings ruin on everyone who wants it, gods included. It matters for two reasons: its **method**, the leitmotif and the continuous "music drama" that replaced opera's arias, and its **meaning**, which critics from Kerman to Adorno still argue about. Here's how it works:

### 1. Making the Ring

- **Sources.** Mostly the *Nibelungenlied* (early thirteenth century) and its older Norse counterpart, the **Edda**. Kellow points out that Wagner took over their sense of the "implacable presence of death": in Norse myth, doom waits for gods and men alike.
- **Written backwards.** Wagner meant at first to write one opera, *Siegfried's Death*. To explain it he added *Young Siegfried*, then *The Valkyrie*, then *The Rhine Gold*, so the librettos were written in **reverse order**. The music was then composed from the beginning (Kellow).
- **Twenty-six years, 1848–74.** In between came the failed Dresden uprising of 1849 (Wagner wrote that he would destroy the order dividing mankind into rich and poor, and fled with a warrant out for his arrest), exile in Zurich, and *Tristan und Isolde*, which interrupted work on *Siegfried* for years.
- **Ludwig II.** The young King of Bavaria paid Wagner's debts in 1864 but owned the Ring, and staged *Rheingold* and *Walküre* in Munich in 1869–70 against Wagner's wishes. Wagner's answer was his own theatre at **Bayreuth**, which opened on 13 August 1876 with *Das Rheingold*.
- **Staging troubles.** In 1876 the Rhinemaidens "swam" on poles fixed to a moving trolley, until seasick singers were replaced by dancers with the singing done offstage. The dragon was built in London and shipped in sections, but its **neck was sent to Beirut** by mistake (Kellow).

### 2. The Story, Night by Night

1. ***Das Rheingold* (The Rhine Gold).** The dwarf **Alberich** steals the gold guarded by the Rhinemaidens. Only someone who **renounces love** can forge it into a ring of world power, and he does. **Wotan**, chief of the gods, whose authority rests on the treaties carved on his spear, has promised the goddess Freia to the giants as payment for building **Valhalla**. He and the fire-god **Loge** trick Alberich out of the ring, and Alberich **curses** it: death to whoever owns it. Wotan pays the giants with it, and at once the giant Fafner kills his brother Fasolt for it. The gods walk over the rainbow bridge into Valhalla.
2. ***Die Walküre* (The Valkyrie).** Wotan fathers the Wälsung twins, **Siegmund and Sieglinde**, hoping for a free hero to win the ring back without breaking his treaties. The separated twins fall in love. Wotan's wife Fricka, guardian of marriage, forces him to let Siegmund die. His daughter, the Valkyrie **Brünnhilde**, disobeys him out of compassion and saves the pregnant Sieglinde. Wotan punishes her by putting her to sleep on a rock ringed with **magic fire**, which only a fearless hero can cross.
3. ***Siegfried*.** Sieglinde's son **Siegfried** is raised by Alberich's brother **Mime**, who wants to use him to get the ring. Siegfried reforges his father's broken sword, **Nothung**, kills Fafner (now a dragon), kills Mime, **shatters Wotan's spear**, walks through the fire and wakes Brünnhilde.
4. ***Götterdämmerung* (Twilight of the Gods).** Alberich's son **Hagen** gives Siegfried a potion that makes him forget Brünnhilde. Siegfried betrays her, and Hagen kills him. Brünnhilde builds Siegfried's funeral pyre and rides into the flames. The Rhine floods, the Rhinemaidens take back the ring, and **Valhalla burns**.

Kellow notes what Wagner changed. In the sources the gold is in a cave, not the Rhine; Siegmund and Sieglinde are brought together by Wotan's will rather than their own passion; and Fricka, in Wagner, became less a goddess defending the rules than a "shrewish, vindictive hausfrau."

### 3. Music Drama: No More Arias

> [!definition] Music drama
> Wagner's name for what replaced opera: **continuous music** in the service of **continuous drama**, with no arias, recitatives or set numbers to stop the action. Music, poetry, drama, staging and acting are united in a ***Gesamtkunstwerk***, a "total work of art," all written by Wagner himself.

- **Why.** Wagner thought Italian and French opera had become "a mere concert in costume" (as Kerman puts it). Why should the action keep stopping for "pretty but undramatic singing"? His model was Greek tragedy (Kerman, Greenberg).
- **The orchestra takes over.** Copland heard Massenet's *Manon* one night and *Die Walküre* the next: with Massenet the orchestra was a pit band, but with Wagner "the entire Philharmonic Symphony had moved into the Metropolitan." Wagner was "by nature a symphonist." Greenberg compares the orchestra's role to the **chorus in Greek drama**, narrating and interpreting.
- **Schopenhauer.** Wagner read Schopenhauer, for whom all experience is either **Will** (drives and emotions) or **Representation** (ideas, reason, appearance; Kerman calls it "Appearance"), and music is our only direct contact with the Will. So in Wagner the singers live in a world of "half-truths and delusions," while the orchestra, through the leitmotifs, "tells the truth" (Greenberg).
- **The singers** mostly don't sing the leitmotifs. They declaim the words in a free line that sometimes blossoms towards melody but never becomes an aria (Kerman).

Copland still answers his own question "Did Wagner achieve reality in the opera house?" with a flat **no**: Wagner swapped one set of conventions for another.

### 4. The Leitmotif

> [!definition] Leitmotif (leitmotiv)
> A "guiding" or "leading" motive: a short musical idea associated with a **person, object, idea or feeling** in the drama, developed and transformed by the orchestra as the story goes on.

Kerman's two sides of it:

1. **Mechanical.** At its worst the orchestra "obligingly sounds the Sword motive every time the hero reaches for his weapon."
2. **Psychological.** At its best a leitmotif tells you what a character is thinking or feeling **while they say something else, or nothing at all**. Leitmotifs hardly ever come back exactly the same, and their transformations track how people and ideas change. In *Die Walküre*, Act I, Siegmund's motive is a transformed version of the storm music, "the storm is in Siegmund's soul," and when Sieglinde bends over him the violins play her motive while the cellos continue his: the orchestra joins them before they have spoken.

### 5. What Is the Ring About?

**Kerman: a modern allegory of greed.** Wagner uses the mythology "to tell a simple modern tale": the world's moral decline through **greed for money and hunger for power**. Gods, dwarfs and warriors stand for groups in nineteenth-century society, each destroying itself for gold. Even the renunciation of love is an allegory, an indictment of a bourgeois world that prized work and discipline over feeling. Love here means **compassion** in the widest sense, the one thing that could save the characters from the ring (on my reading, that is why Brünnhilde's compassionate disobedience is the moral turning point).

**Kellow and Edith Hamilton: heroism and lost causes.** From the Norse side, the hero proves what he is only by **resisting evil while facing certain defeat**. Siegfried and Brünnhilde fit this pattern.

**Directors.** Since Wieland Wagner's bare, symbolic stagings at Bayreuth in the 1950s, the Ring is often staged as **political or social allegory**: Kellow mentions a 1989 Berlin production set in a subway tunnel with Valkyries in biker jackets.

### 6. Adorno: Myth Argued For and Accused

Adorno's 1963 lecture "Wagner's Relevance for Today" is the most searching critique, and it isn't simply hostile.

1. **Modern, not just loud.** Wagner was the first "uncompromising musical nominalist": each work and each musical figure makes its own form instead of filling a scheme. His verdict that number opera was "childish" still stands; when later composers write arias (Stravinsky's *Rake*) they can only do it ironically. And the Ring's great architecture is in ***Siegfried***, not in the *Walküre* hits ("Wotan's farewell," the magic fire) that audiences love.
2. **Violence breaks through.** Against Hegel's dictum that "mere desire, the wildness and brutality of the will" has no place in the theatre, Wagner's music "shudders with the unrelieved violence that lives on today in the world order." The Ring's truth is that in it **violence is still the law it was in prehistory**: "prehistory persists as modernity itself."
3. **For myth and against it.** The music argues for myth; Wagner "glorifies Siegfried, the man of violence." But by showing violence unveiled, the work becomes "an indictment of myth, willingly or not." Adorno quotes Richard Strauss's remark that Wagner tried to **deliver us from myth by means of the leitmotif**: the leitmotif names and identifies, and so sets a limit to the blind ambiguity of myth.
4. **Epic, not drama.** The Ring is told rather than acted, full of long narratives that repeat what we already know (Wotan's monologue in *Walküre* Act II, the riddle scene in *Siegfried* Act I). Adorno even calls it **epic theatre**, adding that Brecht "would be at my throat." Because it is steeped in Schopenhauer, everything is **predetermined**: if Hegel's history is progress in freedom, the Ring is "a phenomenology of the spirit as fate."
5. **Why the ending disappoints.** Adorno thinks the last act of *Götterdämmerung* is **weak**: Brünnhilde's final song is "infinitely weaker" than Isolde's. Not because Wagner's skill failed, but because a **ring** is a closed circle that rules out anything new; redemption from myth can only come as a reprise. "Myth is catastrophe in permanence."
6. **The anti-Semitism.** Productions should at least use their staging to free the dramas "from the stigma of the disgraceful Jewish caricatures Mime and Beckmesser." Kerman also notes Wagner's "vicious" essay attacking Mendelssohn and other Jews in music, which the Nazis later took up.

- **Significance:** for Adorno, "what is magnificent in his work cannot be cleanly divided from what is questionable." That is why he thinks Wagner remains a serious matter.

### 7. The False Götterdämmerung

On 12 April 1945, in the ruins of Berlin, the Berlin Philharmonic played a programme that ended with the **Immolation Scene** from *Götterdämmerung*. Walther Funk thought Hitler had modelled his scorched-earth policy on Wagner's finale: everything had to go down with him, "as a sort of false Götterdämmerung." Ross adds the necessary caution: there is **no evidence** that Hitler was thinking about Wagner or listening to music in his last days (Ross, ch. 9). (Nietzsche's early hope that Wagner would bring a rebirth of German myth, and his later break with him, are in [[Apollo and Dionysus: The Birth of Tragedy]].)

### Conclusion

The Ring tells a myth about a world destroyed by the will to power, and it tells it in a new way: no set numbers, an orchestra that speaks the truth the characters can't, and leitmotifs that turn the whole sixteen hours into one web of transforming themes. Kerman reads it as an allegory of greed, the Norse sources as the heroism of lost causes, and Adorno as a work that argues for myth while exposing it, whose closed circle explains both its power and its disappointing ending. The method changed music for good; the meaning, including Wagner's anti-Semitism and the Nazis' use of him, is still being argued over in every new production.
