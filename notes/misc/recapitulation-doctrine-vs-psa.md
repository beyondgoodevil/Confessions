**Penal Substitution Atonement (PSA):** PSA holds that Christ's death was a legal transaction where He took on humanity’s punishment to satisfy God’s justice and wrath. This view risks portraying the Father as wrathful, the Son as the appeaser, and the Spirit as uninvolved—splitting the Trinity into separate roles, which contradicts the eternal unity and love shared by the three Persons. God’s justice is presented as a force above His own will, requiring satisfaction, which diminishes His freedom and love.

**Recapitulation Doctrine:** Recapitulation teaches that Christ, as the New Adam, unites humanity to God by assuming human nature, living a sinless life, and offering it back to the Father. This restores human nature, reverses Adam's fall, and defeats death. It emphasizes the Trinity’s unity—Christ's actions are not isolated but reflect the will and love of the Father and the Spirit. The Incarnation and Cross are acts of divine love, not penal wrath, bringing healing and communion rather than appeasement.

**Key Differences:**

- **Trinitarian Unity:** PSA risks dividing the Trinity; Recapitulation affirms the perfect unity of Father, Son, and Spirit.
- **Motivation:** PSA emphasizes justice and wrath; Recapitulation highlights divine love and the healing of human nature.
- **Sacrifice:** In PSA, the Cross is about penal satisfaction; in Recapitulation, it is an offering of restored human nature to God for reconciliation and deification.

## Irenaeus on Recapitulation

- *Against Heresies* III.18.1: the Son "**recapitulated in Himself** the long history of mankind, giving us salvation in a summary way, so that what we lost in Adam, namely to be in the image and likeness of God, we might recover in Christ Jesus".
- From Eph 1:10, *anakephalaiōsasthai*: to "gather together in one" (KJV), to sum up under one head.
- Christ goes through **every age** of human life, sanctifying each: infancy, childhood, youth, maturity (*AH* II.22.4).
- **Reversals** (*AH* V.19, III.22): Adam disobeyed at a tree, Christ obeyed on a tree; a virgin (Eve) listened to the serpent, a virgin (Mary) to the angel; "the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary". Adam from virgin earth, Christ from a virgin.
- **Victory**: Christ binds the strong man, defeating the devil by obedience as Adam was defeated by disobedience.

## Athanasius, On the Incarnation

**The "divine dilemma"** (ch. 6–7):
- Man, made from nothing, was turning back toward **non-being** through sin: corruption and death.
- It would be unworthy of God to let His work perish. But it would also be unworthy to go back on His word ("in the day thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die").
- **Repentance alone** wouldn't do: it stops sinning but doesn't heal **corruption** of nature.
- So the Word, incorruptible, takes a body capable of death, offers it to death **on behalf of all**, and so death's power is exhausted on Him and corruption is reversed by His **resurrection**.

Key lines:
- "He became man that we might become god" (ch. 54).
- The image: a king enters a city and lives in one house: the whole city is honoured and protected (ch. 9).
- "**Death has been destroyed**": Christians, even children, now laugh at death (ch. 27-29).

Athanasius does use language of **debt** and **substitution** ("He paid the debt in His death", ch. 20). But the debt is **death** owed to the law of corruption, not a penalty demanded by the Father's wrath. The problem is **ontological** (corruption), not merely juridical.

## Bulgakov, The Lamb of God (ch. 5, "The Work of Christ")

- Bulgakov treats Christ's work under the three offices: **prophet**, **high priest**, **king**. Redemption belongs to the high-priestly ministry, but that ministry is wider than redemption: it also founds the **deification** of human nature. Redemption is "the way to deification".
- **The Lamb slain before the foundation of the world** (Rev 13:8; 1 Pet 1:19-20): creation is already an act of God's sacrificial love, and in creating, God "takes responsibility for its salvation upon Himself". So the Incarnation isn't just a reaction to Adam's fall, as if God first "conducted the experiment" of leaving the world alone. The Incarnation itself is already sacrifice: "a body hast thou prepared me" (Heb 10:5).
- His survey of theories: the Fathers asked to whom the **ransom** was paid (the devil: Origen, Nyssa; God: Gregory the Theologian); Athanasius placed its power in the victory over death; **Anselm**'s "juridical thought" made it a question of the equivalence of sin and sacrifice. Each takes a true idea and makes it **one-sided**.
- Bulgakov doesn't simply reject satisfaction language. He says Christ really bore sin and "God's wrath directed at sin"; in Gethsemane "God's justice received satisfaction". But he recasts it:
    - The relation is **ontological, not juridical**. Socinus asked how one person's suffering can pardon another's sin; Bulgakov: that question is "marred by individualism and juridicism". Christ isn't "another individual": the New Adam contains every human being in His nature, and by love makes our sin His own.
    - The one who pays is **God Himself as Creator**: "You are My work... since I am responsible for you, I take upon Myself the responsibility for your guilt."
    - The **whole Trinity** suffers: the Father as the judge who judges, in His Son, Himself as Creator; the Son as the one judged; the Spirit as the love that offers. The Father doesn't send the Son "while He Himself remained dispassionate".
    - **Gethsemane** and Golgotha are one act; the forsakenness on the cross is "spiritual death", after which "the Divine Trinity is reunited".
- **Death** is the furthest point of kenosis, "the deepest point of His in-humanization", not a dis-incarnation. There's no death "as a positive principle of being, for God did not create it".
- Redemption is objective for all but has to be received freely: grace "does not coerce", it "convinces, captivates, and regenerates".
- (Bulgakov's sophiology is controversial, and his strong language about wrath and the Father's co-suffering is his own. But his insistence that the Cross be read ontologically and Trinitarianly is shared widely in Orthodoxy.)

## The Historical Sequence

| Theory | Who | Core |
|---|---|---|
| **Recapitulation / theosis** | Irenaeus, Athanasius, Cappadocians, Maximus | Christ heals and deifies human nature by uniting it to God and passing through death |
| **Ransom / Christus Victor** | Origen, Gregory of Nyssa | Christ frees us from the devil and death; the "fishhook" |
| **Satisfaction** | Anselm (1098) | Christ restores God's offended honour; a debt of honour |
| **Penal substitution** | Calvin, Reformed (16th c.) | Christ bears the Father's punishment for sin in our place |
| **Moral influence** | Abelard | the Cross shows God's love and moves us |

Most Orthodox writers don't reject **substitutionary** language as such (Isa 53:5, "he was wounded for our transgressions"), but reject making **penal satisfaction of the Father's wrath** the centre.

## More from Irenaeus (Balthasar's selection)

- Balthasar's summary: the second Adam is "the repetition, in divine truth, of the first Adam", going back over the whole development of man at a higher level. Fallen man isn't just "put back on course", he's taken into God's love.
- **The exchange**: "He became the Son of Man to accustom man to receive God and God to dwell in man" (III.20.2). "In His immeasurable love, He became what we are in order to make us what He is" (V, preface).
- **Why it had to be a man**: "had man not vanquished the enemy of man, the enemy would not have been justly vanquished"; had it not been God who saved, "we could never have possessed it securely".
- **Why from Mary and not fresh dust**: if the Word had taken flesh from some other substance, "He would not have recapitulated man in Himself". The likeness had to be kept, so the same nature is healed.
- **Eve and Mary**: "What was bound could not be untied without a reversal of the process of entanglement"; Luke runs the genealogy back from Christ to Adam to show that the Lord gave the fathers rebirth, not they Him.
- **Recapitulation of suffering**: hunger, weariness at the well, tears over Lazarus, sweat like blood, blood and water from the side are "all signs of flesh taken from the earth". Christ recapitulates "the blood shed by the prophets and all the just from the beginning" (Matt 23:35): "He saved in Himself what in the beginning had perished in Adam" (V.14.1).
- **Redeemed by persuasion, not violence**: apostasy had snatched us unjustly; the Word took us back justly, not by force (V.1.1).
- **Man created as an infant**: God could have given perfection from the start, but a creature "newly created" couldn't receive it, as a mother doesn't give a baby solid food. So the Lord came "not as He might have done, but as we were capable of beholding Him", giving Himself "as milk". Man must "first be made, once made to grow, once grown to become an adult... once glorified to see His Lord" (IV.38).
- **The "salvific order of sin"**: God permitted man to be swallowed like Jonah, not to perish but so that he'd learn by experience he's mortal and God alone immortal, and love more, since the one forgiven much loves much. "How can you be a god when you have not yet become a man?... Await the Craftsman's hand patiently" (IV.39.2).

## More from Athanasius (Behr translation)

- **"You shall die by death"** (Gen 2:17) means not just dying but "to remain in the corruption of death" (ch. 3).
- Man is "by nature mortal, having come into being from nothing", but would have "blunted his natural corruption" by staying in the likeness of the One who Is. "Evil is non-being" (ch. 4).
- **Repentance**: "If then there were only offence and not the consequence of corruption, repentance would have been fine" (ch. 7). That one sentence is basically the whole difference from a purely legal model.
- The Word offers His body "to the Father... on behalf of all", so that "the law concerning corruption" is "fully expended in the lordly body". Athanasius does use **substitution** language ("as a substitute", ch. 9) and says death "had to" happen because "what was required from all still had to be rendered" (ch. 20); but what's owed is death, and the cure is life woven into the body.
- **Ch. 44**: why not save by a nod, as He created? Because creation was from nothing, but healing is of something that exists. Corruption "was not outside the body, but attached to it", so life had to be "interwoven with the body". Image: straw wrapped in **asbestos** no longer fears the fire.
- **Ch. 24**: He didn't die beheaded like John or sawn apart like Isaiah, so that His body stayed undivided, leaving "no pretext for those wishing to divide the Church".
