*The Birth of Tragedy out of the Spirit of Music* (1872) was N.'s first book, written while he was a young classics professor at Basel. Professional philologists hated it (Wilamowitz wrote a pamphlet against it). In 1886 he reissued it as *The Birth of Tragedy, Or: Hellenism and Pessimism*, with an "Attempt at a Self-Criticism" that half-disowns it.

N. = Nietzsche, BT = *Birth of Tragedy* (section). For N.'s Greeks more generally (Winckelmann, the contest, Homer) see [[Nietzsche and the Greeks]].

## 1. The Problem: Silenus

- King Midas hunts down Silenus, companion of Dionysus, and asks what is best for man. Silenus laughs: "The very best thing is... not to be born, not to be, to be nothing. However, the second best thing for you is: to die soon" (BT 3).
- The Greeks knew this better than anyone. Their myths are full of it: Oedipus, the wisest man, kills his father and marries his mother; Prometheus is punished for loving mankind.
- Yet they didn't despair. They flourished from Homer to the 4th century. **How?**
- **Young**: the whole book stands in **Schopenhauer**'s shadow, in two ways:
  - the metaphysics: the visible world is appearance, a "dream"; behind it is the one Will, the thing-in-itself;
  - the pessimism: pain is the rule and joy the exception, so we're always close to "nausea" at life and its "absurdity" (BT 7).
- **Reginster**: this is already the problem of **nihilism**, the "terrible wisdom of Silenus" that it's better not to be. See [[Nietzsche on Nihilism and Its Overcoming]].

## 2. Four Responses (Young)

| response | example | how it works |
|---|---|---|
| **none** | the age of the Titans before Homer, when Silenus's wisdom was "popular philosophy" | it doesn't |
| **Apollonian** | Homer, the Olympian gods | "joy in appearances": the world glorified |
| **Dionysian** | Aeschylus and Sophocles | "metaphysical comfort": joy *behind* appearances |
| **Socratic** | Socrates, Euripides, modern science | faith that reason can understand and **correct** existence |

**Young's provocative claim** (following Geuss): the young N. agrees with Schopenhauer that life is **not** worth living. All three, Apollonian, Dionysian and Socratic, are "illusions" by which the Will tricks us into going on living (BT 18). Note the shift:
- "only as an aesthetic phenomenon is existence and the world eternally **justified**" (BT 5)
- becomes "only as an aesthetic phenomenon do existence and the world **appear** justified" (BT 24).

## 3. Apollo

- **Key word: dream.** Apollo = image-making art (sculpture, epic). Dionysus = imageless music. In good dreams, as in good art, "all forms speak to us: nothing is superfluous" (BT 1).
- **Two senses of "Apollonian":**
  - the everyday world of individuals, the *principium individuationis*. Apollo is the god of boundaries, measure and individuality;
  - that same world **transfigured**, raised to glory in Homer.
- "Thus do the gods justify the life of man by living it themselves—the only satisfying theodicy!" (BT 3). The Greek gods "deify everything, whether good or evil". Contrast an art that sets a non-human ideal against human life.
- **Not censorship of facts.** Homer is full of death, so Apollonian art isn't hiding horror. Young: it's a matter of **focus**. Uccello's *Battle of San Romano* has bodies on the ground, but your eye goes to the horses and the armour. The censorship is of **perspective**: the inside view of loss and dying is left out.
- **Why it isn't enough**: it can't face **my own** death. It treats every death "objectively", so Homeric man has no consolation when his own end comes. Hence N.'s line from GS that the Greeks were "superficial—out of profundity".

## 4. Dionysus

- **Key word: intoxication** (*Rausch*). Dionysian music is dithyrambic. It moves the whole body to dance, like the medieval St John's and St Vitus's dancers.
- Metaphysically: the barriers between individuals dissolve. "Under the charm of the Dionysian not only is the union between man and man reaffirmed, but nature... again celebrates her reconciliation" (BT 1). Schiller's and Beethoven's "Ode to Joy".
- **Pure (barbarian) Dionysianism has two faces:**
  - universal brotherhood;
  - a "witches' brew" of sensuality and **cruelty** (BT 2). If identity is universal, sacrificing an individual hardly matters.
  - It also brings indifference to state, family and homeland, a slide toward "Indian Buddhism" (BT 21).
- **The Greeks "spiritualised" it** (Young). The barbarians made real human sacrifices; the Greeks did it **in art**, "in effigy". (Compare Girard on Dionysus and the lynching mob: [[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]].)
- **Hamlet** (BT 7): the Dionysian man, like Hamlet, "has gazed into the true essence of things", and action disgusts him because it "can do nothing to change the eternal essence of things". It isn't that he reflects too much. It's that he **knows** too much. Knowledge kills action; action needs the veil of illusion.

## 5. Tragedy: The Two Together

- Tragedy grew out of the Dionysian **chorus**, communal singing to the god. Actors and plot were "born" from the music later (hence the full title).
- **No spectators.** The Athenian audience identified with the chorus: "the whole is just one sublime chorus" (BT 8). All the citizens were there, paid to attend like jury service. A religious occasion, not entertainment.
- **The tragic effect.** We watch the destruction of the finest of men and feel strangely comforted. Why? Because, with the chorus, we briefly become the primordial One itself and feel that "in the ground of things... life is indestructibly mighty and pleasurable" (BT 7). The destruction of individuals is necessary given "the exuberant fertility of the world-will" (BT 17). Heraclitus's child building sandcastles and knocking them down (BT 24).
- **But not pure Dionysus.** The Apollonian side of the drama "restores the almost shattered individual with the healing balm of illusion" (BT 21). We think it's only about one man's fate, and go home comforted and able to act again.
- So Greece found a middle way, placed between India (pure Dionysian brooding) and Rome (pure Apollonian chasing after power and honour).
- **Young's objection**: in the 1886 "Attempt", N. claims he already had his later "pessimism of strength" in 1872 but spoiled it with Schopenhauerian language. Young says this is a confusion. The 1872 book still teaches resignation. That the Will enjoys the spectacle of destruction does nothing to justify life to those "who have the misfortune to have to be parts of the entertainment", like Christians in the arena.

## 6. Myth

- Tragic heroes are types, not individuals (the actors wore masks), so they have universal meaning (BT 23).
- Religions die when "orthodox dogmatism" turns myth into historical fact, so that it can no longer "throw out new shoots" (BT 10).
- "Only a horizon defined by myths completes and unifies a whole cultural movement" (BT 23). Myth gives a people "a secure and sacred place of origin", the "unwritten laws". Young compares Heidegger's "heritage": ethical tradition carried by exemplary heroes.
- **Modern man is "mythless"**, and the symptoms (BT 23) are:
  1. loss of unity: a "wilderness of thought, morals, and action";
  2. a "greedy scramble" for scraps of foreign religions and cultures;
  3. "feverish agitation": frivolous worship of the here and now.
- Young: in 1872 N. wants a **religious revival**, a rebirth of German myth through Wagner. The nationalism goes after the break with Wagner. The belief that a culture needs shared myth never does.

## 7. The Death of Tragedy

- Tragedy didn't decline. N. says it **died by suicide**, at the hands of **Euripides**.
- **"Euripides brought the spectator on to the stage"** (BT 11): ordinary people speaking ordinary reasoning in everyday situations. The chorus shrinks and the Dionysian goes. What's left is naturalism.
- **Aesthetic Socratism**: "In order to be beautiful, everything must be reasonable", parallel to Socrates' "Only he who knows is virtuous" (BT 12). Euripides was its poet. Socrates was the "second spectator" who didn't understand the old tragedy. The new comedy of Menander came after.
- **Socrates' three optimistic propositions**: "Virtue is knowledge; sin is only committed out of ignorance; the virtuous man is a happy man" (BT 14). "In these three basic forms of optimism lies the death of tragedy." The tragic hero has to become a dialectician.
- **The *daimonion*** (BT 13). In productive people, instinct creates and consciousness criticises. In Socrates it's reversed: the inner voice only says no, and consciousness creates. N. calls him "a true monstrosity *per defectum*".
- **Theoretical man** (BT 15): Socrates is the prototype of a new human type, who believes "that thought... reaches down into the deepest abysses of being, and that it is capable, not simply of understanding existence, but even of **correcting** it". That's the faith behind modern science and technology. It is optimistic: it promises "earthly happiness for all" (BT 18).
- **The limit.** When the scientist reaches the edge of what can be known and sees "how logic curls up around itself at these limits and finally bites its own tail", a new kind of knowledge breaks through, **tragic knowledge**, which "needs art for protection and as medicine" (BT 15). Kant and Schopenhauer had already shown that causality holds only for appearances (BT 18).
- **"Socrates, make music!"** In prison Socrates dreamed again and again that he should "make music", and finally wrote verses (Plato, *Phaedo*). N.'s hope for the future is the "**music-making Socrates**": knowledge that has learned its limits and needs art again.

## 8. Porter: Socrates Was Never Simply the Enemy

Porter reads BT against its own surface, and is the most interesting of the three on Socrates.

- **Socrates is a hinge, not a person**: "the one turning point and vortex of so-called world history" (BT 15). He kills tragedy in the 5th century and is the sign of its rebirth in the 19th: the *dying* Socrates vs the *music-making* Socrates.
- **The artist and the theorist are closer than they look.** BT 15 says the artist clings to what remains covered even after unveiling; the theorist enjoys the endless unveiling. Porter: both cling to the "husk". Each could be the other's mask.
- **Socrates as Silenus.** In Plato's *Symposium* (N.'s favourite dialogue since school at Pforta), the drunk Alcibiades compares Socrates to a statue of **Silenus**, ugly outside and full of little gods inside, and to the satyr Marsyas whose music drives people mad (215a–b). So the "anti-Dionysian" Socrates is literally described as a Dionysian figure. Socrates ends the *Symposium* arguing that the same poet should write tragedy and comedy, then leaves at dawn "the last of the revellers" (BT 13).
- **"The Socratic fallacy"**: the mistake of keeping Socrates and Dionysus as clean opposites. Porter: BT requires us "to read in Socrates the image of Dionysus, and vice versa". N.'s notebooks link "the tragic being" and "the music-practicing Socrates", and say "Plato is a Socratic work of art".
- **The Platonism of BT.** BT 14: Plato, condemning art, ends up "by a detour" back where he was at home as a poet. His Forms are an artistic "beyond". But N.'s own Dionysian metaphysics (the true One behind the "dream" of appearances) is just as much a Platonic beyond. The book is more Platonic than it admits.
- **Later N.** keeps the ambiguity: in *Twilight* Socrates is the *buffo*, the ugly plebeian clown ("The Problem of Socrates"), and Dionysus becomes "the philosopher Dionysus". The 1886 preface teases: "O Socrates, Socrates, was that perhaps your secret?"

## 9. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's valuable**
- It was right against Winckelmann's serene Greeks. Greek religion and tragedy took horror seriously, and the "cheerfulness" was hard-won.
- The account of tragedy as communal and religious, not entertainment, fits what we know of the festivals better than a modern theatre picture.
- The critique of "theoretical man" (the faith that knowledge can correct existence and make everyone happy) looks better every decade. It's the same optimism that makes death unbearable to modern people: Young notes that science promises to remove pain but has nothing to say about death.
- Myth and culture: a people does live by shared stories and exemplary lives more than by rules. The Church knew this: the Lives of the Saints, the feasts, the icons.

**Where it goes wrong**
- **Silenus isn't the last word, and the Bible knows his wisdom.** Job curses his birth (Job 3). The Preacher "praised the dead which are already dead more than the living... Yea, better is he than both they, which hath not yet been" (Eccl 4:2–3). Scripture puts this in the mouth of a suffering man, not a cackling satyr, and it isn't the end of the book. The Christian answer is neither Apollo's veil nor Dionysus's dissolution of the self into the One. It is a **person** who goes through death and rises: "O death, where is thy sting?" (1 Cor 15:55).
- **The "metaphysical comfort" is impersonal.** Becoming the world-will for a moment means the individual is swallowed up, which is just Schopenhauer's (and Buddhism's) annihilation dressed as ecstasy. Young's objection holds: it's no comfort to the one destroyed. Christianity promises the opposite: the person is saved, not dissolved. Compare the Neoplatonic One in [[The Problem of the One and the Many]].
- **N. lumps Christianity with Socratic optimism.** But the Cross isn't "virtue is knowledge, the virtuous man is happy". The righteous man is crucified. Christianity has its own tragic depth, and goes beyond it in the Resurrection.
- **Socrates.** The Fathers weren't "theoretical men" either. For them knowledge of God is not discursive reasoning but union (*theoria*, the purified *nous*). Their limits on reason are like N.'s "logic biting its own tail", but they end in apophatic theology and prayer, not in art as medicine. See [[Christian Platonism]], [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]].
- **Euripides** is now generally read as deeply religious and disturbing, not a rationalist (the *Bacchae*, his last play, is the great Dionysian tragedy). N.'s history was always more parable than scholarship, as Porter's reading shows.
