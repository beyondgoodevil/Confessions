Plato's *Republic* is famous for sending the poets out of the ideal city. But what Plato **actually** bans is narrower than "art," and his reasons are stranger and more serious than they first look. Three modern readers take them seriously in different ways: **Iris Murdoch** (the objection is **religious**), **Alexander Nehamas** (it's an attack on **popular entertainment**, and we still make it about television), and **Myles Burnyeat** (it's about **who controls the culture**). Here's how it works:

### 1. What Plato Actually Says

**Books II–III (education of the Guards).**
1. **Content.** Poetry tells lies about the gods: that they cause evil, change shape, deceive. Heroes lament, laugh, lust and disobey. All of this is cut.
2. **Manner.** Socrates distinguishes plain **narrative** from **mimesis**, where the poet speaks as if he were the character (Homer "as if he were himself Chryses", 393a–b). Guards may imitate good men, and bad ones only in jest.
3. **The dramatist.** A poet who can imitate anything at all is politely escorted to the border (398a). The ideal city runs on one man, one job.

**Book X (adult citizens).**
1. **"Maim the thought."** All imitative poetry seems "to maim the thought of those who hear them and do not as a remedy have the knowledge of how they really are" (595b). Socrates says it despite a lifelong "friendship for Homer."
2. **The three couches.** There is the couch "in nature, which ... a god produced," the carpenter's couch, and the painter's (597b). The painter just carries "a mirror" around (596d); he is "third from a king and the truth" (597e) and makes only "a phantom" (598b).
3. **The greatest charge.** Even the best of us enjoy, in tragedy, the lamenting we'd be ashamed of in our own lives. Poetry "fosters and waters" sex, anger, pleasures and pains "when they ought to be dried up, and sets them up as rulers in us when they ought to be ruled" (606d).
4. **What's left.** "Only so much of poetry as is hymns to gods or celebration of good men." Otherwise "pleasure and pain will jointly be kings in your city instead of law" (607a).
5. **The old quarrel, and an open door.** "There is an old quarrel between philosophy and poetry" (607b). But Socrates invites "lovers of poetry" to make a prose defence showing poetry is "not only pleasant but also beneficial," and admits "we ourselves are charmed by them." Until then, they will chant the argument "as a countercharm" (607c–608a).

### 2. Murdoch: The Objection Is Religious

Murdoch reads Plato's whole philosophy as a **pilgrimage from appearance to reality**, from the shadows of the Cave to the sun of the Good. Art, she argues, sits at the lowest level, ***eikasia***, image-ridden illusion, the prisoners watching shadows.

- **Art apes the spiritual.** Her summary of the charge: art is dangerous "chiefly because it apes the spiritual and subtly disguises and trivializes it." It offers "a false transcendence," "a pseudo-spirituality," a comfortable "fire in the cave, which is mistaken for the sun" (pp. 24–25).
- **A caricature of recollection.** For Plato, beauty prompts *anamnesis*, the soul's memory of the Forms. Art produces "a sort of dangerous caricature of anamnesis": what it seems to reveal is really a shadow "out of the private store-room of the personal unconscious" (pp. 19, 25). Here she brings in Freud and Auden: everyone secretly wants art to be **magic**.
- **Beauty excludes art.** Plato gives beauty a central role (the *Symposium*, *Phaedrus*) but "practically defines it so as to exclude art." Real beauty works on us through love and through the order of the cosmos. The only true artist is the Demiurge, "and only one true work of art, the Cosmos" (pp. 4, 22).
- **Puritan, but not only.** Plato hates the theatre: its vulgarity, ridicule and mob noise. Murdoch thinks he is "right in general ... about the cheapening and brutalizing effect of an atmosphere where everything can be ridiculed" (p. 8).

**But.** Murdoch turns Plato against himself. Plato was a great artist (the Cave, the charioteer, the Demiurge), and the dialogues are artful. Good art "provides work for the spirit": it can purify egoism by "intelligent imagination," and attending to it "can be like praying." The prescription for art is the same as for philosophy: "overcome personal fantasy and egoistic anxiety" and see the world justly, including what is terrible (pp. 27–30). Art is "far and away the most educational thing we have" (p. 31).

### 3. Nehamas: Plato and Television

Nehamas says the embarrassment disappears once we see that Plato's attack is "a specific social and historical gesture" (p. 34).

1. **Not "art."** The fine arts as a category didn't exist (Kristeller). And Plato doesn't ban **painting or sculpture** at all; painting is only the analogy. The target is poetry because, as a medium, it suits **vulgar subjects**: the "irritable" character is easy to imitate, the quiet wise one is not, "especially for a festival crowd" (604e).
2. **Imitator vs imitative.** Book III lets the young imitate good characters; Book X bans "mimetic" poetry. Nehamas resolves the apparent contradiction: what's banned is ***imitativeness***, the urge to imitate anything regardless of its moral quality.
3. **Transparency.** Plato assumes representation is **transparent**: the portrayed grief *looks exactly like* real grief, so our pleasure in it is pleasure in grief itself, and it carries over into life. He never considers that the pleasure might be in the **representation**, an object with its own features.
4. **We still think this way.** Every attack on television's sex and violence ("a vast wasteland"; "we become what we see"; Postman's "spiritual devastation") repeats Plato's argument almost word for word. Greek drama was **popular entertainment**: 17,000 people, free tickets, food thrown at bad actors, women said to have miscarried at the Furies' entrance (pp. 42–43).

- **Significance:** Nehamas' sting: either Plato was more reasonable than we think, or we should re-examine our own views on the popular media. Popular arts look transparent **while they are still popular**; once a genre becomes "fine," we start seeing its conventions and interpreting it.

### 4. Burnyeat: Who Controls the Culture?

Burnyeat starts bluntly: Plato "did no such thing" as banish poetry. The right sort of poetry is everywhere in the ideal city, in hymns, festivals, common meals and songs of praise. What he banished were Homer and the tragedians, and "to rebut Plato's critique of poetry, what is needed is not a defence of poetry, but a defence of the freedom of poets" (p. 54).

- **Poetry was the mass media.** Think of football songs, karaoke, carols from King's, and television, not someone reading T. S. Eliot in bed. *Paideia* means both culture and education, and Plato's message is "culture should be taken seriously for what it is: education" (p. 57). If you care about the souls of the young, you can't just set rules for parents; "the entire culture must be purged."
- **Mimesis = presence.** Burnyeat's key idea: mimesis makes the audience feel **present at the events**, not just told about them. A carol narrates the Nativity, but when Jesus' words are sung in Bach's *St Matthew Passion*, "we hear him" (pp. 58–59). That is why mimesis is so powerful, and so dangerous when its object is bad.
- **The dramatist as one-man band.** In Book III, the dramatist breaks the one-man-one-job principle by voicing everyone and everything (even thunder and frogs). He would be "a one-man band at the street corner asking to join the Berlin Philharmonic." The objection here is political: tragedy and democracy feed each other ("theatrocracy," as the *Laws* calls it).
- **Nothing wrong with mimesis as such.** Plato wants imitation of steadfast, good character, because "what you imitate regularly is what you become" (p. 65).

### 5. An Assessment

- **Common ground.** All three agree the target is narrower than "art." It is the **culture-forming power of performed poetry**, and Plato's worry that we become what we enjoy watching is not silly.
- **Where they differ.** Murdoch finds the deepest motive in Plato's **religion**: images compete with the Good. Nehamas finds it in a mistaken theory of **representation** (transparency). Burnyeat finds it in **politics** (who governs the ethos, and Plato's authoritarian answer). He challenges democrats either to find their own answer "or stop bleating."
- **A Christian aside.** The quarrel didn't stay pagan. Murdoch notes that art "instinctively materializes God," and wonders whether Christian images have become a barrier. That is the iconoclast worry. The Orthodox answer (see the icon notes) is that after the Incarnation the image is no longer "third from the truth" but can be a window onto its prototype.

### Conclusion

Plato doesn't banish art; he bans imitative poetry (Homer and the tragedians) while keeping hymns, praise and music that form good character. His arguments are that poetry tells lies about the gods, that the painter and poet are "third from the king," and above all that tragedy "waters" passions that should be dried up. Murdoch reads this as a religious protest against a false transcendence, which art can nevertheless overcome when it teaches unselfish attention. Nehamas reads it as the first attack on mass entertainment, still alive in our attacks on television. Burnyeat reads it as an argument about controlling a culture that educates everyone. Socrates' open invitation still stands: if poetry can show it is "not only pleasant but also beneficial," it may come home.
