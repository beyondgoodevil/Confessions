Peter Ahrensdorf's book starts from a remark of Plato's. Socrates calls Homer "the best and most divine of the poets", the first "teacher" and "leader" of the tragedians, the educator of Greece, and the "general" of every early philosopher except Parmenides (p. 1). Ahrensdorf takes this seriously. Homer, he argues, was a philosophic thinker who taught the Greeks to rely on human reason instead of the gods, and who hid his own life of thought behind the mask of a singer inspired by the Muse.

Three later philosophers then took a position on Homer. Plato attacked his education as harmful to both the city and philosophy, and replaced it with his own. Machiavelli and Nietzsche, Plato's two great critics, both called on Homer against him. The book follows that order, and so does this note. Sections 1–8 are Homer, 9–11 Plato, 12–13 Machiavelli, and 14–15 Nietzsche.

Ahrensdorf states his conclusion in advance (p. 8). Homer "established a way of philosophizing that was eminently discreet". He built a humane and tragic culture that pointed to the philosophic life as best, but did not praise it openly, "lest its radical freedom of mind weaken the moral and religious underpinnings of society and provoke deadly persecution". The tragedians, the comic poets and Thucydides followed this model. Plato broke with it and openly made the philosopher a hero. "The key to Plato's success, and the price of his success, was his presentation of the philosopher as a defender of morality and religion" (p. 8).

### 1. The surface of the poems

Homer educates, Ahrensdorf says, by "surprising and shocking" his audience (p. 16). The *Iliad* is a Greek poem about a Greek war against Asia, yet it is about Achilles' anger at his fellow Achaians, it treats the Trojans with deep sympathy, and it ends with Achilles' compassion for Priam. The *Odyssey* is about a homecoming, yet it ends with Odysseus slaughtering the Achaians of his own island. "The greatness celebrated in Homer's poems is not a narrowly Greek greatness but rather a universal, human greatness" (p. 17).

The poems also challenge the Greeks' deepest belief, that the gods watch over them (pp. 21–31). Homer's characters trust in "Father Zeus", a name the Achaians and Trojans use 27 times in the *Iliad*. Chryses prays to Apollo for justice. Both armies swear oaths on Zeus to keep the truce. Agamemnon is sure Zeus will punish the oath-breakers: "These things will not go unfulfilled" (*Il.* 4.157–168). In the *Odyssey* a suitor says the gods "range at large throughout the cities, looking upon the hubris and the lawfulness of humans" (17.485–487), and Laertes, hearing that the suitors are dead, cries "Father Zeus, there are indeed still gods in tall Olympus" (24.351–352).

On the surface Homer supports this faith (pp. 27–31). The *Odyssey* has a happy ending brought about by Athena and Zeus, and Odysseus is called joyful 26 times, more than any other character in either poem. In the *Iliad* the gods are on stage in every book but one, Zeus weeps tears of blood for Sarpedon, and the poem is framed by two answered prayers: Chryses' in Book 1 and Priam's in Book 24. "Homer appears, then, on the very surface ... to be a champion of piety, a poet who justifies the ways of the gods to men" (p. 31). Ahrensdorf's method is to separate what the characters believe from what Homer shows.

### 2. The limits of divine power

What Homer shows first is that the gods are divided (pp. 31–41). Zeus's first words in the *Iliad* are about his quarrels with Hera, and 24 of his 38 speeches concern conflict with her or her allies. He boasts that if all the gods hung on a golden cord they could not pull him down, while he could pull up earth and sea with them (8.5–28). Yet he is defied by Hera, Athena and Poseidon, tricked by Hera with the help of Sleep, and he once "shrank in awe" before Night. Achilles reports that the gods once bound Zeus and that Thetis alone saved him (1.396–406). Zeus keeps his throne by threats and, eight times, by giving way.

Behind the quarrels lies an order beyond all the gods. The word *phusis*, "nature", first appears in Greek in the *Odyssey*, when Hermes shows Odysseus "the nature" of the plant that will defeat Circe's magic (10.303). Ahrensdorf, following Strauss, reads this as "a fixed order in the world — a natural order — that is beyond the power of the gods" (p. 37). Homer points to the same order through Oceanus. Achilles claims Zeus is stronger than Oceanus, but Oceanus is the only god who ignores Zeus's summons to assembly (20.4–7), he hid Hephaestus from all the gods for nine years, Hera calls him "the origin of the gods" and Sleep calls him "the origin of all things" (14.201, 245–246). On the shield of Achilles, Oceanus runs round the rim of the whole cosmos, while Zeus does not appear at all. "Oceanus is the all-encompassing natural order" (p. 39). Aristotle said Thales learned from Homer that water is the first principle.

Nature limits the gods in two ways (pp. 41–47). In the *Iliad* they cannot make a mortal immortal. Zeus cannot save his son Sarpedon or his beloved Hector, Thetis cannot save Achilles, and Achilles finds that the ghost of Patroclus has "no mind in it at all" (23.103–104). Even in the *Odyssey*, which raises the hope of Elysium, Athena says that death "being the same for all, even the gods are not able to ward it off" (3.236–238). More important, the gods are bound by their own immortality. They "cannot empty themselves of their immortal nature" (p. 45). This gives them what Karl Reinhardt called their "sublime frivolity". Hephaestus' fall from heaven raises "unquenchable laughter", and when Diomedes stabs Aphrodite she is healed at once, while the nine Trojans he killed just before leave their fathers "lamentation and miserable sorrows" (5.155–158). "Everything is at stake for the human beings in their conflicts ... But for the eternal gods ... nothing of significant or lasting consequence is at stake" (p. 47).

### 3. The limits of divine wisdom and love

Because they cannot die, the gods cannot understand those who do (pp. 47–60). Ahrensdorf gives two cases in which humans see further than gods.

**Odysseus in *Iliad* 2.** Zeus sends Agamemnon a lying dream to lure the Achaians into a rash attack. Agamemnon first "tests" the army by telling them to go home, and they run for the ships. Both rulers, divine and human, assumed the soldiers wanted to fight. The episode is "a comedy of errors about these two rulers ... each of whom tries to manipulate his subjects through deception" (p. 50). Hera and Athena tell Odysseus to stop the flight with "gentle words". Odysseus instead takes Agamemnon's sceptre, speaks gently only to the chiefs, and beats the common soldiers. He knows force is needed because he knows from experience what nine years from home mean: "anyone who remains one month away from his wife is impatient with his many-oared ship ... Therefore I am not indignant with the Achaians" (2.292–297).

**Priam in *Iliad* 24.** Zeus expects Achilles to give back Hector's body out of fear of the gods and desire for ransom. Ahrensdorf shows how often such appeals failed. Odysseus spent 38 of his 82 lines in the embassy listing Agamemnon's gifts, and Achilles replied "Hateful to me are his gifts" (9.378). The dying Hector offered gold and was told "Do not, dog, entreat me" (22.345). Priam mentions the ransom only in passing. Ignoring Hermes' advice to invoke Achilles' mother and son as well, he kisses the hands that killed his sons and speaks only of Achilles' mortal father: "remember your father ... I put my lips to the hands of the man who killed my children" (24.486–506). He can do this because his own grief lets him understand Achilles'. "No god can truly know the heart of Achilles because no god can feel the human passions that burn in that human heart" (p. 59). Achilles then grants a twelve-day truce that no god asked for. So "the gods' immortality, a manifestation of their perfection, paradoxically limits their understanding of human beings" (p. 60).

Divine love has the same limit (pp. 60–63). Zeus lets Sarpedon die to avoid a quarrel and never mentions him again. Thetis weeps for Achilles but makes no effort to stop him going to his death, while Hecuba bares her breast to beg Hector to come inside the walls. Thetis even laments that she was married to a mortal, which comes close to wishing her son had never been born.

### 4. Divine indifference and human providence

The worst revelation comes in *Iliad* 3–4 (pp. 63–70). The two armies agree to end the war by a duel between Menelaus and Paris, and "rejoiced, hoping to put a stop to the miserable war" (3.111–112). Menelaus wins. Then Zeus, at Hera's demand, sends Athena to make the Trojans break their oaths, the oaths sworn on Zeus himself. He even tells Hera she would only be satisfied if she could "devour Priam and the children of Priam ... raw" (4.35–36). Then he gives way, on the condition that she never oppose him when he wants to destroy a city she loves. He says Troy is the city he honours most, "For never did my altar lack of a fair feast" (4.48). Hera offers Argos, Sparta and Mycenae in return. Homer comments: "So she spoke and he did not disobey her, the Father of Gods and Men" (4.68). Ahrensdorf's reading is blunt: Zeus "casually sacrifices the lives of mortals he 'loves' because, it turns out, he does not genuinely love mortals at all" (p. 69).

Homer marks six points where he warns against trusting the gods (pp. 70–73). He calls Agamemnon "fool!" for believing the dream, the first time he calls anyone a fool. He says Zeus took Agamemnon's sacrifices "but increased the unenvied toil". He says the gods would not grant the oath prayers. "Pallas Athena refused" the Trojan women's prayer, which also casts doubt on the prophet who advised it. The gods took no part in Hector's great sacrifice. And when Achilles prays that Patroclus win glory and come back safe, "The Father granted him one, but refused him the other" (16.250). Meanwhile Zeus sits "rejoicing in his glory, looking upon ... men killing and men killed" (11.80–83).

What of Odysseus, Athena's favourite? Ahrensdorf argues that the *Odyssey*'s order hides its story (pp. 75–78). The poem begins only when the gods finally decide to help. Odysseus' own tales in Books 9–12 show ten years in which Zeus, Athena and Poseidon abandoned or punished him. When Athena says "I always indeed stand by you", Odysseus answers that after Troy "I did not see you, daughter of Zeus" (13.314–321). "The surface of this artful poem, then, is a theodicy ... The story of Odysseus proves to be, not of divine providence, but of divine caprice" (p. 78).

Achilles travels furthest from trust to self-reliance (pp. 78–82). He begins sure that "If a man obeys the gods, they heed him greatly" (1.218). Ingratitude makes him ask whether anyone, human or divine, rewards sacrifice: "He still goes down to death, the man who has done no deeds, and the one who has done many" (9.320). After Patroclus dies he tells Priam that "the gods spun life for wretched mortals, that we live our lives in grief, but the gods themselves have no sorrows" (24.525–526), and he grants the truce without calling the gods to witness it. Odysseus, by contrast, recoils from what his wanderings taught him and relies blindly on Athena once home. His happiness, Ahrensdorf says, "is based on a willful delusion" (p. 82).

### 5. Achilles as a tragic model

Homer's education in human excellence begins with heroes who excel in speech and deed, in council and battle (pp. 83–95). Achilles is the leading model. He speaks more than anyone else in the *Iliad* (974 lines, against 602 for Agamemnon), uses the most striking similes, and alone among the heroes questions the dutiful life. He is also the most criticized character and weeps the most. "To imitate Achilles is to imitate a tragic figure, whose greatness, flaws, and sorrows — upon reflection — lead one to consider another, less visible, but superior model of human excellence, that of the contemplative and humane composer ... Homer himself" (pp. 87–88).

Achilles alone has human teachers, Phoenix and the centaur Chiron. Phoenix taught him "to be a speaker of speeches and a doer of deeds" (9.443). At the plague, when Nestor and Odysseus say nothing for fear of Agamemnon, Achilles alone calls the assembly and saves the army (pp. 89–90). War tests duty most sharply, and Achilles knows he will die if he stays.

Then come the doubts (pp. 90–95). What is honour worth from cowards, or at all if one dies for it? He stays because he loves his best companions and sees nothing better at home. But he comes to feel a tension inside friendship itself. Patroclus and he both want to be "the best of the Achaians", and only one can be. The friendship based on military virtue rests on "a good that cannot be truly shared" (p. 93). His very excellence harms the others. Like a mother bird whose chicks never learn to fly, he leaves them no room, and Diomedes, Ajax and Patroclus shine only while he is away. At the funeral games he holds back so they can win. At the end he is "more aware and more humane", but still kills twelve Trojan youths and still threatens Priam (p. 95).

The happiest moment of any human in the *Iliad* is Achilles singing "the glories of men" to Patroclus (9.186–189). He sings independently, to humans and about humans. Right after, he reflects on whether glory can repay death. "Thinking about honor, rather than seeking it or even possessing it, has, for the moment at least, supplied Achilles with some portion of the happiness that has eluded him" (p. 97). Odysseus is a more practised storyteller, but he tells tales for advantage, "always wielding the mind in his breast for the sake of much gain" (*Od.* 13.255). Achilles' singing points, in its reflection and its friendship, to Homer.

### 6. Homer the humane skeptic

Homer narrates 55 per cent of the *Iliad* in his own voice, far more than the 30 per cent of the *Odyssey*, and in that voice he draws a portrait of himself (pp. 102–124).

- **Judgments.** After calling Agamemnon a fool for trusting Zeus, he calls ten more men fools for hoping to cheat death, by gold, speed, mercy or the gods. He states that he is not a god (12.176). He condemns Achilles' prayer as "beyond measure", his treatment of Hector's body as "disgraceful", and the killing of the twelve youths as "evil" (p. 107).
- **Deaths.** He describes 290 battlefield deaths and 134 of them physically: the spear through the teeth and under the tongue, the vein sheared from back to neck, the throat "where the destruction of a soul is swiftest" (22.325). These accounts are medically exact, and they leave no afterlife. His characters shrink from that: they address corpses thirteen times and fight over them as a cow guards her calf. Even Achilles, who says "You will not resurrect him" (24.551), keeps talking to dead Patroclus. "Even the lionhearted warrior shrinks from the truth of our mortality that the clear-eyed poet calmly accepts" (p. 114). Yet Homer also gives obituaries of seventeen ordinary men, such as Simoesius who never repaid his parents for rearing him, and twenty times writes "darkness covered his eyes" from the dying man's side. Wisdom and compassion go together (p. 117).
- **Similes.** Homer gives 309 similes in his own voice, against 33 by all his characters, and they range from stars to wasps, rivers, fire, birds and 84 animals (pp. 117–124). Ahrensdorf calls them "products of Homer's philosophic mind" because they point to "the genuinely common properties of seemingly unconnected things" (p. 121). The wounded Agamemnon is like a woman in labour, which leads one to ask what pain is and whether pleasure can be the good. Menelaus rejoicing at the sight of Paris is like a hungry lion, which asks whether the desire for justice is as visceral and self-interested as hunger. Falling warriors are like felled trees. These are, in effect, Socratic "What is" questions.

In skepticism toward the gods, acceptance of mortality, the quest to understand the whole, the move from perceptible particulars to common forms, and the placing of man between beasts and gods, Homer shows "what were to be hallmarks of classical philosophy" (p. 124).

### 7. The enigmatic singer

Yet Homer never presents the philosophic life (pp. 124–131). He hides even as a poet. The word "poet", in the sense of maker of stories, never appears in Homer; it first appears in Herodotus, who says Homer and Hesiod "composed the genealogy of the gods for the Greeks" (2.53). Homer presents himself only as the Muse's mouthpiece.

The *Odyssey* lets the mask slip. Its singers are called "divine" twelve times, and blind Demodocus seems wholly the Muse's instrument. Yet Phemius sings of the "miserable homecoming" Athena inflicted on the Achaians, and Demodocus of the destruction "on account of the plans of great Zeus" (8.81–82), so both teach distrust of the gods. And when the raging Odysseus is about to kill him, Phemius blurts out that he is *autodidaktos*, "self-taught" (22.347).

Why hide? Ahrensdorf gives three political reasons and one teaching reason (pp. 129–131). Singers live at the mercy of powerful men, like the suitors or the raging Odysseus. Claims of superior wisdom provoke envy, as with Thamyris, whom the Muses silenced. Teachings that shock the pious provoke popular hostility, and later philosophers who were less discreet, Protagoras, Anaxagoras, Diagoras, Socrates and Aristotle, were all prosecuted for impiety. Plato's Protagoras says Homer and Hesiod used poetry as a "disguise" for this reason. And thinking must be one's own: "by presenting the philosophic life as something to be imitated directly, one might risk provoking, in a self-contradictory manner, a dogmatic and thoughtless imitation of an independent-minded and thoughtful way of life" (p. 131). "The path to the life of the mind, Homer suggests, lies through the life of political and military virtue" (p. 131).

### 8. Plato's critique, first point: the gods

Can Plato's real view be known when he speaks only through Socrates? Ahrensdorf thinks so (pp. 132–133). Xenophon's Socrates never attacks Homer, so the critique is Plato's. The *Republic* is narrated to a wider audience, its guardians are to be philosophic, and Socrates repeatedly judges Homer by the standard of truth.

The setting seems to favour Homer (pp. 134–138). Founding a city in speech is a poetic act. The question of whether justice is good is asked of warriors who sacrifice for others, as Achilles asks it. The guardians must be "by nature a philosopher and spirited and swift and strong" (376c), and Achilles is all of these. Why not follow the Homeric education?

Socrates' answer is that the guardians must be raised "first in the false ones" (377a), stories they will not question even as adults (pp. 138–145). Homer's fault is that he does not lie "nobly". Some things should not be told "even if they were true". So "Homer is in certain respects too truthful ... Socrates signals his apparent intention to bring to an end the Homeric enlightenment" (p. 139). Underneath is a disagreement about reason. Homer assumes "a natural robustness to human reason", which can grow through painful experience. Socrates thinks reason is precarious and passion always threatens it, so the soul must be shaped from childhood. When youths hear of gods at war, they will imitate them; the hidden lesson about the gulf between gods and men is too hard, since "Humans instinctively overlook the gulf between divine and human nature" (p. 142). "Socrates criticizes Homer here, then, not precisely for being philosophic, but for being too openly philosophic" (p. 143). Athens followed Homer in mixing questioning with politics; Socrates "appears to favor here a less Athenian and more Spartan model of politics" (p. 145).

Yet Socrates' own theology also frees the guardians from reliance on the gods, more gently (pp. 145–152). His first law says the god is good and cause only of good, "responsible for a few things for human beings, and not responsible for most" (379c). His second says gods never change form, which removes the disguised gods who help Homer's heroes. Socrates even cites the suitor's line about gods roaming the cities, whose next line is "looking upon the hubris and the lawfulness of humans" (*Od.* 17.487); the law quietly forbids belief in providence altogether. He does it without stirring the passions of hope and fear, by offering "a soothing account of perfect but passive divine beings" (p. 151).

Ahrensdorf notes a telling detail. Socrates cites Homer's three key episodes about the gods, *Iliad* 2, 4 and 24, in reverse order, and attacks first the Achilles of Book 24, the hero at his most enlightened, for saying Zeus dispenses good and evil (pp. 147–149). Socrates even invents a line to make the point. Even at his freest, Achilles still hopes in Zeus. So the most advanced Homeric model "falls between two stools": "He is neither a good citizen nor a good philosopher" (p. 152).

### 9. Plato's critique, second point: Achilles, death and grief

Socrates criticizes Achilles each of the five times he names him (pp. 152–171). The attack is partly polemical. Achilles is said to lack courage although he is the bravest hero, to love money although he refused Agamemnon's treasures, and to be disobedient although others rebuked Agamemnon too. Socrates praises Odysseus' endurance, though Odysseus endured in order to kill the suitors and twelve maidservants. But beneath the polemic is a serious point about the passions. In Homer, Achilles thinks because he feels: anger makes him question duty, grief makes him contemplate death. Socrates replies that the same passions overwhelm the questioning.

The focus is death (pp. 156–163). Of seven Homeric passages Socrates bans to make the guardians fearless, six concern Achilles. Socrates gives no argument that death is good. He argues that believing it evil makes men slaves to fear, which harms both political freedom and reason. "Paradoxically, the rational fear of death undermines reason" (p. 159). Hence "reason is in need of lies, a bodyguard of lies, that, from childhood on, protect reason from the passions by calming the passions" (p. 159). Cephalus, who wakes at night "just as children do" terrified of punishment in Hades, shows the fear at work.

Grief is next (pp. 163–171). The decent man must believe that "being dead is not terrible" for his decent friend, and must be "self-sufficient", needing others least. The goal is a man "who is not only fearless but also loveless" (p. 166), so that the young will laugh at Achilles' laments. Homer teaches resignation by the opposite path. Achilles loves and grieves to the full, and it is out of that grief that he tells Priam to stop mourning, while still letting the Trojans mourn Hector in peace. "One must learn such resignation through experience and in some sense through one's passions rather than simply through one's reason" (p. 169). Socrates answers in Book X that we take pleasure in lamentation and that pity, once fed, "is not easily held down in one's own sufferings" (606b). "Compassion, then, is not an ally of understanding, as it appears to be in Homer, but an adversary" (p. 171).

### 10. Plato's critique, third point: Homer too hidden

Socrates never discusses Homer's own judgments, deaths or similes, and blurs Homer's words with his characters' (pp. 172–182). Ahrensdorf explains why. When Socrates retells the opening of the *Iliad* "as if the poet nowhere hid himself", he drops the invocation of the Muse. Homer hides even in his own voice, because he claims to be only a channel for the goddess. So the culminating part of the Homeric education "suffers from a paradoxical defect: It is insufficiently poetic" (p. 178). The singer "lacks flesh and blood, lacks human details, lacks even a name. We know Homer only through his mind" (p. 178). Reason needs habits formed by imitating "the good man" from childhood, so philosophy needs an imitative poetry that puts the philosopher forward as a hero. Book X's attack on all imitation is qualified: Socrates grants that "the wise and quiet character" is hard to imitate and hard to grasp in a theatre (604e), which leaves room for private, beneficial imitative poetry, namely Plato's dialogues. When Socrates asks "my friend Homer" which city he improved, the point is only that we cannot know, because Homer left no portrait of himself.

Plato's answer is the revolution (pp. 182–193). Plato hides himself, but appears in the *Apology*, is pointedly absent in the *Phaedo*, and makes his brothers the main speakers of the *Republic*. "Plato hides himself, then, but he does not hide the way of life he admires and imitates ... for the first time in history ... Plato openly presents a genuine, proper model of excellence, the philosopher Socrates" (p. 183). That Socrates is fearless before death ("to fear death, men, is nothing other than to seem wise but not be so", *Apol.* 29a), serene, self-sufficient to the point of dismissing his wife, apolitical, and devoted to two doctrines, the Forms and the immortality of the soul. These calm the fears of death and of never reaching wisdom. Ahrensdorf notes that both doctrines are questionable in Plato's own text: the Forms are "customarily" affirmed and never proved, Parmenides' objections go unanswered, and the *Phaedo*'s last argument proves only that "the form of life itself" cannot perish (pp. 188–190). Followers may examine them later, in a calm frame of mind formed by imitating a calm Socrates. Above all, Plato's Socrates is pious, a servant of Apollo and "the gift of the god" to Athens (p. 191). "Plato brings the philosopher out from the shadows into the full light of day; but he reveals the philosopher to be a respectably and reassuringly pious figure rather than the radical skeptic he was hitherto suspected of being" (p. 191).

A defender of Homer has two replies, Ahrensdorf says (p. 192). The Platonic education is apolitical, and so may weaken politics and starve philosophy of the experience it needs; that objection will be Machiavelli's. And it is predisposed to dogmatism; that will be Nietzsche's.

### 11. Machiavelli and the return to the active life

Machiavelli never names Homer, but he names Achilles at two key points of *The Prince* (pp. 194–207). Princes should exercise the mind by imitating great men, "as they say Alexander the Great imitated Achilles; Caesar, Alexander; Scipio, Cyrus" (P 14). And the ancients taught "covertly" that Achilles was raised by the centaur Chiron so that a prince would know "how to use both natures", beast and man (P 18). Machiavelli's teacher Marcello Adriani was an *Iliad* commentator.

With Homer and against Plato, Machiavelli makes active, political men his models (pp. 195–202). He gives three reasons. First, an education that glorifies "humble and contemplative more than active men" has "rendered the world weak and given it in prey to criminal men" (*Discourses* 2.2). He blames Christianity openly and Plato by implication, linking both with "republics and principalities that have never been seen or known to exist in truth" (P 15). Second, politics cannot be escaped: "I wish to live quietly and without quarrel!" is an excuse "heard and not accepted" (D 3.2). The only disciples of Plato he mentions failed as conspirators, and Dion and Cicero failed at politics. Third, the contemplative life misleads the mind: imagination opposes "the effectual truth of the thing". So "the separation of philosophy and politics effected by Plato is not only harmful to politics; it is also harmful to philosophy" (p. 202).

Machiavelli was himself a philosopher, Ahrensdorf argues, though he hides it (pp. 202–207). He says "it is good to reason about everything". In his famous letter to Vettori he describes entering at night "the ancient courts of ancient men, where, received by them lovingly, I feed on the food that alone is mine and that I was born for ... and they in their humanity reply to me" (p. 203). He was also Florence's secretary for fourteen years, was tortured, and wrote "I love my country more than my soul". Politics fed his philosophy. The letter itself shows the danger of private study, forgetting poverty and death. His wisdom came, he says, "with so many hardships and dangers for myself". After his torture he wrote that he now thought himself "more of a man than I believed I was" (p. 207).

### 12. The lion, the fox and the end of tragedy

For Homer, Achilles' virtue is tragic (pp. 210–218). It means devoting oneself to others, like the mother bird, yet it seems to deserve a reward, honour. When Agamemnon threatens his prize and the army stays silent, Achilles asks whether the noble life can be good for the one who leads it. Homer leaves "an abiding conflict, a tragic conflict, between the virtuous human being's noble devotion to virtue and his reasonable desire for happiness" (p. 218). That conflict points to the singer.

Machiavelli removes it (pp. 218–225). Of fifteen named virtuous men in *The Prince*, twelve flourish, and Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus and Severus are called "happy". He redefines virtue. "One cannot call it virtue to kill one's citizens, betray one's friends"; yet Agathocles' "greatness of spirit in enduring and overcoming adversities" is virtue (P 8). Glory, which seems to go only to the selfless, is gained by deceiving: "So let a prince win and maintain his state: the means will always be judged honorable ... in the world there is no one but the vulgar" (P 18). There are "two kinds of combat", by laws and by force, and even fellow citizens are combatants: "because men are wicked and do not observe faith with you, you also do not have to observe it with them" (P 18).

Is this what the ancients taught? Ahrensdorf checks Machiavelli's sources (pp. 225–229). Cicero gives the man and beast and the lion and fox, but says fraud deserves greater hatred than force and that faith must be kept even with an enemy. Plutarch's Lysander said "where the lion's skin does not reach, one must stitch on the fox's skin", but Plutarch excuses his cruelty only because it served Sparta, not himself. Both allow cunning only for one's city.

And Homer (pp. 230–240)? Chiron is mentioned just four times and once called "the most just of centaurs" (11.832). Achilles is the most ferocious warrior, and Apollo says he has "destroyed pity" like a lion (24.40–44). But Homer's Achilles hates deception: "that man is just as hateful to me as the gates of Hades, who conceals one thing in his mind, but says another" (9.312–313). The Achilles who has Hector struck down unarmed is Shakespeare's, and Machiavelli's, not Homer's. Odysseus is the master liar and a lover of glory; facing death at sea, he wishes he had died at Troy so the Achaians "would have brought me glory" (5.311). But Homer still makes Achilles the greater model, as Alexander understood. The reason lies in what Achilles wants from honour: a true recognition of real merit from those worthy to judge. He refuses to be honoured "as a god", unlike Hector, and loves the friends who criticize him most. Honour won by "the great pretender and dissembler" could never satisfy him. When he finds even the Achaians' honour self-serving, he is led to divine that there is "a more fulfilling good for a soul such as his own than mere power and praise" (p. 240), the singer's life.

Machiavelli hints at the same doubt (pp. 240–246). He promises "perpetual" glory, but in *Discourses* 2.5 sides with the "philosophers" that the world is eternal and argues that floods, plagues and changes of religion and language wipe out all memory, so eternal glory is an illusion. His happy princes do not point beyond themselves, so he hides his philosophy even more than Homer. Partly this is because his world was weakened by the open praise of contemplation. Partly it is caution about imitation. *The Prince* shows that Pertinax was ruined by imitating Marcus and Caracalla by imitating Severus; one must imitate with prudence, and prudence cannot itself be imitated. That applies even more to philosophy, whose goal is wisdom. "To imitate Machiavelli, and perhaps any philosopher, is to seek out wisdom, in loving and questioning conversation with philosophers past and present, but ultimately to follow one's path to wisdom on one's own and for oneself" (p. 246).

### 13. Nietzsche: Plato versus Homer

Like Machiavelli, Nietzsche attacks Christian weakness in the name of antiquity, but chiefly in the name of Greece, the "highest culture", and Greece was founded by Homer (pp. 247–258). "The greatest fact in the education of Greece remains that Homer became pan-Hellenic so early. All the intellectual and human freedom the Greeks attained goes back to this fact" (*Human, All Too Human* 1:262). Against him stands Plato, who prepared Christianity: "Christianity is Platonism for the 'people'". Hence: "Plato versus Homer: that is the complete, the genuine antagonism" (*Genealogy* 3.25).

Yet Nietzsche's stance is ambiguous (pp. 253–258). He names Plato, not Homer, among the eight dead thinkers he consults in the underworld "like Odysseus". He hopes people will one day read Xenophon's *Memorabilia* instead of the Bible. And like Plato, not Homer, he openly holds up the philosopher as the model human being, the free mind who "demands reasons, the others faith". In that respect "Nietzsche reveals himself to be a follower of Plato rather than of Homer" (p. 258).

What Nietzsche admires in Homeric Greece is its acceptance of human nature (pp. 258–269). The Greeks did not condemn the "terrible" drives for victory and pleasure but gave them a "right of the second rank", channelling them into war, politics, games and contests. Christianity tried to cut them out, "castratism", and so turned them inward into bad conscience and self-torture. Homer made this possible by giving the Greeks gods who were "the mirror image of the most successful exemplars of their own caste" (HATH 1:114): "Thus do the gods justify the life of man: they themselves live it — the only satisfactory theodicy!" (*Birth of Tragedy* 3). Homer was himself "profoundly unreligious". And Nietzsche, like Ahrensdorf, sees the Homeric gods' indifference: we suffer "so that the poets shall not lack material".

Nietzsche prefers Odysseus to Achilles (pp. 270–278). "What did the Greeks admire in Odysseus? Above all, his capacity for lying and for cunning and terrible retribution ... his being able to be whatever one wants ... his mind is the admiration of the gods" (*Dawn* 306). Achilles is divided by his passions and wants to be truly noble; Odysseus wants only to win. Nietzsche reads him as an image of the free mind, who breaks his mother's heart as the free mind must break the hearts of those who love it, and who parts from life "as Odysseus parts from Nausikaa — blessing it more than in love with it". Ahrensdorf notes that this Odysseus is idealized. Only 3 of Nietzsche's 27 references to the *Odyssey* come from its second half, where Odysseus trusts the gods completely. Homer's Achilles, not his Odysseus, comes closest to freedom from providence. Nietzsche never calls Homer a philosopher. His inaugural lecture on Homer already complained that one finds "nowhere the solid kernel of a powerful personality". His highest tribute is to imitate Homer the deifier by making a new god, Dionysos, who "is a philosopher".

### 14. Nietzsche's critique of Platonism

Does Nietzsche want to bring back Homeric Greece (pp. 278–289)? He seems to: "the necessity of counter-Alexanders who will retie the Gordian knot of Greek culture"; a "new caste that will rule Europe"; master morality against slave morality. But only two of 296 aphorisms in *Beyond Good and Evil* sketch that politics, and the book ends not with a law but with his "beloved — wicked thoughts" and a poem "From High Mountains". By his own account Homeric culture was fragile. Its gods deified only the strong and could not console the weak: "when Greek mankind at last, one and all, grew sick, this was a reason for the demise of such gods" (*Dawn* 424). All humans are weak in the face of death, and Homer's heroes pray to the gods far more than Nietzsche admits. Every culture mixes master and slave morality, so none can satisfy human nature for long. And a noble culture could not even appreciate Homer: "We enjoy Homer again ... perhaps it is our most fortunate advantage" comes from democratic, mixed Europe (*BGE* 224).

So "beyond good and evil" is a demand on philosophers, not peoples: "that he take his stand beyond good and evil and leave the illusion of moral judgment beneath himself" (*Twilight* 7.1). Nietzsche praises master morality to wake his democratic age from its certainty, as Socrates would have challenged an aristocratic one (pp. 289–292). The philosopher is "greatest who can be loneliest, the most concealed, the most deviant" (*BGE* 212), which is closer to the Socratic philosopher of the cave and the gadfly than to the Homeric noble (pp. 293–295).

Why then attack Plato (pp. 295–305)? Nietzsche calls dogmatic philosophy a "grimace" that great things wear "in order to inscribe themselves in the hearts of humanity", Platonism in Europe being one. He asks how "the most beautiful growth of antiquity" could catch such a disease, whether "the evil Socrates" corrupted him. Ahrensdorf reads this as saying Plato did not believe in Platonism. Plato wore it as a mask to make philosophy look moral and pious after Socrates' execution. Nietzsche cites Epicurus calling Plato an "actor", the volume of Aristophanes under his deathbed pillow, and the old distinction between esoteric and exoteric teaching. Plato's error was rhetorical, and it worked too well: everyone came to think philosophy is dogmatic and moral, "The philosophical life is misinterpreted". Nietzsche's cure was Thucydides and *The Prince*. Yet he follows Plato, not them, in openly praising the philosopher; he only reverses the mask, presenting the philosopher as "the first immoralist", as Antichrist, as "a terrible explosive endangering everything". Even so, "every philosophy also conceals a philosophy" (*BGE* 289). In this "does Nietzsche, the critic of Platonism and therefore of Plato himself, reveal his fundamental affinity with Plato" (p. 305).

### Conclusion

Ahrensdorf's book argues three things. The first is that Homer was a philosophic thinker. The poems begin from the audience's trust in Father Zeus and lead it, through quarrels among the gods, the hint of a natural order in *phusis* and Oceanus, and the gods' inability to understand or love beings who die, to a sober reliance on human providence. Achilles, the greatest and most questioning hero, follows that road furthest. Behind him stands the singer, whose judgments, accounts of death and similes reveal a calm, compassionate and inquiring mind, but who hid himself behind the Muse for safety and so that his listeners would think for themselves.

The second is that the later tradition defined itself against this model. Plato thought Homer trusted reason too much, made passion the road to understanding, and hid so well that only Achilles was left to imitate. He answered by bringing the philosopher into the open, as a pious, fearless and self-sufficient Socrates, at the price of making philosophy look like a defence of morality and religion. Machiavelli went back to Homer's practice of pointing readers to the political life and hiding philosophy, but dropped the tragedy, so that his happy princes no longer point beyond themselves. Nietzsche praised Homer as the founder of the highest culture, but in the decisive point sided with Plato: he too put the philosopher at the centre, only now as skeptic and immoralist, to undo the damage Platonism had done.

The third is that there are many ways to live a philosophic life. A philosopher may appear as inspired singer, adviser to princes, champion of morality or open immoralist, depending on his times and his nature. Ahrensdorf hopes this will make readers ask whether some poets, statesmen, historians and even theologians of the past may have been philosophers "in the reticent manner of Homer" (p. 308).
