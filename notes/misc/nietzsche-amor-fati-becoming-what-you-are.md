> "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose." (Romans 8:28)

> "Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father. But the very hairs of your head are all numbered." (Matthew 10:29–30)

> "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good." (Genesis 50:20)

Nietzsche left two short formulas about fate. **Amor fati**, love of fate, is about how you think and feel about what has to be. **"Become who you are"** is about what you do with it. Robin Small's way of pairing them helped me: amor fati is the attitude and becoming who you are is the practice. Both are paradoxes on purpose. How do you *love* what you can't change? How do you *become* what you already *are*? And how can a fatalist give a command at all? Here's how it works:

N. = Nietzsche, GS = *Gay Science*, Z = *Zarathustra*, BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, TI = *Twilight of the Idols*, EH = *Ecce Homo*, NCW = *Nietzsche contra Wagner*, KSA/KSB = the standard German editions of the notebooks/letters.

## 1. Background: Necessity, Chance and the Teenage Essays

- **The schoolboy essays.** At 17 (1862) N. wrote "Fate and History" and "Freedom of the Will and Fate" for his school literary club. Both draw heavily on **Emerson's essay "Fate"**. So the theme is there from the start, before Schopenhauer.
- **Necessity.** In the free-spirit books N. is a hard determinist, partly via Paul Rée. His image is the **waterfall** (HAH 106): we think we see free will in the countless bendings and breakings of the waves, but "all is necessary, every movement mathematically calculable". An omniscient mind could do the same for every human action. Later he says that "occurrence and necessary occurrence is a tautology". But he also rejects "laws of nature" as a moralising, legal metaphor. Things don't *obey* anything. They just happen as they must.
- **Chance.** And yet he loves the word **chance**. Zarathustra calls himself the "redeemer of accidents" (Z II "On Redemption"; III "On Old and New Tablets" 3). In "Before Sunrise" he says that "By Chance" (*Von Ohngefähr*) is "the most ancient nobility of the world", and he gives it back to all things. Small notes this probably echoes Luther's Bible at Wisdom 2:2, "we are born at all adventure", which there is the creed of the *wicked*. N. likes that kind of reversal.
- **Fate is not the same as necessity.** Small's point: "fate" means an overall **pattern for one life**, not just that every event has a cause. Robert Solomon's model fits: the **outcome** is fated but the **path** isn't. Oedipus can take any road he likes and he still ends up in Thebes.
- **The thought that lifts you.** In a late note, N. says fate is an *elevating* thought for the one who grasps that he **belongs to it**. That is the key to everything below: you are not outside fate, looking at it. You're part of it.

## 2. Emerson and the Stoics

**Emerson** ("Fate", "Spiritual Laws")
- Man is himself part of fate and so "can confront fate with fate".
- The best use of fatalism is "to teach a fatal courage". Compare N.'s "live dangerously" (GS 283).
- Each person has a "selecting principle" that picks out what belongs to him. It turns up almost word for word in EH "Wise" 2: the well-turned-out person is an *auswählendes Princip*, a selecting principle.

**The Stoics**
- N. mocks them in BGE 9: "According to nature" you want to live? Imagine nature, "wasteful without measure, indifferent without measure". Isn't living precisely "wanting to be different than this nature"? The Stoics claim to read their law out of nature, but really their pride wants to prescribe their morality *onto* it (Del Caro trans.).
- But he keeps their tag. Seneca (after Cleanthes): *ducunt volentem fata, nolentem trahunt*, "the fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling". The NCW epilogue's "not merely bear it, but love it" is a step beyond the willing dog trotting behind the cart.
- **The idle argument.** The old objection to fatalism: if I'm fated to recover, I'll recover whether I call the doctor or not, so why call him? Chrysippus answered that the calling of the doctor is **co-fated** with the recovery (Cicero, *On Fate*). N.'s own version: the single human being "is a piece of *fatum* from the front and from the rear", so telling him "Change yourself!" is demanding that everything be changed, "even retroactively" (TI "Morality as Anti-Nature" 6). His choices are part of what's fated, not alternatives to it.

## 3. Turkish and Russian Fatalism

This is where N. makes his own distinction.

| | Turkish fatalism | Russian fatalism |
|---|---|---|
| **Where** | *Wanderer and His Shadow* 61 | GM II 15 ("resolute fatalism without revolt"); EH "Wise" 6 |
| **Picture** | Man on one side, fate on the other. You can fight, but fate always wins, so give up, or do anything at all | You are yourself a piece of fate. The struggle is imaginary, and so is the resignation |
| **Result** | Folded hands or caprice. Both nihilistic | Lying still in the snow like the Russian soldier on campaign: not reacting, conserving yourself |
| **N.'s verdict** | The error behind people's fear of determinism | "To accept oneself as a destiny, not to wish oneself 'different'" (EH) |

- WS 61 (Handwerk trans.): Turkish fatalism "makes the fundamental mistake of opposing humans and fate, as if they were two different things". In truth "every human is himself a part of fate". Even "the fear of the belief in fate is fate". And to the "poor frightened one": *you* are "the invincible moira that rules even over the gods".
- GM II 15 uses the Russian example for how wrongdoers once took punishment: like a sickness or a misfortune, with "that resolute fatalism without revolt that even today gives the Russians... an advantage over us westerners".
- In EH he says Russian fatalism kept him for years clinging to almost unbearable places and people because chance had put them there. Anyone who tried to wake him from it felt like a mortal enemy.
- **Stambaugh**: N. never kept the terms apart, so she does it for him. "Fatalism" is the Turkish thing, the target. "Fate" is the Russian thing, his own idea. "He loved fate, not fatalism."
- She also notes a late note where he says the belief in **divine providence** is "the most paralyzing belief for hand and reason there has ever been", and that modern fatalism is its unconscious heir. So for him Turkish fatalism and Christian providence are on the same side: both make fate a power outside you.

## 4. Amor Fati in 1882

**GS 276 (New Year's 1882)**
- "I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things; then I shall be one of those who make things beautiful. *Amor fati*: let that be my love henceforth!"
- Then: I don't want to wage war on the ugly, or accuse, or even accuse the accusers. "Looking away shall be my only negation." Some day I wish to be only a **Yes-sayer**.
- Note the grammar: *I want to learn*. It's an **aspiration**, not an achievement.
- **Small's reading.** Most scholars think "seeing as beautiful" means a new interpretation that turns horror into beauty. Small suggests it might be more like **unlearning**. His example: it took European settlers in Australia generations to see the bush as beautiful, because they kept painting it with European eyes. Seeing the necessary as beautiful might mean dropping the old frames, not adding a new one.

**The letters: "submission to God"**
- In the same months N. uses an odd word in his letters: *Gottergebenheit*, "submission to God", always in quotation marks, usually as "fatalistic 'submission to God'".
- To Overbeck (summer 1882) he says he is in a fatalistic "submission to God" and that he **calls it amor fati**, so much so that he'd leap into a lion's jaws.
- What was going on? **Lou Salomé.** A trip to Messina cut short by the scirocco, then the accident of meeting Lou in Rome: everything seemed to be working out for the best. To Ida Overbeck: fate always strikes me for my benefit, so how could I fear it, "especially when it meets me in the completely unexpected form of L.?"
- So in the letters amor fati is less a love of necessity than a **mood of optimism**, a sense that everything is turning out well. Small notes the irony: the mood helped bring on the crisis, since N. sat in Leipzig trusting fate while Lou and Rée went off to Berlin together.

**GS 277: "Personal providence"**
- The very next section attacks what it calls **personal providence**. At a certain "high point in life" everything that happens seems to turn out for the best: bad weather or good, losing a friend, sickness. It all seems to be something "that must not be missing". That's the moment we're in the most danger of believing in a "petty deity" who personally knows every hair on our head.
- N.'s explanation: it's our own **skill at interpreting and arranging events**. And sometimes good old **chance** guides our hand, and "the wisest providence could not think up a more beautiful music" than the one our foolish hand then plays.
- Small's points:
  - "Providence" is a word N. almost never uses in print. Why call it our "hardest test"? Probably because his own letters had just been talking exactly like a believer in providence. GS 277 is partly him explaining himself away.
  - Historically **fate and providence were the same thing seen from two sides**. Chrysippus (via Calcidius): God's will is providence, and as the series of causes it's also called fate. Boethius: providence is the plan in God's mind, fate its unfolding in the world. Aquinas accepts this but says to avoid the word "fate" because of astrology. So N.'s "providence" in GS 277 is really what Boethius called fate.
  - The "illusion" of providence is like an optical illusion, something real and instructive, coming from a deep **drive to find meaning**. N. doesn't say to get rid of it, just to know what it is.
- **Ridley** calls this a **"naturalized theodicy"**. Leibniz justified the whole world from God's point of view. N. tries to justify each life from **inside it**, first person: look back and find that even the worst things were indispensable *to you*.

## 5. Amor Fati in 1888

The phrase disappears for six years, then comes back three times in his last working year.

1. **NCW Epilogue.** Am I not more indebted to the hardest years of my life than to any others? What's necessary, seen from above, is also the useful par excellence: one should not only bear it, one should love it. "*Amor fati*: that is my inmost nature." My long sickness gave me a "higher health", made stronger by whatever doesn't kill it. ("What does not kill me makes me stronger" is TI "Maxims" 8.)
2. **EH "Clever" 10.** "My formula for greatness in a human being is *amor fati*: that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity." Not merely bear what's necessary, still less conceal it ("all idealism is mendaciousness in the face of what is necessary"), but **love** it.
3. **The Dionysian note** (KSA 13, 16[32] = *Will to Power* 1041). N.'s "experimental philosophy" anticipates even the most fundamental nihilism, but it doesn't halt at a No. It wants "to cross over to the opposite of this — to a Dionysian affirmation of the world as it is, **without subtraction, exception, or selection**", wanting "the eternal circulation". "The highest state a philosopher can attain: to stand in a Dionysian relationship to existence — my formula for this is *amor fati*." The next line goes further: this state perceives not only the **necessity** of the sides of existence denied until now, but their **desirability**.

**What changed**
- 1882: an **aspiration** ("I want to learn"). 1888: a **mark of greatness**, and N. claims to have reached it. EH opens with his 44th birthday, a perfect autumn day, and asks how he could fail to be grateful to his **whole life**.
- Small notices something about that gratitude: it has **no one to thank**. Gratitude is normally *to* someone. Kant says the same in the third *Critique*: the person enjoying a beautiful scene in a moral mood feels a need to be grateful to someone. Neither Kant nor N. calls the feeling a delusion. (I come back to this below.)
- **Goethe** is the model: TI "Skirmishes" 49 credits him with a "joyous and trusting fatalism".
- **The link to eternal recurrence** becomes explicit: "not in all eternity". GS 276 opens Book IV, and GS 341, the demon, closes it. See [[Nietzsche: Eternal Recurrence]].
  - Ridley thinks the link is a **mistake**. Amor fati means saying yes to the real truths of life after God: the past is gone, nothing can be done about it, time devours everything. Eternal recurrence *denies* exactly those truths by pretending the will can "will backwards". It's one of the "shadows of the dead God", a way of making the present moment eternal the way the Christian does. Interesting counterweight to Loeb.
  - Stambaugh goes the other way: willing everything back ("Thus I willed it!" in Z II "On Redemption") is how the will is freed from **revenge against time**.

## 6. Surrender or Affirmation?

Small frames the debate with an anecdote from William James's *Varieties*:
- Margaret Fuller used to say, "I accept the universe." When someone repeated this to Carlyle, he said: "Gad! she'd better!"
- Emerson, Fuller's friend, wrote to her that he found in her view "a certain wilfulness and not pure acquiescence".

The Nietzsche scholars split the same way:
- **Affirmation / will:** Magnus and Gillespie read amor fati as a call to affirm the world, even to *will* responsibility for it.
- **Surrender:** Béatrice Han-Pile says the proper attitude is "surrender, not erotic pursuit, affirmation or wilfulness". Small thinks she makes the stronger case.
- N.'s 1881 note plays both parts: "the task is to live in such a way that you must wish to live again — you will anyway!" The first half is Fuller (a task). The second half is Carlyle (you've got no choice). Small says to hear it as a **dialogue**, not a contradiction to be resolved.

## 7. "Become Who You Are"

**Pindar**
- The line comes from Pindar, *Pythian* 2: roughly, "become such as you are, having learned it". N. drops the "having learned" (Babette Babich points this out). That matters, because he doesn't think self-knowledge is the road.

**Zarathustra's animals** (Z III "The Convalescent") tie the formula to fate: "your animals know well, O Zarathustra, who you are and must become: behold, you are the teacher of the eternal recurrence — that is your destiny!" Small notes that Zarathustra never actually teaches it in the book. He is still becoming who he is at the end.

**GS 270 (1882)**
- "What does your conscience say? — 'You shall become the person you are.'"
- It's one of eight question-and-answer lines ending Book III (GS 268–275): what makes one heroic, what do you believe, where are your greatest dangers ("in pity"), what is the seal of liberation ("no longer being ashamed in front of oneself").
- People usually compare these to a **catechism**. Small has a better model: the Victorian **"Confessions" albums**, a parlour game in which guests wrote their favourite virtue, idea of happiness etc. on printed pages. In Germany the best-seller was called *Erkenne Dich Selbst!* The point of the genre is that the answers are **individual**, not prescribed. (Marx filled one in for his daughters. So did the family dog.)
- It's **conscience** that speaks, and in the imperative, *Du sollst*, like "Thou shalt". N.'s first notebook draft had "my morality" instead.

**GS 335 "Long live physics!"**
- Don't trust your conscience's verdicts blindly. They have a prehistory in your instincts, likes and dislikes. You need a "conscience behind your conscience".
- "We, however, want to become those we are — human beings who are new, unique, incomparable, who give themselves laws, who create themselves." To that end we must be the best students of everything **lawful and necessary** in the world.
- Ridley: here **honesty** both gives the command and makes it possible. You become who you are by finding out what in you and your circumstances is really necessary and what only pretends to be.

**GS 290 "Giving style"**
- "One thing is needful: to 'give style' to one's character." Survey your strengths and weaknesses, fit them into an artistic plan. Here add second nature, there remove first nature, through long daily practice. Conceal the ugly that can't be removed, or reinterpret it until it's sublime.
- Ridley connects this to GS 276: remove what's ugly and **unnecessary**, and learn to see as beautiful (or look away from) what's ugly and **necessary**.
- **BGE 188**: all freedom and mastery in language, art and thought grew under the "tyranny of rhyme and rhythm", owing to "the tyranny of such arbitrary laws" (Del Caro). Necessity is a **condition** of freedom, not its limit. Only someone who accepts the rules of chess can castle.

## 8. Character and Fate

**The idle argument again**
- If N. is a fatalist, what's the point of "you shall"? Magnus: the descriptive fatalism of recurrence seems to defeat the imperative.
- Small's answer: early on N. sided with Schopenhauer against imperative ethics. Character is innate, "an incurable disease", so "thou shalt" treats symptoms. But N. is full of commands anyway ("live dangerously", "remain faithful to the earth", "become hard"). Solution: the self is a **society of drives**, and if a drive could talk, it would talk in **imperatives**. "You shall become who you are" is just one drive's voice, with no "higher" source.

**Granite or becoming?**
- N.'s Basel lectures gloss Heraclitus' "character is fate" (*ēthos anthrōpōi daimōn*) as: the individual's destiny is his inborn character. *Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks* 7 says the same: who could demand of such a philosophy an ethic of "thou shalt"? "Man is necessity down to his last fibre, and totally 'unfree'", if freedom means changing one's *essentia* "arbitrarily, like a garment".
- BGE 231: learning changes us, like nourishment does, but "at the bottom of us" there is "something unteachable, a **granite of spiritual fatum**, of predetermined decision and answer to predetermined selected questions". In every cardinal problem "an immutable 'this is I' can be heard" (Del Caro). (Maybe from Emerson's "floor of granite".)
- But elsewhere character changes, even through radical "metamorphoses" (Z I). Plato's soul with parts, Roux on the struggle of parts inside an organism: personality as drives in **dominance and subordination**, with a ruling passion as health.
- **The chameleons** (late note): people whose various traits sit side by side without conflict or cooperation. They're happy and secure, even "interesting", but "they change, they do not become". That's the model of *not* becoming who you are.
- "Only those who change remain related to me" (BGE, "Aftersong").

## 9. Ecce Homo: How One Becomes What One Is

- The subtitle makes the whole book an answer. Small notes a predecessor: *Richard Wagner in Bayreuth* is about how Wagner "became what he is". In EH N. tells us to read his own name wherever that essay says "Wagner".
- **The shock**, EH "Clever" 9: if your task is far above the average, nothing is more dangerous than catching sight of yourself with it. "To become what one is, one must not have the faintest notion **what** one is." So even the blunders, side roads, delays and "modesties" have their meaning.
- Keep the surface of consciousness clear of any great imperative. Meanwhile the organising "idea" grows deep down, leads you back from wrong roads, and trains the capacities you'll need, before giving any hint of the task.
- Hence the dietary advice, the walks, the choice of climate in the first part of EH. N. presents his life as a work of art shaped by the "secret artistry of instinct" (Ridley).
- **Self-creation?**
  - **Nehamas** (*Life as Literature*), Rorty and Nagel: becoming who you are = creating yourself, and Z is built around this.
  - **Leiter**: the answer to the subtitle is that one becomes what one is "by making no special effort", because one becomes it **necessarily**. Talk of creating oneself is a familiar word in an unfamiliar sense.
  - **Small** sides with Leiter. "Self-creation" appears only alongside other things, like Goethe disciplining himself to wholeness in TI, which is really the old ideal of *Bildung*. So "become who you are" ends up very close to amor fati. Both answer: how do we live with fate?
- Small's last words point to Z III "On Old and New Tablets" (sec. 19 in Kaufmann): "the most necessary soul, which out of sheer joy plunges itself into chance; the soul which, having being, dives into becoming".

## 10. Stambaugh: Amor Dei and Amor Fati

Stambaugh compares N. with **Spinoza**, whom N. in 1881 hailed as his "precursor".
- **What's loved.** Spinoza's God and N.'s fate share: not a personal Creator, no purposes, strictly necessary, immanent.
- **What kind of love.** Spinoza: love is joy with the idea of an external cause. The **intellectual love of God** comes from the third, intuitive kind of knowledge, and "is the very love with which God loves himself".
- For N., in "On the Great Longing", Zarathustra names his soul "destiny" and "turning of need" (*Not-wendigkeit*, a pun on necessity). So the soul doesn't just love fate. It **is** fate. "Soul *lives* fate" the way Sartre said "I exist my body". That's parallel to Spinoza's identity of our love with God's own.
- **Freedom.** For both, freedom is not escaping necessity but acting from your own inner necessity, like a painter getting a stroke exactly right.
- The difference: Spinoza's is a **logical** triumph at the world's necessity. N.'s is **Dionysian**, about creation and destruction.

## 12. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What I take from it**
- The Turkish/Russian distinction is genuinely good. A lot of what passes for Christian "acceptance" is really Turkish fatalism with a God attached: folded hands, "it's God's will", the world as a machine you can't touch. N. is right that this is paralysing. But it isn't the Fathers' doctrine. For them God's providence includes our acts, the way Chrysippus' "co-fated" doctor call does. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]] and [[Free Will and Divine Foreknowledge]].
- **GS 290 is practically ascetic theology.** Survey your passions, cut back first nature, add second nature by daily practice, turn the passions to good use. The Desert Fathers talk the same way about anger turned against the demons. And BGE 188 on freedom through the "tyranny" of rules is exactly the logic of a monastic rule or a prayer rule.
- **EH "Clever" 9** has a real insight. You don't become who you are by staring at yourself and planning. Too early a self-image ruins it. The Fathers warn against *planning one's own holiness*: humility doesn't know it's humble.
- Ridley's "naturalized theodicy" is honest. Most people over forty do look back and see that their worst years made them. The question is what that experience is *of*.

**Where it fails**
- **Gratitude with no addressee.** This is the weak joint. EH's "how could I fail to be grateful to my whole life?" is a feeling that, by its grammar, wants a "to whom". Kant felt it too. Small says N. reports an experience outside familiar categories. I'd say it's a very familiar category with its object cut off. St John Chrysostom died in exile, worn out by a forced march, saying "Glory to God for all things". That's the same feeling, completed. "In every thing give thanks" (1 Thess 5:18) works because there is Someone to thank.
- **GS 277 is a strawman, and it gives itself away.** N. mocks a God who "knows every little hair on our head". That's Christ's own phrase (Matt 10:30), and it isn't about a genie arranging good weather. In the same breath Christ says the sparrows **fall**. Providence in the Gospel doesn't mean nothing bad happens. It means nothing falls outside the Father's care. Note that N. had to explain away his *own* experience to keep the theory, and Small more or less admits it.
- **"Wanting nothing different, not backward."** Here the Christian has to part company. Amor fati in its 1888 form forbids **repentance**: if I want nothing to be different, I can't wish I hadn't betrayed my friend. The Gospel holds two things at once: Joseph's brothers really did evil, and God really meant it for good (Gen 50:20). Paul anticipates the shortcut: "Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid" (Rom 6:1–2). The West's *felix culpa* comes closest to amor fati, and even there the fault is still a fault. See [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]].
- **Love needs a beloved.** Stambaugh's comparison is fair to Spinoza: a God who can't love back. N. goes further: the soul *is* fate, so love of fate becomes love of oneself under the name of the cosmos. Han-Pile's "surrender" reading is the more attractive N., but surrender to *what*? Gethsemane is the model of surrender, "nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt" (Matt 26:39). It is surrender to a Father, not to a necessity, and Christ still prays "let this cup pass". That is honesty N. should have admired: he doesn't pretend to love the cup.
- **Fate and providence: Boethius was right.** Small's history shows that the Christian tradition had already done what N. was looking for. It put necessity, chance and purpose together without making people into puppets. N. rejected the purpose and then spent a decade trying to get its comforts back: gratitude, things "turning out for the best", a destiny. Ridley calls eternal recurrence a shadow of the dead God. I'd say amor fati is one too.

**"Become who you are" and theosis**
- The structure is very Christian. Paul's ethics is indicative followed by imperative: "ye are dead... mortify therefore" (Col 3:3–5); "If we live in the Spirit, let us also walk in the Spirit" (Gal 5:25). Augustine to the newly baptised: be what you see, receive what you are. This answers the "idle argument" better than N. does. You *are* already something by gift, and you're commanded to become it.
- The Fathers distinguish **image** (given, can't be lost) and **likeness** (to be grown into). That's "become who you are" with content. The "granite" at the bottom isn't a fatum but the image of God. The organising "idea" that leads you back from side roads is what Maximus would call your *logos*, God's intention for you. See [[Image and Likeness]] and [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- And N.'s warning holds there too. You don't reach the likeness by staring at it. "Your life is hid with Christ in God" (Col 3:3): hidden even from you.
