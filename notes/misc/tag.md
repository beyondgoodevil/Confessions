The **Transcendental Argument for God (TAG)** begins by establishing that **knowledge**—whether it’s scientific, moral, or any other form of true understanding—requires **transcendental categories** (such as logic, metaphysics, and morality). These categories are necessary conditions for any coherent knowledge claim. For example, scientific data relies on principles like **logic, consistency, and the laws of reason**, which cannot be derived from the data itself but must already be in place for the data to be meaningful.

However, these transcendental categories cannot simply exist on their own. Their very nature demands that they be **grounded** in something **non-contingent** and **eternal**, because they are universal and objective—yet **human minds are finite, subjective**, and **contingent**. This means they **cannot be the source** of such categories. Therefore, the existence of transcendental categories necessitates a **necessary being** that transcends the physical universe and is the foundation of these categories. This necessary being is **God**.

The **impossibility of the contrary** becomes clear here: without God, these **transcendental categories** could not exist in a meaningful, **objective**, or **necessary** way. Without a **necessary foundation**, these categories would become **arbitrary** and **subjective**, and we would lose the **objectivity** that makes knowledge possible. There could be no universal truth, no standard for logic, and no moral law that applies to all beings. Without God, we would be left with **only subjective opinion**, and no claim to true knowledge would be coherent.

Thus, the reason we can have knowledge is because the transcendental categories exist, and the **only way** they can exist in an **objective, universal, and necessary** way is because of the existence of God. Without God, **knowledge itself** becomes impossible, because these necessary conditions for knowledge would be absent. The very structure of knowledge **presupposes** the existence of God, and **without God**, there is no basis for knowledge at all. Knowledge, therefore, **cannot exist** without God, and any system of thought that denies God's existence is **incoherent and self-refuting**.

## What a Transcendental Argument Is

- From **Kant**: a transcendental argument asks what must be true for **experience** (or knowledge, thought, language) to be possible at all.
- **Form**:
    1. X is the case (we have experience / we reason / we know things).
    2. Y is a necessary condition of X.
    3. So Y.
- What makes it powerful: the opponent can't deny X without using X. Kant's Transcendental Deduction argues the categories (cause, substance, unity) are conditions of any experience. Strawson revived the form against skeptics about other minds and the external world.
- **Reppert's** argument from reason has the same structure: reasoning presupposes intentionality, mental causation and the relevance of logical laws; materialism can't supply them; so materialism undercuts itself. See [[The Argument from Reason]] sec. 5.

## The Presuppositional Versions

- **Cornelius Van Til** (Reformed, 20th c.): Christian theism is the precondition of **intelligibility**. Unbelievers "borrow capital" from the Christian worldview whenever they reason.
- **Greg Bahnsen** (famous 1985 debate with Gordon Stein): the "proof of God's existence is the impossibility of the contrary". Logic, science and morality presuppose God; the atheist uses them while denying their foundation.
- **Orthodox version** (Jay Dyer and others): adds that only the **Trinity** solves the one and the many, and that the Orthodox, not the Reformed, Trinity does it: monarchy of the Father, the logoi, the energies. See [[The Problem of the One and the Many]].

## Objections and Replies

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| **Only how we must think** (Stroud, 1968): transcendental arguments only show what we must **believe** or how we must think, not what's actually true | the TAG says the unbeliever already presupposes these things are **actually** true (logic really holds); if they're only "how we must think", the skeptic has to use logic to say so |
| **"Why God?"** Couldn't something else ground logic: brute necessity, Platonic abstracta, the structure of the mind? | brute necessity is no explanation; Platonic abstracta are inert and can't be known (the epistemic access problem); the mind is finite and contingent. See [[Philosophy of Logic: What Grounds the Laws of Logic]] |
| **"Why your God?"** Even if a mind grounds logic, why the Christian God? | the Trinitarian version (see the presuppositional versions above) argues only a God who is both one and many can ground unity and diversity. That's the strongest and most contested step |
| **Circular** | all arguments for ultimate standards are circular in a sense ("you must use logic to prove logic"). The question is whether the circle is **vicious** or whether it's the unavoidable circularity of first principles. See [[A Circular Argument]] |
| **The "impossibility of the contrary"** isn't shown, only asserted | it requires going through each alternative worldview one by one, which no one has done exhaustively. The TAG is strongest as a challenge, weaker as a proof: best used, as Reppert says of his own argument, not as a knock-down proof but as a demand: give an account of logic, truth and meaning on your view |
