> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... All things were made by him... In him was life; and the life was the light of men... And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." (John 1:1–4, 14)

Why did John call Christ the **Logos**, a word Greek philosophy had used since Heraclitus? And how much philosophy is in the Fourth Gospel? Engberg-Pedersen's *John and Philosophy* gives the boldest recent answer: John is thinking in **Stoic** physics.

## 1. What the Prologue Says

Engberg-Pedersen reads John 1:1–5 as a **normative cosmogony**: an account of creation that is also about value and knowledge.
- **Upward**: the Logos is "with God" and "was God". As close to God as possible without being simply identical, which matches the rest of the Gospel: "I and my Father are one" (10:30), yet "my Father is greater than I" (14:28).
- **Downward**: "All things were made (*egeneto*) by him". This is **creation**, not just providence: the verb *gignesthai* echoes Genesis. So "the Word was made (*egeneto*) flesh" is another act of the same creating.
- **Normative**: the Logos made everything, light and darkness, but "in him was **life**", and that life was "the **light** of men". Creation has a point (life), and human beings are meant to **see** it. So cosmology, ethics and knowledge aren't separate here; what scholars call "soteriology" is built into the cosmology.

## 2. Where "Logos" Comes From: the Candidates

Engberg-Pedersen follows C. H. Dodd's survey, then corrects it.

| background | what it explains | what it can't |
|---|---|---|
| **The Hebrew "Word of the LORD"** (Gen 1, "God said"; "By the word of the LORD were the heavens made", Ps 33:6). Boyarin adds the Aramaic *Memra* of the Targums | the Word as creative and pre-existent; the rabbis called Torah "life and light" | "the Word was God" and "became flesh" |
| **Wisdom** (Prov 8:22–31; Wisdom of Solomon): Sophia beside God's throne, making the world, dwelling with men | the personification; Wisdom "pitched her tent" among men | Dodd: "it would be idle to look for any real anticipation of the Johannine doctrine of incarnation" |
| **Philo** (Platonist Jew): the Logos is God's "firstborn", the intelligible world of ideas, God's blueprint and "instrument" (*organon*) | "the Word was with God" (the Logos distinct from a transcendent God) | it puts a layer **between** God and the world, so God stays inaccessible. John says the opposite: seeing Jesus is seeing the Father (14:9) |
| **Stoicism**: the Logos is the active, rational principle in matter; it **is** God | "In the beginning was the Logos, the Logos was God, all things came into being through it" would be, as Dodd says, "directly intelligible and acceptable to any Stoic" | "with God": a Stoic has no God beyond the world |

Diogenes Laertius on the Stoics: "the passive principle... is matter, whereas the active principle is the logos that is in it, that is, God. For being everlasting he creates each and every thing."

**Dodd's conclusion**: John's Logos carries the Old Testament Word, but it also means something like "Stoicism as modified by Philo", parallel to Wisdom.

**Engberg-Pedersen's correction**: drop Philo's Platonism and go straight to Stoicism, with "with God" taken from the Wisdom tradition. Philo separates God from the world; John wants to connect them.

## 3. Engberg-Pedersen's Thesis: Logos and Pneuma

**Logos and pneuma are one reality.**
- In Stoicism the logos (the rational, cognitive side) is carried by **pneuma**, a fine, hot, physical "breath" that pervades matter and holds it together.
- John 1 has both: the Logos in the Prologue, and the Spirit descending "like a dove" on Jesus in the Baptist's witness (1:32–33). Engberg-Pedersen takes them as "two sides of the same coin": "the logos stands for the cognitive side and the pneuma for the purely material one". "God is a Spirit" (4:24) is literally true for him.

**How the Logos "became flesh"**: this is the book's most controversial claim. Jesus of Nazareth was born of Mary after John the Baptist. He **became** Jesus Christ, the "only begotten", "when... the logos descended upon him in the form of pneuma from above and remained on him" at the Jordan. "That was the moment when 'the logos became flesh'. Jesus was not 'incarnated'. Jesus Christ was."
- That, he argues, solves the Baptist's riddle, "he that cometh after me is preferred before me: for he was before me" (1:15, 30). The **man** came after John; the **Logos-pneuma** that came upon him was before.

**Through the rest of the Gospel:**
- **"Born of water and of the Spirit"** (3:5): believers receive the same pneuma Jesus received; "that which is born of the Spirit is spirit" (3:6).
- **The living water** (4:14, 7:38–39) is the pneuma: "this spake he of the Spirit".
- **Jesus' logos**, his word, is more than his statements. Using the Stoic distinction between the **spoken** word (*logos prophorikos*) and the **inner** word (*logos endiathetos*), Jesus' "words" carry the logos-pneuma itself. "The words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life" (6:63). Hence the frightening "my flesh is meat indeed" (6:55): what is "eaten" is the pneumatic body.
- **Resurrection and ascension** are worked by the same pneuma: "I am not yet ascended to my Father" (20:17); the pneuma takes Jesus back to the God who "is a Spirit".
- **The Paraclete** (14–16) is "he" as Jesus continuing, a person, and "it" as pneuma, a material and cognitive power. Its work is literally *paraklēsis*, encouragement, so that the disciples can live the life of mutual love.

## 4. More Philosophy in John

- **Signs** (John 2–12, the "Book of Signs"). The Stoic theory reported by Sextus Empiricus: a sign is the true antecedent of a true conditional ("if this, then that") that is "revelatory of the consequent". So the healed blind man reasons from the sign to "If this man were not of God, he could do nothing" (9:33). But he needs Jesus to tell him the further step, that Jesus is the Son of Man (9:35–37).
- **Two kinds of belief.** Throughout, John distinguishes an "initial" belief, positive but incomplete (believing because of signs, 2:23–24; "many believed", 8:30), from **full** belief, which grasps and holds Jesus' logos and comes with possessing the pneuma. Engberg-Pedersen compares the Stoic division between the rare **sage** and everyone else, the "fools".
- **Friendship** (15:13–15, "I have called you friends"), a Greco-Roman ethical topic, and "bearing fruit" as ethical practice.
- **No "realised eschatology".** Against Bultmann, he argues that believers don't already have eternal life in the full sense. He sees three stages:
  - Jesus' time
  - believers' time, when they have the pneuma, the "first instalment" (Paul's word)
  - the last day and the resurrection
- **Against a Platonising John.** For him, "true" bread and "true" vine don't mean images of a higher Platonic reality but what really leads to eternal life.
- **Method**: a "narrative philosophical" reading. Follow the argument step by step through the story, and use ancient philosophy "heuristically" without needing to prove John read the Stoics. John knew and reworked **Mark**, and develops Paul's themes of being "in Christ" and receiving the Spirit.

## 5. Assessment

*Mine.*

**What's helpful**
- Taking the Prologue as a claim about **creation and its purpose** (life and light), not just a hymn.
- The Philo comparison is a good test. John isn't a Platonist putting a mediating layer between a distant God and the world. In John, the one who has seen Christ has seen the Father (14:9). That is the same instinct as the Fathers' insistence against Arius that the Logos is **fully** God, not a demiurge in between. See [[Council of Nicea: Arianism]].
- Pneuma as something real and active, not just "attitude", fits the Orthodox stress that the Spirit truly **indwells** and transforms, including the body. The bread of life discourse is about real participation. See [[The Holy Spirit in Orthodox Theology]].
- The two kinds of belief and the Stoic "sign" are a useful lens on John's many scenes of misunderstanding.

**Where it goes wrong**
- **Material God.** A Stoic God is a fine material body. Making "God is a Spirit" mean God is physical pneuma contradicts the whole patristic reading (and John's "No man hath seen God at any time", 1:18). The Fathers used the Stoic *logos endiathetos / prophorikos* language too, but only as an analogy for the Son's eternal generation, and they were careful to deny that it was material.
- **Adoptionism.** "Jesus was not incarnated; Jesus Christ was", with the Logos coming on a man at his baptism, is close to the Ebionite and adoptionist positions the Church rejected. It also runs into Nestorius's "assumed man", which Ephesus (431) condemned. For John, the one born of Mary is the Word: the Word "was made flesh", not "came upon flesh". See [[Council of Ephesus: Nestorianism]], [[Nestorian Key Beliefs]].
- It separates what John joins. The Baptist's "he was before me" is easier read as the eternal pre-existence of the **same person** (8:58, "Before Abraham was, I am").
- **The Spirit as a person.** Engberg-Pedersen makes the Paraclete "he" only because it is Jesus continuing. That merges the Son and the Spirit. Contrast "another Comforter" (14:16) and the later Trinitarian reading. See [[The Trinity]].
- **Logos and logoi.** A more patristic way to use the same insight about a rational order of creation is Maximus: the many logoi of creatures held in the one Logos, who is a Person, not a world-soul. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
