"I believe in the resurrection of the body." For that to be good news, the one raised has to be *me*, not a copy. So: what makes someone the same person at two times? Sec. 7 is mine.

## 1. Several Questions, Not One

| Question | Asks |
|---|---|
| **Who am I?** (characterisation) | what traits make up "my identity" in the everyday sense. Not this note |
| **Personhood** | what is a person? Locke: "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places" |
| **Persistence** | what makes a past or future being *numerically the same* as you? |
| **Evidence** | how do we tell? memory, bodily continuity |
| **Population** | how many people are here? (split brains, multiple personality) |
| **What am I?** (ontology) | an animal? a brain? a soul? a bundle of experiences? |
| **What matters** | is identity even what we care about in survival? |

Persistence is about **numerical** identity (one and the same), not **qualitative** (exactly alike). See [[Identity and Leibniz's Law]].

## 2. The Psychological View

**Locke**: you are the same person as whoever's experiences you can remember. Same consciousness, same person.

Problems:

- **Transitivity** (Reid's brave officer): the old general remembers being a young officer; the young officer remembered being flogged as a boy; the general doesn't remember the flogging. So general = officer, officer = boy, general ≠ boy. Identity can't work like that.
- **Circularity** (Butler): to tell a real memory from a false one, you have to know it was *your* experience. Which assumes identity.

Repairs:

- Chains of overlapping memories, not direct memory.
- **Quasi-memory**: memory-like states caused in the right way, defined without presupposing identity.
- **Psychological continuity** generally: memories, beliefs, intentions, character, causally connected over time.

**Fission**: your two brain hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies, Lefty and Righty. Both are psychologically continuous with you. Both can't *be* you (they aren't each other).

- **Non-branching** (Parfit, Shoemaker): you survive only if no one else is equally continuous with you. So fission is death. Odd result: if one hemisphere is destroyed you survive; if both are transplanted you die.
- **Multiple occupancy** (Lewis): there were two people all along, sharing a body until the split. Needs temporal parts (sec. 5).

## 3. The Animalist View

- **Olson, van Inwagen, Snowdon**: you are a human animal. You persist as long as that organism keeps living, whatever happens to your mind.
- Fits common sense about foetuses and dementia: *you* were once a foetus, though with no psychology at all.
- Objection: in a brain transplant, surely *you* go with the brain? Animalist: maybe not. The organism stays behind, like with a liver transplant.

**Too many thinkers**: if you are a person who isn't identical to the animal, but the animal thinks too (it has your brain), then two thinkers are thinking your thoughts. And how would you know which one you are? Animalists say: so you are the animal. Same structure as Zimmerman's "too many minds". See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]] sec. 2.

## 4. The Simple View

- **Anticriterialism** (Merricks, Swinburne, Lowe): there's no informative criterion. Psychological and bodily continuity are *evidence* for identity, but identity isn't made of them.
- Usually goes with **dualism**: you are, or have, a soul, and identity is the soul's.
- Swinburne: identity is simple and all-or-nothing, which is why fission cases feel like they must have an answer even when every physical fact is known.

## 5. Temporal Parts

- **Four-dimensionalism** / perdurantism (Lewis, Sider): you're a "space-time worm" with a different temporal part at each moment, like a road has different parts at different places.
- Persistence = being made of the right kind of linked stages.
- Lets you say two people overlapped before fission. Cost: lots of overlapping candidates for "I", so it can be indeterminate what you are. Crisp's objection: I have my headaches directly, not by having a part that has them. See [[Presentism vs Eternalism]] sec. 3.

## 6. Parfit: Identity Isn't What Matters

- *Reasons and Persons* (1984): what we care about in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness. Identity is just whether that continuity takes a one-to-one form.
- Fission is as good as ordinary survival, though by the non-branching rule you don't survive it. So identity can't be what matters.
- Parfit found this liberating: less of a wall between me and others, and less dread of death.
- Replies (Sosa, Merricks): identity does matter. My future pain matters to me in a way my copy's doesn't.

## 7. The Resurrection

*Mine.*

**Why the question is sharp for Christians**

- Resurrection isn't survival as a soul only. It's a **bodily** raising. "This corruptible must put on incorruption" (1 Cor 15:53). "In my flesh shall I see God" (Job 19:26).
- After death the body decays and its atoms end up elsewhere, even in other people. What makes the risen one *me*?

**How each view does**

| View | Resurrection |
|---|---|
| **Psychological** | easy to state (God restores my psychology) but **duplication** problem: God could make two such beings; which is me? If neither, I'm not raised |
| **Animalist** | the organism dies and decays. Gap. Van Inwagen once suggested God secretly preserves the corpse and leaves a simulacrum. Others: "body-snatching" by a fission at death. Both look desperate |
| **Simple view + dualism** | the soul continues between death and resurrection and carries identity. God gives it its body back. Coherent, but needs a soul |
| **Temporal parts** | a worm with a gap in it. Depends what links the stages across the gap |
| **Parfit** | "it doesn't matter whether it's you" isn't the Christian hope |

**The Fathers**

- They faced this exact objection (Celsus, Porphyry): bodies eaten by fish eaten by men, whose bodies are...
- **St. Gregory of Nyssa**, *On the Soul and the Resurrection*: the soul remains bound to the elements of its own body after death, knows them, and at the resurrection gathers them back, like a householder recognising his own pottery among others'. So the **soul** carries identity, but it's tied to *this* body.
- **St. Athenagoras**, *On the Resurrection*, and **Tertullian**, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, argue the same way.
- **St. John of Damascus**: man is neither soul alone nor body alone but both together. Separation at death is unnatural and temporary.
- **Origen** proposed that a body's "form" (*eidos*) carries identity, not its matter. Partly rejected later as too spiritualising, though something like it is close to modern answers.

So the patristic answer is roughly: **the simple view, with a soul that is the form of a particular body**. Hylomorphism, not Cartesian dualism.

**Christ as the pattern**

- The risen Christ is the same: the wounds remain (John 20:27). He eats (Luke 24:42-43). Yet changed: passes through doors, not at first recognised.
- So the risen body is *continuous* with the old, not a replacement, but transformed. "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (1 Cor 15:44). "Spiritual" = wholly filled by the Spirit, not "made of spirit".
- Same body, new mode: the logos of human nature unchanged, its *tropos* renewed (Maximus). See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]] sec. 3.

**And the theological reason it's me**

- On every view, at bottom, it's God who makes the risen one me. The question is whether there's anything in *me* He preserves. The Orthodox answer: the soul, kept in God's hand ("the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God", Wisdom 3:1), and the body's own logos.
