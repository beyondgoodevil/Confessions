Many Christians read the Old Testament's appearances of God as appearances of the Son before his incarnation. When the Angel of the Lord speaks to Hagar, wrestles with Jacob or calls to Moses from the bush, they see Christ. This reading is ancient: Justin Martyr held it in the second century, and the Reformers revived it. It has had a recent boom in evangelical writing under the name "christophany." In *Knowing Jesus in the Old Testament?* (2015) the Australian Old Testament scholar Andrew Malone argues that the case for it is far weaker than its popularity suggests. His target is not the Son's presence in the Old Testament, which he affirms, but the claim that Old Testament theophanies were appearances of the Son *alone*. This note sets out his three-part argument in full. It then sets beside him Victor Hamilton's commentary on the Angel of the Lord and the Orthodox tradition as Leonid Ouspensky presents it in *Theology of the Icon*, which reads every visible appearance of God as the Son's for a reason Malone does not discuss: the theology of the image. Your earlier notes [[The Angel of the Lord]] and [[Jesus in the OT as Theophany]] take the christophany reading; this note tests it.

### 1. What Is Being Claimed

The word "christophany" means "an appearing of Christ." In older scholarship it named the risen Christ's appearances to the disciples and to Paul. The *Oxford English Dictionary* dates the English word to 1846, when German and French studies of Justin Martyr used it for his view that the Son appeared in the Old Testament (ch. 1). Malone distinguishes three ways a christophany can relate to a theophany, an appearing of God:

1. Every christophany is a theophany, since "Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9).
2. Every Old Testament theophany includes the Son, since the Trinity acts together. On this view each one also includes what scholars call a "pneumatophany," an appearing of the Spirit.
3. Old Testament theophanies are appearances of the Son *alone*, and the Father and Spirit take no part.

Only the third is in dispute. Its modern revival Malone traces to James Borland's 1976 dissertation, published as *Christ in the Old Testament* (1978; 2nd ed. 1999), which says that "Christ alone appeared in the human-form theophanies ... solely the second person of the Godhead." Walter Kaiser wrote its foreword and championed the idea; preachers such as Mark Driscoll then taught it as settled. Driscoll's *Doctrine* says "the Old Testament teaches about Jesus through appearances that he makes before his birth, or what are called Christophanies." Malone's foil throughout is his own colleague Michael Bird, whose *Evangelical Theology* endorses Borland. He judges the idea's popularity to be "like a pious urban legend ... Everyone believes it primarily because the person before believed it" (ch. 1).

He grants the attractions. Christophanies make the Old Testament more obviously Christian. They give weight to New Testament texts that seem to place Jesus in Israel's history (John 8:56; 12:41; 1 Cor. 10:4, 9; Jude 5). They guard against the idea that God "was once one and became three only later," they appeal through novelty and antiquity, and they offer a bridge to Jewish and Muslim readers. But he insists that the question is not a test of orthodoxy: treating it as one is "outrageous." His worry is the opposite of the usual one. The christophany reading, he thinks, "can inadvertently paint God the Father in quite a negative light: a recluse who's unwilling or unable to interact with his world. That's one legacy of Justin Martyr we don't need to inherit" (ch. 1).

The case rests on three arguments, which form the book's three parts: that the Father is invisible, that the Angel of the Lord is the Son, and that the New Testament says the Son was active in the Old.

### 2. Is God the Father Invisible?

The oldest argument is a syllogism, reprised by Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, Eusebius and Calvin:

> 1. Old Testament figures are said to have seen God, but 2. God the Father is invisible, therefore 3. Old Testament figures saw the Son, not the Father. (ch. 2)

Justin put the second premise vividly: "Anyone with even half a brain would not dare suggest that the Maker and Father of everything left all the things of heaven and beyond and appeared on a small patch of the earth" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 60.2, Malone's paraphrase). Malone notes the syllogism is incomplete even on its own terms: nothing in it explains why the visible one must be the Son rather than the Spirit.

**The Old Testament says God was seen.** The first premise is undisputed. The Hebrew verb *r'h* says God "appeared to" Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Joshua and David, and that Hagar, Jacob, Samson's parents, Micaiah, Isaiah and Amos "saw" God. At Sinai seventy-four elders "saw God," and the text says so twice (Exod. 24:9–11). Several who see God are surprised to survive: Jacob at Peniel ("I saw God face to face, and yet my life was spared," Gen. 32:30), the elders ("God did not raise his hand"), and Manoah ("We are doomed to die! We have seen God!", Judg. 13:22). The surprise assumes that seeing God is possible but dangerous. Malone concludes that "the Old Testament knows nothing of an invisible God," and quotes Rudolf Bultmann to the same effect: "In the OT the notion is pervasive that humanity can see God with human eyes" (ch. 2).

**Exodus 33:20 is a prohibition, not a description.** "You cannot see my face, for no one may see me and live" is the key verse for both sides. Malone argues it says what Moses is not *permitted* to do, not what is impossible: the reason given ("and live") implies the seeing could succeed, with fatal results, and God at once lets Moses see something (33:21–23). The same idiom is used when Pharaoh tells Moses, "the day you see my face you shall die" (Exod. 10:28), which hardly makes Pharaoh invisible. Borland and Kaiser, in their own Exodus commentaries, "conflate the two meanings of 'cannot'" (ch. 2).

**The New Testament's "invisible" means "unseen."** The tension comes from a handful of New Testament texts: "No one has ever seen God" (John 1:18; 1 John 4:12), "the image of the invisible God" (Col. 1:15), "whom no one has seen or can see" (1 Tim. 6:16). Malone makes four points.

- **The word.** The Greek *aoratos* was a new word, coined from Platonic negative theology. In use it usually meant "not seen" rather than "unable to be seen." Josephus uses it of the temple's closed interior, a city hidden in the mountains and the valleys around Masada; Plutarch uses it of women kept away from men and of catapults hidden from the enemy; the Septuagint's Isaiah 45:3 promises Cyrus "concealed, invisible (*aoratos*) riches," which are ordinary loot. Like Paul's "unknown god" (Acts 17:23) or "unwashed hands," the word describes what is not the case, not what cannot be (ch. 3).
- **John means recognition.** John 1:18 cannot mean God is physically unseeable, because in the same Gospel Jesus says, "The one who has seen Me has seen the Father" (14:9). Seeing in John is a way of knowing; as Marianne Meye Thompson puts it, "In John, God is not so much invisible as unrecognized." Calvin had said the same: "When he says that none has seen God, it is not to be understood of the outward seeing of the physical eye." Read literally, John 5:37 ("You have never heard his voice nor seen his form") would also deny that God was ever *heard*, which contradicts Sinai, the baptism and the transfiguration (ch. 3).
- **Superlatives are relative.** Kings calls Solomon, Hezekiah and Josiah each incomparable; Paul's doxology calls God "the only Ruler" and the one "who alone has immortality," though the Lamb is King of kings and believers will put on immortality. In idol-filled Ephesus, where pagans could visit their gods' images at any time, calling God "invisible" and "unapproachable" contrasts him with idols; it does not define his tangibility. Even christophany advocates concede this: Jerome Neyrey reads the terms as expressing "God's unknowability," and Anthony Hanson grants that "There is no suggestion that God is naturally and essentially invisible" (ch. 3).
- **The New Testament expects God to be seen.** The pure in heart "will see God" (Matt. 5:8), believers will see "face to face" (1 Cor. 13:12), and the angels "continually see the face of my Father in heaven" (Matt. 18:10).

**A better doctrine: God is seen when he chooses.** Malone's own conclusion follows a line that runs from Irenaeus to Bavinck and John Frame. Irenaeus wrote: "For man does not see God by his own powers; but when He pleases He is seen by men, by whom He wills, and when He wills, and as He wills" (*Against Heresies* 4.20.5). Frame: "God is essentially invisible. This means, not that he can never be seen under any circumstances, but rather that, as Lord, he sovereignly chooses when, where, and to whom to make himself visible." The Old Testament's rules about approaching God (the Sinai perimeter, the Most Holy Place, the Levites forbidden to look at the furniture) fit this. So does Esther, where the king's seven princes "saw the king's face" and anyone entering uninvited died: people die "not because they have seen God but because they have not followed the rules in approaching him" (Samuel Meier, quoted ch. 4). Every theophany, Malone adds, is an accommodation in which God's full glory is held back, and so is the incarnation itself, which is why Justin's objection in principle to the Father's "descent" proves too much (ch. 4).

His decisive question for the christophany side is this: "What is the difference between God the Father and the preincarnate Son that supposedly renders one of them impervious to human sight while the other makes regular appearances?" (ch. 4). Borland himself, Malone notes, ends up conceding that invisibility applies to the Son too and that the Father and Spirit can "relinquish" it.

### 3. Who Is the Angel of the Lord?

The Angel of the Lord is the christophany case's star witness: "This group of [Angel] theophanies is by far the one most widely identified with the Son" (Charles Gieschen, quoted ch. 8). The argument is a second syllogism: the Angel is hard to distinguish from God, yet sometimes seems distinct, so he must be someone "simultaneously God and not God" (ch. 5). Malone sets out three candidates.

| | Candidate | Main support | Main problem (Malone) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | God himself, under another title | The Angel speaks and acts as God, is worshipped, and those who meet him say they have seen God | Why use the title at all? Zech. 1:12 has the Angel address God |
| 2 | A created messenger who speaks for God (Augustine) | Ancient envoys could speak as their senders (Gen. 44:9–10; Deut. 29:2–6) | No text ever distinguishes the two; the Angel never introduces himself as messengers did |
| 3 | God the Son (Justin, the Reformers, Borland) | Explains both identity and distinction; the Son is "sent" (John 17:8) | Must prove distinction as well as divinity, and then that the person is the Son and not the Spirit |

**History.** Justin's reading held for several centuries. Augustine reasoned that if the Father is invisible, so are the Son and Spirit (the doxologies of 1 Timothy include the whole Trinity), so the Angel must be a creature; his view held for more than a millennium. The Reformers returned to Justin, and Borland can fairly call the view "the most ancient view of the church" and "the usual conservative biblical position." Candidate 1 has barely been considered, but Malone thinks it is "slowly starting to overtake the other two" (ch. 5).

**The case for Candidate 1.** Malone's own preference is the Steven Bradbury of the field, the Australian skater who won Olympic gold in 2002 because everyone ahead of him fell (ch. 6). Everyone accepts the evidence that the Angel is God: he is identified as God (Gen. 16:7–13; Exod. 3:2–6; Judg. 6:11–16), calls himself God (Gen. 31:11, 13), receives worship (Josh. 5:14) and is recognized as God by those he meets. Malone adds evidence from the phrase itself.

- *Mal'āk* means any messenger. Between a fifth and a half of singular uses are human, as are nearly ninety per cent of plurals. Haggai and a priest are each called "messenger of the Lord" (Hag. 1:13; Mal. 2:7). The phrase is a job description, "no more a specific individual than is 'the courier from Australia Post'" (ch. 6).
- The "of" may not mean "sent by." Hebrew often uses "of" to mean "that is," as in "daughter (of) Zion" and "the river (of) Euphrates." Following Douglas Stuart, the phrase may mean "the angel that is Yahweh."
- Scripture never says "God sent the Angel of the Lord," never uses a phrase like "messenger of Solomon," and keeps repeating the full title within a story (ten times in fourteen verses of the Balaam episode), as if "Lord" mattered as much as "angel."
- The Gideon story "runs as smoothly as possible if 'Angel of the Lord' is also another title for God" (ch. 6).

Why would God appear under such a title? Malone suggests the Prince-and-the-Pauper pattern: God moving among his people incognito, recognized only at the end, perhaps as part of an "educational programme" preparing them for the incarnation (ch. 6).

**Against Candidate 2.** Malone wanted Candidate 2 to work and found it did not (ch. 7). Parallel accounts, where they exist, merge the Angel with God rather than separating them: Jacob blesses in the name of "God ... God ... the Angel" (Gen. 48:15–16); Hosea says Jacob "struggled with God, struggled with an angel," whom he names "Yahweh, the God of hosts" (Hos. 12:3–5). Only one text clearly has the Angel address God (Zech. 1:12), and it comes from the latest books of the Old Testament, which may reflect the growth of Jewish angelology. That the Angel speaks of "the Lord" in the third person proves nothing, since people speak of themselves that way: Jesus prays, "Glorify your Son, that your Son may glorify you ... Jesus Christ, whom you have sent" (John 17:1–3). Above all, Samuel Meier, the specialist on ancient messengers, judges that "The behaviour of the mal'ak YHWH in many of these disputed passages is precisely that of a deity and not a deity's messenger," and that he "does not behave like any other messenger known in the divine or human realm." Real messengers name their sender, as Gabriel does ("I am Gabriel. I stand in the presence of God," Luke 1:19). Zechariah 3 sums up the difficulty: Malone challenges readers to "Identify who says 'I have taken away your sin' (3:4) and defend your answer" (ch. 7).

**Against Candidate 3.** The christophany reading needs two things: that the Angel is divine, and that he is distinct from God. Its advocates prove only the first. Borland's key chapter is titled "The Christophany Proved to Be an Appearance of God," and Gieschen calls the Angel "indistinguishable from God" before concluding he is "a personal being clearly distinguishable from God" (ch. 8). Even if both were proved, nothing would identify the Son rather than the Spirit, who also takes bodily form (Luke 3:22), is "sent" (Ps. 104:30; Isa. 48:16), and is credited with leading Israel out of Egypt (Isa. 63:9–14; Hag. 2:5). F. F. Bruce said of Acts 8 that "it is difficult to see any real distinction between 'the angel of the Lord' and 'the Spirit of the Lord'." Malone quotes John Walton's verdict:

> When Justin identified the angel of the Lord as the preincarnate Christ, it was not because of exegetical problems with the Old Testament passages. Rather, it reflected his determined intention to find the triune Godhead in the Old Testament in order to defend the cause of Christianity ... The interpretation of the angel as Christ did not arise from exegetical problems but from an aggressive attempt to read distinctives of Christian theology back into the Old Testament with an apologist's agenda. (ch. 8)

Malone ranks the candidates 1, 2, 3. He admits Candidate 1 is "the least satisfying" because it identifies the Angel least precisely, but concludes, with David Howard, that "The exact nature or personality of this divine self-revelation are not known precisely because the Scriptures are silent on the question" (ch. 8).

### 4. What the New Testament Says

The third argument rests on four texts (ch. 9): Jesus' "Abraham rejoiced at the thought of seeing my day" and "before Abraham was born, I am!" (John 8:56, 58); John's comment that Isaiah "saw Jesus' glory" (12:41); Paul's "that rock was Christ" and "We should not test Christ, as some of them did" (1 Cor. 10:4, 9); and Jude 5, where the best manuscripts, followed by the Nestle-Aland 28th edition and the ESV, may say that "Jesus ... saved a people out of the land of Egypt."

Malone's response turns on how later writers describe earlier events (ch. 10). We say "the Queen was born in 1926," N. T. Wright observed, without implying she was queen as a baby. The Bible does the same: Dan is named in Genesis centuries before the city had the name; Chronicles values David's gifts in Persian darics; Exodus 6:3 says God was not known to the patriarchs by the name Yahweh, though Genesis uses it 165 times. The New Testament even calls Abram "Abraham" before his name was changed, and Paul and James alter the Septuagint's "Abram believed God" to "Abraham believed God." So when the New Testament says Isaiah saw Jesus' glory, it describes the event in the language of its own day; it does not tell us what Isaiah saw or understood.

On the texts themselves (ch. 11):

- **John 8:56** says nothing about a theophany; Abraham saw Jesus' "day," which may mean his joy at Isaac's birth, prophetic foresight, or his present life with God.
- **John 12:41** concerns glory, which in John the Son always shares with the Father. Acts 28:25–27 attributes the same vision's words to the Holy Spirit, and in Revelation 4 the throne is occupied by the Father. Calvin judged that "it is wrong to restrict this vision to the person of Christ, since the prophecy refers rather to God without any differentiation," and Theodore of Mopsuestia that Isaiah "saw the glory that is common to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit."
- **1 Corinthians 10** draws on a Jewish legend of a rock that followed Israel. In Exodus 17:6 Yahweh stands *on* the rock rather than being it, and a "spiritual rock" is not a human-form appearance. Paul is adding Christ to the identity of Israel's God (as with the Shema in 1 Cor. 8:6), not replacing God with him.
- **Jude 5** is a textually uncertain word in an unclear verse.

Malone also turns the method around. If New Testament statements settle Old Testament events, other New Testament texts suggest the Son appeared only at the incarnation: Christ was "revealed in these last times" (1 Pet. 1:20), and "now He has appeared one time, at the end of the ages" (Heb. 9:26 HCSB), his only other appearance being his return "a second time" (9:28). When Jesus cites the burning bush, a key Angel passage, he calls the speaker "God" (Mark 12:26). And every New Testament throne scene seats the Father, not the Son, on the throne. On the hermeneutical point he quotes J. I. Packer: "The Spirit's distinct personhood can, and according to the NT should, be read into the OT, but cannot be read out of it" (ch. 11).

### 5. Why It Matters to Malone

Malone names two motives (ch. 12). The lesser is soteriological. Some advocates claim the Old Testament saints had conscious faith in Christ. Paul Blackham writes that "Moses is an apostle of Jesus Christ – the gospel preached by Moses is exactly the same gospel preached by the New Testament apostles," and Hanson that "Abraham saw Christ, and was justified, like all Christians, through faith in Christ." Christophanies are recruited against "inclusivism," the view that people such as Rahab or Naaman were saved without explicit faith in Jesus. Malone shares the concern but thinks it needs a better defence than a weak argument.

The greater motive is reading the Bible well. Everyone, he admits, massages texts to fit their theology, and that includes him. His example of an orthodox conclusion argued badly is the claim, repeated by Pink, Lloyd-Jones, Ryrie, Morris, Packer, Carson, Frame and Piper, that John's masculine pronouns for the Spirit break Greek grammar and so prove the Spirit is a person. All of them "have been looking at the wrong word." He ends with four things the christophany case would need: a reason why invisibility applies to the Father alone and to sight alone; a single Angel shown to be distinct from the Lord; New Testament language shown to match Old Testament experience; and evidence that the Father and Spirit abstained from appearing. Until then, he concludes, "it better honours the Trinity and the text of Scripture to allow that the Father and the Spirit are themselves involved in Old Testament theophanies."

### 6. Hamilton: A Representation, Not a Representative

Victor Hamilton, commenting on the first Angel story (Gen. 16), reaches Malone's Candidate 1 from the exegesis alone. He rejects Gunkel's old theory that the Angel was inserted to soften crude appearances of God, because "the introduction of the angel in the narratives is too spasmodic and leaves other bold anthropomorphisms untouched": "the angel is the accompaniment of the deity's anthropomorphic appearance, rather than being a dilution of it." He counts 58 uses of "angel of Yahweh" and eleven of "angel of God," and lists eight stories that both equate the Angel with God and seem to separate them. His conclusion:

> It is clear from the above that the angel of Yahweh is a visible manifestation (either in human form or in fiery form) of Yahweh that is essentially indistinguishable from Yahweh himself. The angel of Yahweh is more a representation of God than a representative of God. (on 16:7)

He quotes R. North: *mal'āk* "means representation, as a 'presence' or manner of rendering oneself present, rather than the specific form of this representative which is messenger."

### 7. The Orthodox Answer: Ouspensky and the Icon of the Father

Malone writes as an evangelical and does not engage the Eastern tradition, which holds a christophany reading for reasons rooted in its theology of the image. Leonid Ouspensky's *Theology of the Icon* sets this out in a chapter on the Great Council of Moscow (1666–67), which forbade icons of God the Father.

The starting point is the Seventh Ecumenical Council (787), which quoted Pope Gregory II's letter to the iconoclast emperor Leo III: "Why do we neither describe nor represent the Father of the Lord Jesus Christ? Because we do not know what He is ... And if we had seen and known Him as we have seen and known His Son, we would have tried to describe Him and to represent Him in art" (p. 154). Christ can be painted because he became visible in the flesh; the Father, who did not, cannot. John of Damascus: "I boldly draw an image of the invisible God, not as invisible, but as having become visible for our sakes by partaking of flesh and blood" (p. 379).

From the seventeenth century, however, Russian and Greek icons often showed the Father as a white-haired old man, labelled "Sabaoth" and justified by Isaiah's "my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts" (Isa. 6:5) and Daniel's "Ancient of Days" with hair "like pure wool" (Dan. 7:9). The Moscow Council condemned this:

> for no one has ever seen the Lord Sabaoth (that is, God the Father) in the flesh. Only Christ was seen in the flesh ... To paint on icons the Lord Sabaoth (that is, the Father) with a white beard, holding the only-begotten Son in his lap with a dove between them is altogether absurd and improper, for no one has ever seen the Father in His divinity. (p. 371)

The Council added that "Sabaoth is not the name of the Father only, but of the Holy Trinity," and that Daniel's Ancient of Days "is not taken to mean the Father, but the Son at His Second Coming" (p. 372).

Ouspensky defends the Council from the Fathers and the liturgy. John of Damascus reads the Old Testament sightings this way:

> And Adam saw God ... Jacob saw and struggled with God ... Daniel saw the likeness of a man, and one like a son of man coming before the Ancient of Days. No one saw the divine nature, but the image and figure of what was yet to come. For the invisible Son and Word of God was to become truly man. (*On the Divine Images* 3.26, p. 375)

The hymns for the feast of the Meeting of the Lord sing, "You have been seen by the prophets, Jesus, as much as it was possible to see You then," and call the infant Christ "the Ancient of Days who formerly gave the law to Moses." Ouspensky states flatly that "There is not one liturgical text that ascribes the prophetic visions or the title 'Ancient of Days' to God the Father" (pp. 375–76). Cyril of Alexandria explained the Son of Man coming to the Ancient of Days as "the Son attained the glory of the Father." Hilary of Poitiers wrote: "the one who has appeared is the true God. But let no one pretend he has seen God the Father." Ouspensky notes one patristic exception, Hippolytus, for whom "the Ancient of Days is none other than God and the Lord of all things, the Father of Christ Himself" (p. 375 n. 9).

His conclusion is a christophany reading, but a carefully qualified one:

> there is no direct revelation of the Divinity in the Old Testament, and all the prophetic visions are visions not of the nature or of the divine Persons, but of the glory of the Divinity ... the anthropomorphic image of the Godhead, the beholding of His glory by the Old Testament prophets, can only refer to Christ, either in the context of the Incarnation or in that of the Second Coming. (pp. 376–77)

Two qualifications matter for the comparison with Malone. First, for Ouspensky the prophets saw *glory*, which is "common to the three Persons, Father, Son, and Spirit," but "manifested by the One who was to become man" (p. 377). This is close to Malone's own reading of John 12:41 and Theodore of Mopsuestia. Second, Ouspensky does not claim the Son appeared in his divinity before the incarnation; Theodore the Studite called the attempt to circumscribe "the unincarnate Word" in space "idolatry" (p. 379). The appearances are prefigurations: "the image and figure of what was yet to come." Nor was the tradition unanimous. Ouspensky records that St Nicodemus the Hagiorite defended icons of the Father as the Ancient of Days, and that Sergius Bulgakov thought such icons "legitimated through ... accepted use in the Church" (pp. 373, 378 n. 13).

### 8. Assessment

Malone's book is a model of a certain kind of argument: it does not deny that christophanies happened but shows that the usual evidence does not establish them. On his own ground he is strong. The invisibility argument does sit badly with the Old Testament, and the linguistic case that *aoratos* means "unseen" and that John's "seeing" is recognition is sound. His best single point is the demand that advocates of Candidate 3 prove distinction as well as divinity; most of the literature he surveys proves only the second, which in fact supports Candidate 1. His use of Meier on ancient messengers seriously damages the Augustinian view, and the observation that the Spirit fits the "sent" evidence as well as the Son is a fair challenge that christophany writers rarely meet. His reminder that the New Testament describes the past in its own vocabulary is basic hermeneutics, but it does cut against readers who move straight from "Isaiah saw his glory" to a claim about what Isaiah saw.

His weaknesses are of two kinds. First, his "relative superlative" readings of 1 Timothy 6:16 and John 1:18 work hard: "whom no one has seen or can see" is a strong phrase to read as rhetoric, and a reader may suspect he is doing to the New Testament what he accuses others of doing to the Old. Second, and more important, he frames the debate entirely within modern evangelical exegesis. He treats the patristic christophany reading as the product of Justin's Platonism and apologetic agenda, but he does not engage the tradition that developed it most fully, in which the reason for reading theophanies christologically is not a Platonic dislike of matter but the logic of the incarnation and the image.

That is where Ouspensky matters. The Orthodox reading answers Malone's "decisive question" (what is the difference between Father and Son that makes one seen and the other not?) directly: the Son is the one who became incarnate, and the Old Testament visions are "the image and figure of what was yet to come." This is not Borland's claim that the Son appeared *instead of* the Father. It is closer to Malone's second sense of christophany, in which a common divine glory is shown through the person who would later take flesh. On that reading the two positions are not as far apart as they first look: both reject the idea of a reclusive Father and an errand-running Son, and both locate what the prophets saw in God's glory. The real disagreement is whether the Old Testament visions point forward specifically to the incarnate Son (the liturgy says yes) or to God undifferentiated (Malone says that is all the text allows). That is a question about how far the Church's later reading belongs to the meaning of the text, and Malone's own principle, borrowed from Packer, that some things can be read *into* the Old Testament but not *out of* it, gives the Orthodox reading more room than his conclusion suggests.

Your notes [[The Angel of the Lord]] and [[Jesus in the OT as Theophany]] state the christophany reading without argument. After Malone they would need to say which sense of christophany they mean and on what grounds; after Ouspensky, they could ground it in the theology of the icon and the liturgy rather than in the Father's invisibility or the Angel's title, which are the two weakest supports.

### Conclusion

Malone shows that the three standard arguments for Old Testament christophanies are each weaker than their popularity suggests. The Old Testament says plainly that God was seen; the New Testament's "invisible" is better read as "unseen"; the Angel of the Lord behaves like God rather than like any known messenger, and nothing in the text singles him out as the Son rather than the Father or Spirit; and the New Testament texts add the Son to Israel's God rather than substituting him for the Father. His own conclusion is modest: the Angel is God, appearing incognito, and the whole Trinity was at work in the theophanies. Hamilton's commentary arrives at the same place from Genesis. The Orthodox tradition, as Ouspensky presents it, reads the same evidence christologically, but on different grounds: not because the Father cannot be seen, but because the only image of God the Church admits is the one who became flesh, and the prophets saw his glory "as much as it was possible to see You then."
