## 1. Dynamic vs. Static

- **Thomism**: God is pure act—completely actualized, with no change, becoming, or potentiality. His essence and existence are identical, and He cannot change or manifest in the way creatures do.
- **Gregory of Nyssa**: God’s essence is unchanging, but His energies (manifested through dunamis) are active in creation, leading to theosis. God’s energies are dynamic, facilitating relationship with creation without implying change in God's essence.

## 2. Potentiality in Gregory

- **Aquinas**: God’s pure act means He is without potentiality—completely complete and immutable, with no capacity to change or become other than He is.
- **Gregory**: Dunamis refers to God's power and capacity to act through His energies in creation, drawing creatures into union with Him. It does not imply potentiality in the sense of becoming, but emphasizes active divine engagement in the world.

## 3. Theological Implication

- **Thomism**: Pure act makes God absolutely transcendent and immutable. There is no process of becoming or transformation in God.
- **Gregory**: God’s energies express His will and power in creation, while His essence remains immutable. This allows for a relational understanding of God’s interaction with creation, leading to theosis, but without changing God.

## 4. The Word-Concept Fallacy

- **Gregory’s Dunamis**: Refers to divine power and capacity actively expressed through creation but does not imply a static, unchanging nature for God.
- **Thomistic Pure Act**: Refers to an unchanging state of complete actuality, where there is no potentiality or becoming, emphasizing God’s static, unchanging nature.

One of the word-concept fallacies that many make is assuming that because both dunamis and pure act can be translated into English as "power" or "potential," they are identical in meaning and theological implications. This is not the case:

- The term **dunamis** in Gregory of Nyssa refers to divine power or energy, which is always actualized through God's actions in creation. However, it does not imply that God has potentiality in the sense of change or becoming. Gregory emphasizes that God's essence remains unchanging, while His energies are actively engaged in creation, facilitating the process of theosis.
- The concept of **pure act** in Thomism refers specifically to an unchanging state of complete actuality, where no potential remains for God to be other than He is. This means there is no becoming, no unfolding, or even an expression of power outside the divine essence.

This confusion arises because both terms describe aspects of divine perfection, but the metaphysical understanding behind each term is fundamentally different.

## 5. Conclusion

St. Gregory of Nyssa does not teach the Thomistic concept of pure act. Gregory’s understanding of dunamis emphasizes God’s active engagement with creation through His energies, facilitating theosis, while maintaining His immutable essence. This contrasts with Aquinas' static notion of pure act, where God cannot change or manifest in the world as Gregory teaches.

Gregory’s use of dunamis refers to the active manifestation of God's divine energies in creation, facilitating theosis and allowing creatures to participate in the divine. This is a relational and dynamic understanding of God's engagement with creation, where His immutable essence remains unchanged while His energies interact with the world. This is distinct from the static, metaphysical notion of pure act found in Thomism.
