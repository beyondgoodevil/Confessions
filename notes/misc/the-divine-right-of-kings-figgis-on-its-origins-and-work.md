Figgis wrote this book as a Cambridge prize essay in 1892 and published it, rewritten, in 1896. It opens by agreeing with the common judgment that "never has there been a doctrine better written against than the Divine Right of Kings" (p. 1), and then complains that its critics "have commonly been at little pains to understand it." The book is an attempt to understand it: where it came from, what it was for, why it held so many minds for two centuries, and why it then faded.

His answer is that the doctrine was a weapon. It was made to meet the claim of the Pope, and later of the Presbyterian Kirk, to rule over states by divine right. The only effective reply to a claim of right was a counter-claim of right, and in the sixteenth century only a king could make it. Seen this way, the theory was the popular form taken by the idea of sovereignty, and its believers were, on several points, nearer to modern political science than the Whigs who mocked them.

The note follows the book. Sections 1–2 cover the definition and the medieval background. Sections 3–9 are the history, from Pope and Emperor to the Jacobites. Sections 10–13 are Figgis's analysis, and section 14 his conclusion.

### 1. The four propositions

Figgis sets out the doctrine in four propositions (pp. 5–6):

1. **Monarchy is a divinely ordained institution.**
2. **Hereditary right is indefeasible.** Succession goes by primogeniture and cannot be lost by usurpation, "even though the usurping dynasty has reigned for a thousand years."
3. **Kings are accountable to God alone.** "A mixed or limited monarchy is a contradiction in terms."
4. **Non-resistance and passive obedience are enjoined by God.** Resistance to a king is always a sin and "ensures damnation". If he commands what God forbids, God is to be obeyed, but on the model of the first Christians the penalty must be patiently endured.

He gives the texts behind them: Samuel's warning about kings, "By me kings reign" in Proverbs, the dream of Nebuchadnezzar in Daniel, "Render unto Caesar", Christ's word to Pilate that his power was "given thee from above", Romans 13, and 1 Peter 2. David's refusal to touch "the Lord's anointed" and his "Against thee only have I sinned" were read as proof that a king answers to God and no one else (pp. 7–8).

Two things are often treated as part of the doctrine that were not. The patriarchal theory, which made the king the heir of Adam's fatherly power, was "merely the best argument" for it and came late (p. 148). Unction was not essential either. Richard II believed that "Not all the water in the rude rough sea" could wash off the balm, but when the League argued that Henry IV of France could not be king without anointing at Rheims, royalists played unction down. One English tract says that "Royal Unction confers no grace, but declares a just title only" (p. 10).

The doctrine was popular, not academic. It was "proclaimed in the pulpit, published in the market-place, witnessed on the battle-field" (p. 3). Its writers did not aim to convert other nations, and each looked only at his own country. Figgis's method follows from that: "The theory is the outcome of facts far more than it is of thinking" (p. 5), and "facts are the parents of theories far more than theories of facts" (p. 16).

### 2. Kingship before the theory

Early English kingship was elective within the royal family. The Witan chose among the king's kin, and coronation, not birth, made the king (pp. 20–21). Three things turned this into a birthright.

- **Feudal land law.** The king became the supreme landowner, and the crown came to be handled like a fief. Fiefs were passing to the eldest son undivided, and so did the kingdom (pp. 22–24). When John took the throne over his nephew Arthur, the rule was not yet settled. Edward I was on crusade when his father died. The barons elected him four days later, he reigned from that date, and he was not crowned for nearly two years, so coronation stopped being needed to make a king (pp. 26–27). Edward II succeeded with no interregnum at all: "The Crown has become a birthright" (p. 27).
- **The distinction between person and crown.** It appears in the case against Gaveston under Edward II. The doctrine that homage and allegiance are owed to the crown more than to the person, so that subjects may guide the king back to reason by force, was condemned at the trial of the Despensers. Coke later called it a "damnable and damned opinion". But the idea that one could fight the king's person in defence of his office came back in the Civil War (pp. 29–30).
- **Bracton.** The thirteenth-century lawyer said the king is "under no one but God" and also under the law. For him there was no contradiction, because custom could rule without any lawgiver above it. Seventeenth-century pamphleteers read him as making the king above the law, and quoted him "only less frequently than the Bible" (p. 33). Filmer said the king is under "the directive, but not the coactive power" of the laws. Bracton's line that "only God can make an heir" (p. 36) treated birth as a judgment of God, like the ordeal or the lot.

There was still no theory of sovereignty in medieval England. Custom ruled, feudal rights were divided, and nobody asked who had the last word (pp. 30–31). The question came from elsewhere.

### 3. Pope and Emperor

The ideal of the Holy Roman Empire was one Christian society with two heads, Pope and Emperor, under Christ as the real king. It could not survive once each head claimed to be over the other (pp. 38–41). Figgis's main historical claim is that the Papacy built the first theory of sovereignty by divine right, and the doctrine of the divine right of kings was the reply.

The canonists argued from the Donation of Constantine, the Pope's transfer of the Empire from the Greeks to the Franks, the sun and moon as the greater and lesser lights, and the two swords. Innocent III told the Emperor at Constantinople that the priesthood was set "over the nations and over the kingdoms" (Jeremiah 1:10). Boniface VIII's bull *Unam Sanctam* (1302) put it most fully: a body with two heads is a monster; the temporal power is subject to the spiritual; the supreme spiritual power is judged by God alone; and "Whoso resisteth the power" in Romans 13 now meant the Pope (pp. 47–50). Figgis calls this "the exact converse of the theory of the Divine Right of Kings" (p. 51). It was, like the royal doctrine later, a theory of obedience and not of liberty.

The imperialists answered with the same tools. Unity is "the soul of government", and a divided sovereignty is anarchy. Marsilius of Padua in the *Defensor Pacis*, Ockham in the *Dialogus* and Dante in the *De Monarchia* argued that the Empire comes straight from God. Dante saw the electors not as choosing the Emperor "but as merely announcing God's choice" (pp. 55–63). John of Jandun put it in one line: "Potestas imperialis est immediate a Deo, non a Papa", imperial power is directly from God, not from the Pope (p. 65). Marsilius was the most modern of them, with his representative people and his near-toleration, but he was weaker in controversy, because his case rested on utility and not on right (pp. 60–62).

The Emperor's claim to lordship of the world held national sovereignty back for a time. The Papacy then weakened the Emperor so far that kings could take over his rights. Richard II was called "Entier Emperour de son Roialme", and Henry VIII's Statute of Appeals declared that "this realm of England is an Empire" (pp. 42–44). On this account the divine right of secular governments to be free of papal control took shape in the fourteenth century as the divine right of emperors, and came back in the sixteenth and seventeenth as the divine right of kings (pp. 44–45).

### 4. Wycliffe and Richard II

Wycliffe's *De Officio Regis* is the first English statement of the doctrine (pp. 67–72). The king is God's vicar in temporal things. He reflects the godhead of Christ and the priest his manhood. Christ cleansing the temple was an act of a king, and his crucifixion an act of a priest. Following Augustine, Adam was the first king and Cain the first priest. The king is above the laws and keeps them of his own will.

Wycliffe spoiled the case with his doctrine that dominion is founded on grace. A tyrant has power but not true lordship, so in God's cause he may be resisted. That conceded the Pope's case, since someone must judge whether a ruler is in grace, and that someone would be the Church. Figgis notes that Wycliffe's view is Bellarmine's with the terms changed. The lesson he draws is that there was "no completely effective method of meeting the Papal claim to political supremacy save by a theory of absolute non-resistance and Divine Right" (p. 72).

Richard II acted the theory out. The Statute of Praemunire said the crown was "in no earthly subjection". The articles of his deposition charged that he said *leges suae erant in ore suo, et aliquotiens in pectore suo*, his laws were in his mouth and sometimes in his breast (p. 75). At the Shrewsbury Parliament of 1398 a committee was given the powers of Parliament, which Figgis reads as an attempt at a written *lex regia*. The Bishop of Exeter preached on Ezekiel's *Rex unus erit omnibus* and argued that a mixed monarchy is anarchy (p. 77). Richard also believed that unction gave sacramental grace (p. 79). His accession as a boy, over his uncles, was the triumph of strict representative primogeniture (pp. 79–80).

### 5. Tudor obedience

The revolution of 1399 was constitutional, but Henry IV still felt he needed a fiction about descent from Edmund Crouchback, which shows how strong the hereditary idea had become (p. 81). With Richard of York, "the notion of indefeasible hereditary right first appears in English history" (p. 82). Richard of York claimed the crown as right heir "as it accordeth with God's law and all natural laws", and the judges declined to rule because the matter was "above the law, and passed their learning" (p. 83). Henry VIII's naming of his successors by statute and by will put royal power above legitimism (pp. 85–86). Elizabeth's title was doubtful, and while Mary Stuart lived the claims of strict heredity could not be pressed. James I had nothing but his birth (pp. 86–88).

The Reformation made the theory necessary. The Act of Supremacy created a new prerogative, and the papal claim had to be answered by a single, visible authority. "Only under the form of monarchy does the notion of sovereignty readily lend itself to popular exposition" (p. 91). Figgis concludes that "a doctrine of sovereignty vested by Divine Right in the king was the indispensable handmaid of a national Reformation" (p. 92).

What Tudor writers stressed was obedience, not a theory of government (p. 93). They did not repeat Wycliffe's mistake of leaving room for resistance.

- **Tyndale**, *The Obedience of a Christian Man* (1528), argued that the Pope was the real anarchist, under whom "kings are but shadows, vain names and things idle" (p. 94).
- **Gardiner**, *De Vera Obedientia* (1535), saw that the real question was the limits of obedience in extreme cases, and denied that Scripture set any. He called the two-lights argument a "blind distinction and full of darkness" and held that the prince is "the whole prince of all the people and not of part" (pp. 94–95).
- **The Homilies** on obedience under Edward VI and against wilful rebellion under Elizabeth taught non-resistance as religion. Figgis grants the later Whig point that they spoke of obedience to law and gave no guidance for a king like James II who broke it (p. 96).
- **The 1535 translation of Marsilius** left out the chapter on restraining a bad prince, as not "pertaining to this realm of England" (p. 97).
- **Bilson**, *The True Difference* (1585), covered the historical ground: the relations of Popes and Emperors from Constantine to Justinian. Figgis explains why such dusty history mattered. National independence could not rest on fact alone. "The Pope claimed a Divine Right for his position, and this could only be met by a counter claim not of fact, but of right" (p. 97).

The turning point was Pius V's bull of 1570, which excommunicated Elizabeth and released her subjects. From then on there was a rival claimant "across the water" and a papal command to treason, and every loyal Englishman had to hold that his queen reigned by divine right (p. 100).

The Catholic side, meanwhile, discovered popular rights. The Jesuit Parsons, writing as Doleman (1594), argued that "Propinquity of birth or blood alone" gave no right to a crown, that the commonwealth could choose and change its government, and that it could remove a ruler who broke the conditions of his office (pp. 101–102). Figgis stresses that these doctrines were "proclaimed purely in the interests of the Papal monarchy, without the smallest enthusiasm for liberty" (p. 102). In an irony he enjoys, the book was reprinted by the Puritans in 1647 and by the Whig supporters of the Exclusion Bill. A tract written to keep a Protestant off the throne nearly kept a Catholic off it instead (p. 103). Doleman, Bellarmine and Suarez became "the bêtes noires of Anglican divines" (p. 103).

His summary of the century is that English writers, answering the papal claim, produced a doctrine "in its essential meaning no other than the Imperialist theory of two centuries and a half before" (p. 105).

### 6. France and the Salic law

The French Wars of Religion rehearsed the English quarrels of the next century. Figgis draws three parallels. Huguenots and Leaguers both claimed the right to make war on the king for God, and "the Presbyterian and the Papal theories of politics are at bottom identical" (p. 107), since each would put the state under a church. The League's war on Henry III in the name of the crown matched the Long Parliament's war on Charles I. And Henry IV's winning of the throne with almost nothing but his birth was "the supreme triumph of legitimism" (pp. 107–108).

The French version differed in one way. Henry IV was not the heir by primogeniture. By the English rule the crown would have gone through a woman to the Spanish Infanta. His right came from the Salic law, which excluded women, and the Salic law was plainly a human institution. So French theory was "less theological, less transcendental, more legal and local than English" (p. 109). It could not claim that nature had made its rule of succession. Barclay accepted Bellarmine's line that keeping the true faith is of divine law but having this or that king is of human law, and added that once we have a king, obeying him is of divine law (p. 121). The Salic law also carried national pride. Without it, Edward III would have been the lawful king of France, and a French writer could call it the salvation of the country (p. 122).

The opponents of the crown produced the ideas that ran through all later theories of popular rights.

- **The *Vindiciae contra Tyrannos*** sets out the original compact "full-blown" (p. 112). It has two contracts: one between God and king and people together, which later writers dropped, and one between king and people, which is Locke's. Its argument rests on utility: free men would never have given a king power over their lives and goods. Like Locke, it thinks it can "put a hook into the nose" of Leviathan (p. 113). Yet it forbids private men to resist. Only the officers and corporate bodies of the realm may do so (pp. 114–115), and Figgis jokes that on this view a county council could lawfully lead a rising against the vaccination acts.
- **Hotman's *Franco-Gallia*** is a historical argument that the Frankish kingdom began as a limited monarchy, and that whatever powers the people once had they still have. Here, as in the English Whigs, constitutional arrangements are a "fundamental law" that time cannot change (pp. 116–117).

After the death of the Duke of Alençon in 1584 left the Protestant Henry of Navarre as heir, the Huguenot ideas passed to the Catholic League (p. 118). The royalist answer was Gallican. Kings are of divine appointment. The Pope cannot take away what he never gave. The deposition of the Merovingian Childeric in 752, the papalists' favourite precedent, was explained away (p. 119). But French Catholics granted the Pope's spiritual authority, so they were less clear than the English on the unity of sovereignty. Barclay was willing to call papal and royal power equal (p. 120).

Bodin stands to the French royalists as Hobbes to the English, but Figgis rates him higher. A mixed state, Bodin said, has never existed and cannot even be imagined, "attendu que les marques de souveraineté sont indivisibles" (p. 124). He defined law as the sovereign's command, and avoided Hobbes's mistake of founding sovereignty on a contract. "In depth and accuracy of thought his treatise far surpasses the Leviathan" (p. 125). He was translated, used as a text-book at Cambridge, and read by Hobbes, Filmer and Leslie (pp. 127–128).

Two Scots in France linked the French debate to England. Blackwood's *De Vinculo Religionis et Imperii* argued that Calvinism must be false because it teaches resistance, in the same way that Anglicans argued Rome must be false for the same reason. Its real aim was "to assert the political necessity of persecution" (pp. 130–131). His *Apologia pro Regibus* against Buchanan says that kings are heirs not of kings but of the kingdom. Barclay's *De Regno* answered Buchanan, the *Vindiciae* and the Leaguer Boucher together (p. 129). Both influenced James VI, and Filmer named them as his forerunners. Still, Figgis judges that the English theory was "a plant of indigenous growth" (p. 127).

### 7. From James I to the Civil War

James had every reason to hold the strictest form of the doctrine (pp. 135–136). His English title, barred by two Acts of Parliament, rested on descent alone. The Catholic writers attacked it. And in Scotland he had been lectured and thwarted by Presbyterian ministers whose clericalism was "more irritating and meddlesome" than the Pope's. In *The True Law of Free Monarchies* (1598) the doctrine appears "complete in every detail" (p. 136). Parliament's act on his accession did not give him the crown but recognised that it came to him "by inherent birthright" (p. 136).

The country did not yet share his clarity. Overall's Convocation Book taught that a government set up by successful rebels has God's authority. James wrote angrily to Archbishop Abbot that on this principle a Spanish conquest would be lawful (pp. 137–138). Parliament resented his claim that its privileges were royal grants, and Coke refused his wish to sit as a judge. Laud thought Mainwaring's royalist sermons of 1628 unwise to publish (p. 139).

The Long Parliament made the doctrine a popular force. The Canons of 1640 declared that "The most high and sacred order of kings is of Divine Right", that any "independent coactive power, either papal or popular" was treason against God, and that subjects who bore arms against the king, even in defence, would "receive to themselves damnation" (pp. 140–141). The Canons say nothing of hereditary right, because Charles's title was not in question. Figgis's general rule is that "dogma never takes definite shape, save as a result of its denial" (p. 141). Passive obedience became a passion after the Civil War showed what resistance cost. The distinction between rulers *de facto* and *de jure* came from the killing of Charles and the exclusion of his heir. Cromwell's army taught royalists that a usurper has no moral claim, and *Eikon Basilike* gave the cause a martyr (pp. 141–142). So the theory's origin is Tudor, but its "widespread prevalence was certainly due rather to the Civil War than to any more remote causes" (p. 143).

Figgis puts its value here, against the Whig historians. It bore witness "to the need of continuity in national life and to the paramount importance to a State of a law-abiding habit" (p. 144). He credits it with the unusually orderly course of English constitutional history.

### 8. Filmer and the turn to nature

Filmer's *Patriarcha*, published in 1680 at the height of the Exclusion crisis, changed the method of the argument (pp. 146–154). Earlier writers piled up texts and added, as an ornament, that kingship is natural, as in families or flocks of geese. Filmer reversed this. He argued that kingship is natural, and therefore ordained by God, the author of nature. His whole case is the identity of the kingdom with the family and of royal with fatherly power. This made the *Patriarcha* "the only rational system of absolutist politics" (p. 147). Figgis reduces it to a syllogism (p. 153):

> What is natural to man exists by Divine Right.
> Kingship is natural to man.
> Therefore Kingship exists by Divine Right.

It looked stronger than the old method and was weaker. A wall of texts could only be met by denying the reading. "The arguments drawn from isolated texts seem to modern readers the most absurd part of the theory of Divine Right. They are in reality the strength of the position" (p. 154). Once the question was what is natural, anyone could argue, as Locke did, that self-preservation and utility are natural too. Filmer was "the herald of its decadence" (p. 150). "For direct Divine Right has been substituted a constructive theory of Divine approval" (p. 150), and in this sense Filmer "paved the way not only for Locke, but for Rousseau."

Figgis argues that Filmer and Locke shared one standpoint (pp. 155–158). Both believed in a state of nature and drew inalienable rights from it. For Filmer there was one natural right, the father's, and "All men are born slaves". For Locke all are born free and equal. Filmer saw what Locke did not, that political society is natural and necessary and not a thing men choose. He also read Genesis as the only authentic record of early society, which gave his theory "the pregnant truth that the state is an organism not a machine" (p. 155). But the gap between Adam and modern kings was fatal, and Filmer could bridge it only by saying that possession gives the best right where no better claimant is known. Locke's system is "as much more unhistorical in its basis, as it is more reasonable in its conclusions" (p. 158).

The general lesson is that the natural rights of the Whigs were the old doctrine in new dress. Both came from the wish to find "some immutable basis for politics" above expediency. "A universal theory of the state is a chimera", and Burke's arguments against the French revolutionaries apply as well to Bellarmine and Filmer. "The theory of natural rights is the old theory of Divine Right disguised" (p. 151).

### 9. After 1688

Nalson's *Common Interest of King and People* (1678) went further. It opened with self-preservation and the desire for happiness and "might have been written by Bentham" (p. 161). This gave the case away. No crowd would believe that keeping an oppressive king was useful. "If acuter minds have come to the conclusion that a revolution is always inexpedient, the only method of making their opinion practically effective will be by inducing the vulgar to believe that it is always iniquitous" (p. 162). The old theology had this advantage: "The more foolish it is from a common-sense point of view, the more clearly is it the duty of those who look beyond this world" (p. 163).

The Revolution and the Act of Settlement ended indefeasible hereditary right in practice, and the danger from Rome that the theory was made to meet was gone. What remained was feeling (pp. 164–170). The nonjurors drew on the past, and the cause of the "king over the water" gathered every love of what was old and losing. "To the one Divine Right was a force of practical value ... To the other it was a memory and a vain regret" (p. 164). "From a practical force it has become a romantic sentiment" (p. 166), alive in Jacobite songs and the novels of Scott, the feeling that "touches all dying causes with a sunset charm" (p. 168). The divine right of the Stuarts became "the symbol and the sacrament of the contrast between right and might" (p. 169).

The clever Tories despised it. Bolingbroke wrote that "a divine right to govern ill is an absurdity; to assert it blasphemy" (p. 168), which Figgis thinks misses the point entirely. Yet the Sacheverell trial, the plots and the risings of 1715 and 1745 show it was not dead. It caused the only High Church schism in English history (p. 170).

The last stage was Berkeley's *Passive Obedience* (1712), which said nothing of heredity and argued from natural law: obedience to the supreme power is a law of nature, and a law of nature has no exceptions (pp. 171–172). Bishop Butler kept only a residue. Government is part of the course of nature and so divine, reverence for it rests on its being God's ordinance, and the exceptions should be "as little brought to mind as may be". The doctrine had been "whittled down to a few harmless truisms" (pp. 172–173). Its legacy was the sense "that government in general is divine, because it is natural, and that obedience to law is a religious duty" (p. 174).

### 10. Against Pope and Presbytery

Chapter VIII turns from history to analysis. The phrase "Divine Right of Kings" is mostly negative (p. 175). Every theory of the age claimed divine right for something: the Pope, the Presbytery, the people of the *Vindiciae*, Sidney's inalienable popular sovereignty. The royal doctrine differed "not ... in alone claiming Divine Right for the supreme authority, but in claiming that the king is the supreme authority" (p. 176). If it was the stupidest theory of politics, that cannot be because it gave government a divine sanction, since all its rivals did the same.

Its enemy was clericalism. "The royalist authors have the Pope on the brain" (p. 177). Sermons on the anniversary of Charles I's execution blamed it on the Jesuits. The *Patriarcha* opens against Bellarmine. Even Hobbes gave a whole part of *Leviathan* to the "Kingdom of Darkness". Dissenters were called Jesuits, and Figgis insists this was not loose abuse. The royalists believed the essence of popery was the claim to release subjects from allegiance. Filmer said "the main and indeed the only point of Popery is the alienating and withdrawing of subjects from their obedience to their Prince", and Hickes called resisting Protestants "Papists under a Protestant dress, wolves in sheeps' clothing" (p. 182). So the hatred of the Jesuits was "rather political than religious" (p. 179), the anger of patriots and not the moral indignation of Pascal (p. 181). Figgis adds in a note that Suarez and Mariana, the Jesuit theorists of popular sovereignty, were "vastly superior in lucidity and grasp to Locke and Sidney" (p. 183).

The charge against Presbyterianism was even more just (pp. 185–197). Cartwright made the magistrate the Church's servant, who must "throw down their crowns" before it and punish at its direction (p. 189). Goodman, writing against Mary Tudor in 1558, made the Church's view of idolatry the test of every ruler's title, and condemned her alliance with Spain as a breach of God's word (pp. 190–191). The Second Book of Discipline claimed for the Kirk an indirect temporal power much like Bellarmine's: the ministers do not hold the civil sword, but they "teach the magistrate how it should be exercised" (p. 193). The magistrate became "the mere tenant-at-will of the Kirk" (p. 194). The General Assembly of 1582 demanded that no league be made with Catholic powers (p. 196).

Figgis judges this worse than Rome. The Papacy "was at least a standing witness to the need of international morality" and saw politics from a universal height. A Presbyterian state would have had "most of the defects without any of the merits of clericalism" (pp. 186–187), more searching, more local and more narrow. "New presbyter is but old priest writ large." He also answers the defence that the Kirk claimed no sword of its own. Neither did the medieval Pope, whose rule was "emphatically an instance of government by consent" (p. 198). What both claimed was the power to dissolve the bond of conscience between ruler and subject. The doctrine of divine right answered exactly that. It asserted "that the State has a claim to obedience on moral and religious grounds, that it has a right to exist as in accordance with human nature and God's will, and is based on something better than the right of the stronger" (p. 200).

This explains the clergy's part. The Caroline divines' talk of the king's power over doctrine sounds Erastian, but it claimed no more than legal omnipotence, the same omnipotence by which Parliament could in law abolish Christianity tomorrow (p. 201). Laud knew that the supremacy over the Church could only change hands, and that with Charles it protected the Church from the Puritans (p. 202). So the theory was not "the fiction of a time-serving hierarchy" (p. 203). It was anti-clerical, and yet it had to be made by clergy, because "None but the clergy could meet the Pope on his own ground" (p. 205). The Jesuits were right that only a spiritual authority could say whether men were bound in conscience to obey. They were wrong that the state had no moral claim apart from the orthodoxy of its ruler (p. 204).

Passive obedience shows the doctrine was religious. A purely political absolutism, like *Leviathan*, simply forbids resistance. A Christian one has to face martyrdom, and so must tell men what to do when the king persecutes the true faith (pp. 206–207). That case arrived with James II, who tried to use the anti-papal weapon for the Pope. "What wonder that the sword broke in his hands!" (p. 209). The clergy who turned against him were loyal to the purpose of their doctrine, if not to its letter.

### 11. What the clerical side won

Figgis does not leave it there. The claims of Pope and Presbyter also did necessary work. In an age when every state enforced one religion, a church claiming authority over the state was "the only form possible to those times" of the rights of conscience (p. 212). If the state is all-powerful and persecution is a duty, a theologian-king can impose a heresy. Better that the power with the sword should take its view of truth from the power without one than that the state should prescribe religion on its own.

He sets out three stages (pp. 213–214):

1. **The state makes its own religion**, as when Rome required worship of the Emperor.
2. **The state takes its religion from the Church** and still persecutes at its direction. This contains the germ of toleration, because the state admits it cannot judge opinion.
3. **Toleration**, where the state gives up any claim to rule belief.

Only the third stage ends the quarrel. Once the state stops enforcing truth, the Church has no need to claim supremacy, and the state can be granted its legal omnipotence. Neither side saw that "toleration alone could set the conflicting claims at rest" (p. 215), but "each side was right in its main contention" (p. 216). We owe to the royalists "the stability and independence of the English State", and to their opponents the recognition of liberty of thought (p. 216).

### 12. Non-resistance and sovereignty

Chapter IX takes the political side. Almost every writer of the seventeenth century, Figgis argues, taught non-resistance to whatever he took to be the supreme authority (pp. 219–225). Resistance was allowed only as obedience to something higher: God, the Pope, the Kirk, the law. Even the *Vindiciae* forbade private men to rise. Parliament fought the king's person in the name of his authority. The 1688 settlement said James had "abdicated". The Whig Samuel Johnson held that Christians must suffer persecution when the laws command it. Locke and Sidney did not say that resistance to law is ever right. They said that unjust laws are not laws "properly so called" (p. 224). These ideas were developed "as part of a theory of obedience and not of liberty" (p. 220). So the real quarrel between royalists and Whigs was not about resistance at all. It was "as to the existence of a sovereign one or number not subject to law" (p. 223).

The causes were medieval. The Middle Ages had been a long struggle between government and anarchy, ending in the Wars of the Roses and a reaction toward "peace at any price" (p. 226). Men revered the Common Law as the order that had replaced feudal violence, "the breath of God", older than kings and Parliaments (p. 227). Coke could say that the Common Law will control an Act of Parliament against right and reason (p. 227).

But the Tudors had made sovereignty a fact. Their legislation had changed religion, property and the succession. Men were forced to ask whether law comes before the lawgiver or the lawgiver before law. The quarrel between the king and the judges was "inevitable in the nature of things" (p. 229). Coke wanted the Common Law, interpreted by the judges, to be sovereign and nobody else, which would have left England like the Punjab under Ranjit Singh as Maine described it, where the person habitually obeyed never made a real law (p. 230). "It is the King and his supporters, be it observed, who first saw the change. Parliament, unwilling at first to claim the sovereignty, denies that it exists" (p. 231). Figgis concludes that on this issue "The true leaders of progress in this matter were the believers in Divine Right" (p. 233). Most men saw sovereignty because they saw it on the head of Henry VIII or Elizabeth (p. 234).

Hence his central thesis: "The Divine Right of Kings on its political side was little more than the popular form of expression for the theory of sovereignty" (p. 235). The proof is that many of its supporters extended it to all established governments. Hickes's *Jovian* applies the ban on resistance to republics. Digges's *Unlawfulness of Subjects taking up Arms* reads like a popular abridgment of *Leviathan* with Scripture added. Leslie wrote that "For the word King, I mean no more than the supreme authority", and that "There is no medium possible betwixt non-resistance upon any pretence whatsoever and a full licence to resist upon every pretence whatsoever" (p. 238).

The royalists kept telling their opponents that every state has a last resort. Hobbes said "The name of tyranny signifieth nothing more nor less than the name of sovereignty". Digges said someone must be trusted. Laud wrote "Turn the knot which way you will, all binding to obedience will be grievous to some." A pamphleteer asked: "The will of one man is contrary to freedom, and why not the will of five hundred?" (p. 239). They wrote with "the half-amazed irritation of a teacher" faced with pupils who think a state can have no power exempt from law (p. 239).

Locke's *Second Treatise*, on this reading, is "an attack directed far more against the idea of sovereignty than against the claims of monarchy" (p. 240). It makes the legislative supreme and then fences it with limits whose breach dissolves the state. By that test, Figgis remarks, hardly a Parliament since the Reform Act has failed to dissolve the state. Milton and Sidney took Rousseau's ground, that the people are sovereign, which is more consistent than Locke but untrue as a matter of fact, and Sidney then gave arbitrary lawmaking power to Parliament (pp. 241–242). That the Whigs could believe in an original compact shows they thought law came before the state, since a contract binds only where a sovereign enforces it (pp. 242–243).

Some of the royalists' odder doctrines make sense in this light (pp. 244–246). Their "fundamental law" meant what modern writers call the fundamental conceptions of politics: a state needs a sovereign and subjects. Indefeasible hereditary right was the inalienability of sovereignty. The claim that Parliament's privileges were royal grants that could be recalled expressed its indivisibility, as in Bodin. This is also why the affinity between the royalists and Hobbes was "far closer than is often supposed" (p. 246).

### 13. Organism against machine

Why then did they hate Hobbes? His irreligion, his contempt for Aristotle and his Erastianism counted against him, but Figgis names two deeper reasons (pp. 246–249). First, "Alone among the men of his time Hobbes realised, that politics are not and cannot be a branch of theology", and then went to the other extreme of treating theology as a branch of politics (p. 247). Second, he founded the state on a contract.

The contract was the one clear idea of the anti-royalists, and it denied what the royalists cared about most: that the state is a natural growth. For Locke, Sidney and Milton the state is a device that can be taken apart and remade. Hobbes's machine lasts for ever, but it has no life or growth. The Whig view that the people might do without a constitution if they were better men makes the state a necessary evil, which Figgis traces back to the medieval papalist idea, stated openly by Gregory VII and hinted at by Aquinas, that the state is a consequence of the Fall (p. 249). Against this the believers in divine right held that political society is natural to man, and their appeals to Scripture, the family and history were all governed by that one thought.

"No theory of government was ever more untrue to the facts of life than is that of Locke" (p. 250). Both sides taught non-resistance, misread Scripture and lacked a utilitarian theory, so on those points neither was ahead. Where they differed, the royalists were right twice: every state has a supreme power above its laws, and government is natural and necessary. Figgis sums it up in Aristotle's phrase that man is by nature a political animal (p. 251).

He ends the chapter with Burke. If we honour Burke for defending the historical, organic state and the value of "prejudice" against Rousseau, we cannot sneer at Filmer and Leslie for saying the same against Locke (p. 252). The theory also expressed a need for some bond of conscience to tie men to a government beyond its usefulness. He quotes Carlyle that "all available authority is Mystic in its conditions" and Laud's sermon: "no laws can be binding if there be no conscience to obey them; penalty alone could never, can never do it" (p. 253).

### 14. Figgis's conclusion

The last chapter collects the argument (pp. 254–266).

- **Origins.** The doctrine drew on very old feelings, the sanctity of the medicine man, priestly kingship, the divinity of Roman emperors, and on the common Christian sense that obedience is a religious duty. It took definite form only when the Papacy turned that sense to its own use.
- **A doctrine of liberty.** In its essential meaning it was "a doctrine of liberty—the freedom of political societies from subjection to an ecclesiastical organization" (p. 255). It had to give sovereignty to the king and not to Parliament, because "Against the traditional splendours of the tiara it would have been vain to set up any lower dignity than the Crown." The one country whose break with Rome was popular, Scotland, fell under a worse church rule.
- **A transition.** Before politics could become secular it had to "assert for itself a coequal right to exist with theology" (p. 256), and against a ruler who claimed to be God's vicar that claim could only be made by divine right. Figgis compares the Reformation, which freed science, art and letters from one method. In politics, freedom from theology was only possible by claiming a theological sanction for a non-theological politics (p. 258).
- **Success.** The deposing power and the papal plenitude of power have vanished rather than been refuted, and Newman explained papal infallibility as no threat to civil allegiance (p. 259). The Church's old claim survives in toleration.
- **Why it faded.** "The theory of Divine Right did not lose popularity because it was absurd, but because its work was done" (p. 261). The arguments against it were as good in 1598 as in 1688, as Parsons shows.
- **Legacy.** The English respect for law and dislike of violent breaks with the past, the land "Where freedom slowly broadens down / From precedent to precedent" (p. 262), owe much to it. The "abdication" of James II was a transparent fiction, but "The phrase was false, but the sentiment it expressed was profoundly true." "You can do anything with bayonets except sit on them" (p. 263). The law-abiding sense of most Englishmen "is the priceless legacy bequeathed to our own day by the believers in the Divine Right of Kings" (p. 264).
- **Errors.** Indefeasible hereditary right was never rational. Royalists exaggerated the prerogative and the impotence of Parliament. Their great fault was to make absolute and universal a theory relative to particular conditions, and their opponents did the same with natural rights. "The main practical lesson of the history of this doctrine is that of the relativity of all political dogma" (p. 265). We now "treat a faith which has ceased to be credible as though it were never creditable" (pp. 265–266).

The book closes with a warning. The seventeenth century failed in its attempt to use the Bible as a treatise on law and government. Figgis asks whether the new attempt to find a social order in the Sermon on the Mount is likely to do better (p. 266).

The appendices give the succession statutes, which show the idea of election fading into inherent right. Richard III's title of 1484 blends inheritance with election. Henry VII's is a brisk enactment. Elizabeth's rests on an Act of Parliament. James I's recognises a crown that came to him "by inherent birthright" (pp. 267–270). Another appendix traces popular sovereignty to the conciliar theory of Gerson, who argued that a council can judge and depose a Pope, and so supplied the arguments later used against kings (pp. 271–274). ### Conclusion

Figgis takes a doctrine that his contemporaries found too foolish to explain and shows it as a tool made for a purpose. The purpose was the independence of the state from the Church. The medieval Papacy had built the first theory of sovereignty by divine right, and the only answer that could work in its terms was a rival claim of the same kind. The imperialists made it for the Emperor, the Tudors and Gallicans for their kings, and the Stuart clergy against both Rome and Geneva. Its four propositions came at different times and for different reasons. The hereditary principle came from feudal law and from kings like Henry IV of France and James I who had nothing else. Passive obedience was made a passion by the Civil War.

The analytic chapters make two larger claims. The first is that the theory was the popular form of the idea of sovereignty, and that on this point its believers saw more clearly than Coke, Locke or Sidney. The second is that it held, against the contract theorists, that the state is a living, natural thing that needs loyalty as well as interest to hold together. Figgis gives the other side its due too. The clerical claims carried the rights of conscience in the only form that age allowed, and the long fight between the two ended only in toleration.

His verdict is that the theory died when its work was done: Rome no longer claimed to depose kings, Presbyterianism no longer threatened to rule England, and politics no longer needed theology's leave to exist. What it left behind was a habit of obedience to law and a distrust of sudden breaks. Its error, shared with its enemies, was to make a weapon for one struggle into an eternal truth.
