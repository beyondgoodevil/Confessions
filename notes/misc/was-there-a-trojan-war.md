The Greeks never doubted that their ancestors had sailed to Troy and destroyed it. Thucydides argued about the size of the army, Herodotus about whether Helen was really inside the walls, but neither questioned the war. Modern scholars have been divided ever since Heinrich Schliemann began digging at Hisarlık in 1870. At one extreme Carl Blegen, who excavated the site in the 1930s, wrote that "it can no longer be doubted" that a Mycenaean coalition under a recognised overlord fought the people of Troy (quoted by Finley, p. 160). At the other, Moses Finley ended his review of the evidence a century after Schliemann: "Homer's Trojan War, we suggest, must be evicted from the history of the Greek Bronze Age" (p. 177).

The question has changed since Finley wrote. New excavations at Troy since 1988, Hittite texts that name the city, and Linear B tablets from Thebes have been read as settling it. This note sets out the kinds of evidence one at a time and what each writer makes of them. It does not repeat the separate question of who Homer was and when he composed, which is the subject of [[Who Was Homer? The Homeric Question from Antiquity to Today]].

### 1. What the Ancient Historians Thought

**Thucydides.** The opening of Thucydides' *History* treats the Trojan War as a historical event to be measured like any other. Agamemnon raised the army, he argues, "more… by his superiority in strength, than the oaths of Tyndareus, which bound the Suitors to follow him" (1.9.1, Landmark). He anticipates a modern objection: "Mycenae may have been a small place… but no exact observer would therefore feel justified in rejecting the estimate given by the poets," since a ruined Sparta would look weaker than it had been and a ruined Athens stronger (1.10.1–2). He accepts Homer's 1,200 ships but reckons the crews small, and explains the ten years by poverty:

> Even after the victory they obtained on their arrival—and a victory there must have been, or the fortifications of the naval camp could never have been built—there is no indication of their whole force having been employed; on the contrary, they seem to have turned to cultivation of the Chersonese and to piracy from want of supplies. (1.11.1)

If they had stuck to the siege, he concludes, "the capture of Troy would have cost them less time and less trouble," and the expedition was "inferior to its renown and to the current opinion about it formed under the tuition of the poets" (1.11.2). Page shows in an appendix that Thucydides' argument assumes the Greek wall was built on arrival, and so that his text of the *Iliad* did not yet contain the passage in Book 7 where the wall is built in the tenth year (pp. 315–23).

**Herodotus.** Herodotus dated the war "about 800 years ago" (2.145.4), roughly 1250 BC. Egyptian priests told him that Helen had spent the war in Egypt with King Proteus, and he agreed with them on grounds of probability:

> If Helen had been in Troy, the Trojans would certainly have returned her to the Hellenes, whether Alexandros concurred or not. For neither Priam nor his kin could have been so demented that they would have willingly endangered their own persons, their children, and their city just so that Alexandros could have Helen. (2.120.1–2)

The war happened; only the cause was wrong. The Trojans "did not have Helen and therefore could not give her back," and the gods arranged their destruction to show "that when great injustices are committed, retribution from the gods is also great" (2.120.5). The later chronographer Eratosthenes fixed the fall of Troy at 1184/3 BC. Page calls all such ancient dates "merely speculative guesses" built on genealogies (p. 95 n. 159); Lane Fox calls them "historically worthless" (ch. 13).

### 2. Finding Troy

Travellers from the sixteenth century looked for Troy at Alexandria Troas, then, from 1785, at Balli Dağ above the village of Bunarbashi, where two Frenchmen claimed to have found Homer's hot and cold springs (Luce, pp. 124–26). An Englishman, Jacob Bryant, argued that Troy had never existed; Byron replied that he had stood on the plain for more than a month in 1810 and "venerated the grand original as the truth of history" (Luce, p. 126). Charles Maclaren argued from the text alone in 1822 that Troy lay at Hisarlık, a mound whose Turkish name means "fortress"; Frank Calvert, who owned part of it, made a sounding in 1865 and steered Schliemann there (Luce, p. 126; Lane Fox, ch. 13).

Finley's five points of agreement, written in 1974, still stand as a baseline. "Ancient Troy was at Hissarlik, as virtually the whole of antiquity believed… and the Troy which was besieged and captured by the Achaeans, if there was a Trojan War, was the city we now call Troy VIIa" (p. 168). Errors of scale in Homer were to be expected, and the literary merits of the poems were irrelevant to their history. He was scornful of attempts to prove the case from details: Priam's night drive to the Greek camp and back covered "roughly the same as that from the Royal Academy to Tower Hill" (p. 169).

Luce, who visited the Troad repeatedly, finds the topography more telling. Homer has Poseidon watch the battle from Samothrace, which seems impossible on a map; but the peak rises over Imbros and is visible from Troy on a clear day, and Luce "found it hard to resist" Kinglake's conclusion that Homer had been there (p. 121). Six descriptions are used only of Troy in Homer — "mighty citadel", "solidly-built", "well-walled", "well-towered", "beetling" and "rich in horses" — and all fit the fortifications of Troy VI and VIIa; horse bones are common only in those layers (pp. 127–28). The one Homeric landmark that cannot be found is the pair of springs, one hot and one cold, by the walls (*Iliad* 22.147–56); Walter Leaf thought the poet had gathered a feature of the whole Troad into one place (Luce, pp. 126–27).

### 3. The Layers of Troy

Troy was occupied from about 3000 to about 1000 BC in a series of settlements numbered from the bottom. Schliemann at first took the burnt second city, with its gold hoard, for Priam's Troy; Page shows that the "Treasure of Priam" belongs to Troy II, about a thousand years too early, and that its gold "had neither past nor future" at the site (pp. 45–48). Shortly before his death Schliemann accepted that the Homeric city must be the much later sixth.

**Troy VI** was a new foundation with new pottery, horses and monumental walls. Page called its south wall "a masterpiece of masonry", built "not of panic but of pride" (pp. 59–60). He estimated the citadel at a few hundred inhabitants: "a moderate sprinter could cover the ground in twenty-five seconds" (p. 54). He found no gold, no Hittite objects and no evidence that Troy taxed traffic through the Dardanelles; its wealth came from farming, spinning (Schliemann collected 7,737 spindle whorls) and perhaps horses (pp. 66–70). Troy VI was shattered by an earthquake around 1300 or 1275.

**Troy VIIa** (now often called VIi) was rebuilt by the same people with small houses crowded against the walls and storage jars sunk into the floors. It was burnt in a fierce fire. Page concluded that the excavations "do confirm it up to this point: at a date quite close to that of tradition, Troy was most thoroughly burnt; and the burning was evidently the work of man" (p. 74). Finley was not persuaded. The evidence of a Mycenaean army, he said, was "limited to a single bronze arrowhead found in Street 710"; Blegen's date for the fire moved from 1275–1240 to 1260, apparently to put Troy before the fall of Pylos; and the storage jars made it seem as if the Trojans had prepared for a siege for thirty-five years (pp. 160–61). He quoted Blegen's own collaborator John Caskey: "the physical remains of Troy VIIa do not prove beyond question that the place was captured at all. An accidental fire, in unlucky circumstances, on a day a strong wind was blowing, might account for the general destruction" (p. 170).

**Korfmann's lower town.** The excavations led by Manfred Korfmann from 1988 changed the picture of Troy's size. Magnetic survey and test trenches found a rock-cut ditch about 400 metres south of the citadel, a gate with a causeway and palisade, and a second ditch further out. Latacz reports the excavators' estimate of about 200,000 square metres and 6,000–7,000 inhabitants, perhaps more (pp. 25–26, 37). Frank Kolb had written in 1984 that Troy VI and VIIa were "wretched little settlements"; Latacz notes that he later withdrew the claim for Troy VI (pp. 25, 290 n. 16). Korfmann classed Troy as an Anatolian "royal seat and trading town": ditches, mudbrick on stone and towers are Anatolian, the grey pottery is Anatolian and only about one per cent Mycenaean, and stelae stood before the gates as in Hittite cities (Latacz, pp. 37–40). Latacz goes further and calls Troy "a leading member of a kind of Hanseatic league", controlling ships held up by the north wind at the mouth of the Dardanelles (p. 48). The dispute in Germany grew bitter: Kolb called Korfmann a "Däniken of archaeology", and a 2002 symposium at Tübingen ended without agreement (Latacz, pp. ix–xi). Strauss, writing for a general audience, accepts the excavators' lower town and their view that Troy VIi "was probably destroyed by human violence" (p. 233).

The physical traces of a sack remain modest. Strauss lists them: a partial male skeleton by a doorway, scattered unburied bones, a girl buried inside the town, two spear points, three arrowheads (one of a mainland Greek type) and a cache of 157 sling stones. He concludes fairly that none of this "proves beyond doubt that Troy was destroyed in a sack", and that the archaeology is consistent with the Greeks as attackers "but does not prove it" (pp. 180–81). Lane Fox reaches the same verdict: the attackers are "unknown" (ch. 13).

### 4. The Hittite Documents

Finley thought only a new written source could settle the matter. The Hittite archives from Hattusa have since been read as that source.

**Page in 1959.** Page devoted his first and third lectures to the Hittite texts, and they show how much has changed. In the 1920s Emil Forrer announced that the archives named Atreus, Eteocles and Alexander of Ilios; Ferdinand Sommer dismissed most of this as "a balloon, a gasbag" (Page, p. 3). The central question was whether the land called Ahhiyawa meant the Achaeans. Page accepted that it did, but placed Ahhiyawa on Rhodes, arguing from a long Hittite letter that the king of Ahhiyawa must have lived beyond the Hittite army's reach but close to the coast (pp. 11–17). He found the background to the war in a different text: the annals of Tudhaliya, which list twenty-two lands that rose against the Hittites under the name of Assuwa, ending with Wilusiya and Taruisa. Taruisa = Troia was "possible", Wilusiya = Wilios less certain (pp. 102–07). His conclusion was that "the historical background of the war of Greeks and Asiatics, the Trojan War, is to be found precisely here,—in the conflict between rival forces, Achaeans and the League of Assuwa, over the territory at long last vacated by the Hittites," with the attack on Troy "perhaps within a decade (either way) of 1230 B.C." (pp. 109–10).

**Wilusa = Ilios.** Later work moved Ahhiyawa to the Greek mainland and placed Wilusa in the Troad. Latacz traces the steps. Frank Starke, using a bronze treaty tablet found at Hattusa in 1986 and a letter from the king of the Seha River Land, located all the western Anatolian kingdoms and left only the north-west for Wilusa; the letter says a raider attacked Wilusa and then Lazba, Lesbos, which was visible from it (pp. 82–83). In 1997 J. D. Hawkins read the inscription at the Karabel pass near Izmir as "Tarkasnawa, King of Mira", which fixed Mira and pushed Wilusa "back into its home in the Troad" (Latacz, pp. 88–89). Latacz concludes: "Ilios or Wilios is not the product of the Greek imagination, but a real historical site" (p. 90). He notes that the Greek name had an initial *w*, Wilios, and that in about 45 per cent of its occurrences in the *Iliad* the metre requires it (p. 217).

**What the texts say about Wilusa.** The treaty between the Hittite king Muwattalli II (c. 1290–1272) and Alaksandu of Wilusa records that Wilusa had long been a loyal client which "regularly sent envoys", promises Hittite help — "If some enemy arises for you, then I, my Majesty, will not abandon you" — and lists among the oath gods "the (divine) Underground Watercourse of the land of Wilusa" (Latacz, pp. 75, 105–10). The excavators found an artificial cave and water channels at Troy dating to the third millennium (Latacz, pp. 83–85). The name Alaksandu has long been compared with Alexandros, Paris's other name in Homer; Latacz thinks it a Greek name in Hittite dress (p. 117). Around 1250 the Hittite king wrote to the king of Ahhiyawa as "my brother", an equal, about a raider who used Miletus as a base, and mentioned "that matter of Wilusa (?), over which we quarrelled" (Latacz, p. 124). Late in the century another letter asked for the restoration of Walmu, a deposed king of Wilusa: "Just as he was previously king of the land of Wilusa, so shall he be again!" (Latacz, pp. 112–13). A small bronze seal in Luwian hieroglyphs, the only Bronze Age writing found at Troy, came to light in 1995 (Latacz, pp. 49–51).

**Caution.** John Bennet's chapter in the *New Companion*, written before the Karabel reading, accepts Ahhiyawa as a reference to Mycenaean Greeks but warns: "Whether we can go so far as to link such references to a specific series of historical events associated with the site of Troy itself is less clear" (p. 519). Strauss judges that the identifications Wilusa = Troy and Ahhiyawa = Achaeans are "not certain" but "likely" (p. 233). No Hittite text mentions a Greek siege of Wilusa. The Hittite king's quarrel "about Wilusa" depends on a damaged reading. And Homer, as Page pointed out, shows "no awareness of the Hittites" at all (p. 93 n. 138).

### 5. The Names of the Attackers

Homer calls the Greeks Achaeans, Danaans and Argives, never Hellenes. Latacz argues that the first two are Bronze Age names that could not have been invented in Homer's time, when no general name for the Greeks existed. Ahhiyawa in Hittite corresponds to Achaiwia. An inscription from the funerary temple of Amenhotep III (c. 1390–1352), published in 1966, lists a land called Tanaja or Danaja next to Crete, with places including Mycenae, Thebes, Messenia, Nauplion, Kythera and Elis (Latacz, pp. 130–31). On this view each name entered the poetry in its own period, and all three survived because their different metrical shapes were useful to poets (pp. 133–36).

Page argued similarly from heroes' names. Names ending in -eus, such as Achilleus, Odysseus and Atreus, went out of use after the Mycenaean age, and "there never was a story about Achilles except in connexion with a war against Troy: the name Achilleus indicates that the person was the subject of story at least as far back as the 12th century B.C." (p. 197). Linear B tablets contain a man called A-ki-re-u and a "Trojan woman", but Page was careful to say that such matches prove only that the names were in ordinary use (pp. 197–99).

### 6. The Catalogue of Ships

The Catalogue in *Iliad* Book 2 lists twenty-nine contingents, 1,186 ships and 178 places (Latacz, pp. 219–22). It has been the main witness for the defence since T. W. Allen in 1921.

**Page's case.** Page made four arguments (pp. 120–54). First, the Catalogue ignores the Dorian occupation entirely: there is no Dorian Corinth, Argos or Messenia, and no Thessalians. Second, about a quarter of its places could not be found by later Greek geographers. Strabo wrote of three Arcadian towns that "it is difficult to find these and you would be no better off if you did find them, because nobody lives there" (p. 122). A poet after the Dark Age could not have known such names, and once in Homer they would never have been forgotten. Third, places carry distinctive epithets, such as Messe "of many doves" and Enispe "windy", which "must originally have been applied to their place names for one reason only,—because they were true" (pp. 123–24). Fourth, the Catalogue conflicts with the rest of the *Iliad*: Boeotia comes first, Achilles rules a small territory, Odysseus brings only twelve ships, and Agamemnon is stripped of Argos. Page concluded that it was a Mycenaean "Order of Battle", composed in Boeotia and "rather carelessly inserted into the Iliad" (p. 134). The ship numbers, on the other hand, he thought partly Ionian and "logistically absurd" (p. 152).

**The Theban tablets.** Latacz adds evidence Page did not have. More than 250 Linear B tablets were found at Thebes in 1993–95, in a palace burnt about 1200. They show Thebes ruling Euboea, and they name places that appear together in one line of the Catalogue — "they who held Eleōn and Hylē and Peteōn" (2.500) — and Eutresis (2.502), a town destroyed at the end of the Mycenaean period and not resettled until about 600 BC (Latacz, pp. 238–47). Edzard Visser found no misplaced name in the whole Catalogue (Latacz, p. 223). The list also omits every Greek city on the coast of Asia Minor, which no poet working in Ionia would have done on his own (pp. 232–38). Latacz concludes that "the entire tale of Troy must definitely have been composed in the Mycenaean age" (p. 248).

**Against.** Finley quoted John Chadwick, the co-decipherer of Linear B: "I believe the Homeric evidence to be almost worthless… One major reason is precisely the complete lack of contact between Mycenaean geography as now known from the tablets and from archaeology on the one hand, and from Homeric accounts on the other" (p. 174). Porter calls the Catalogue a mélange of archaic and Bronze Age geography (ch. 4). Lane Fox thinks it a later addition to Homer's poem by rhapsodes (ch. 14). Latacz himself grants that the Catalogue would prove an old list of Mycenaean places used for some overseas expedition, not that the expedition was against Troy. Page saw the gap and closed it with the Trojan Catalogue: its knowledge of Asia Minor is thin and old-fashioned, yet it preserves names lost by historical times, such as Alybe "the birthplace of silver" and Mount Phthires near Miletus. He argued that the Mycenaeans could have had only one reason to versify a list of peoples in north-west Asia Minor — an expedition there (pp. 137–44).

### 7. Bronze Age Memories in the Poetry

A second line of argument looks for Bronze Age survivals inside Homer's language.

**Objects.** Page and Luce collect the classic examples. The boar's-tusk helmet in *Iliad* 10 went out of use by the twelfth century; Martin Nilsson concluded that "there is no explanation left but the one that the description was preserved through the lapse of time by the epic tradition" (Page, pp. 218–19). Ajax's shield, carried "like a tower", is a body-shield of the sixteenth century, and Page argued that Ajax himself was an older hero drawn into the story: "The magnet of the Trojan War has attracted this hero of earlier adventures" (p. 235). The Achaeans are "well-greaved", which on Egyptian monuments distinguished them from their enemies; the Trojans alone are "horse-taming", and the horse first appears at Troy with the founders of Troy VI (pp. 245, 252). Luce adds Andromache's advice to Hector:

> but draw your people up by the fig tree, there where the city
> is openest to attack, and where the wall may be mounted.
> Three times their bravest came that way, and fought there to storm it
> (*Iliad* 6.433–35, Lattimore)

A weak stretch of older walling survives in the western circuit; Leaf thought it curious that this should be "exactly confirmed by the existing remains", and Luce sees "a memory of actual fighting round a particular set of walls" (pp. 129–30).

**Language.** Bennet sets out the linguistic case. Some lines scan only if Homer's words are put back into a form older than the Linear B tablets. The standard example is the line about Meriones, "equal to Enyalios the man-slayer", which scans only with a syllabic *r* that had already disappeared from the Greek of the tablets (pp. 525–26). Geoffrey Horrocks showed that tmesis, the separation of verb and prefix, was already gone from the tablets' prose but survives in the epic. Bennet concludes that dactylic poetry had "a role to play in the Bronze Age narrative tradition" and that epic song continued "at least from the early Mycenaean period (c. 1700 B.C.) to Homer's day" (pp. 524, 531). Latacz adds the formula *Iliou proparoithen*, "before Ilios", which scans only with a genitive form older than Homer, and the fact that Homer makes Lesbos part of Priam's realm (24.543–46) and has Achilles sack it — which no poet would have said after Greeks settled the island about 1050 (pp. 267–73).

**The limit of the argument.** Finley's reply was that such relics prove nothing about events. He wished the other side would "abandon their dance of triumph" over the boar's-tusk helmet; "Who has ever pretended that every single word in the Iliad… is as imaginary as the mermaids and the abode of the gods?" (p. 168). Bennet agrees that the poems' society is not Mycenaean. The tablets reveal bureaucratic palaces, slaves, specialised crafts and a ruler called the *wanax*, almost none of which the epic remembers. He ends with Finley: "Homer is not only not a reliable guide to the Mycenaean tablets; he is no guide at all" (p. 533). A Bronze Age tradition of poetry is one thing; a Bronze Age war is another.

### 8. If There Was a War, What Kind?

Those who accept a historical core describe very different wars.

- **Page** saw a conflict over territory vacated by the collapsing Hittite empire. Mycenaean traders turned to conquest: they were "not men with a mission: they were men with a quest", like the conquistadores (p. 111).
- **Luce** follows Page and adds a motive: control of the Troad and the Hellespont. Helen "can hardly account for an expedition on this scale" (p. 131). His summary of the Mycenaean pattern: "first trade with your neighbour, then covet his wealth, and finally attack him" (p. 136).
- **Latacz** proposes a scenario. Archaeology at Miletus shows that the Achaeans lost their foothold there to the Hittites in the second half of the thirteenth century. Their next target on the coast would naturally be the rich trading city of Troy (pp. 284–86). He refuses to tie this to the earthquake or the fire, but concludes that the evidence for a war "is already almost overwhelming", and that the near future may show that "Homer is to be taken seriously" (pp. 286–87).
- **Strauss** reconstructs a war of raids. Bronze Age kingdoms could not keep 100,000 men in the field; he estimates about 15,000 a side and some 300 ships (pp. 41–42). "There was no siege of Troy," he writes, and "the Trojan War consisted mainly of low-intensity conflict and attacks on civilians"; "the Iliad is to the Trojan War what The Longest Day is to World War II" (p. 5). He reads the ten years as a Near Eastern idiom, "nine times and then a tenth", meaning "over and over until finally" (p. 7), and dates the war between 1210 and 1180 (p. 10).
- **Trevor Bryce**, quoted by Latacz, thinks a series of Mycenaean attacks on Wilusa merged in the poetry into one great war (Latacz, p. 283).
- **Finley** suggested a different context: the wave of destruction around 1200 that the Egyptians blamed on the "Sea Peoples". It was a hypothesis "from the ground", but one that contradicts the Homeric picture (pp. 172–73).
- **Porter** offers a cultural reading. The Trojan War may be an Iron Age memory of the general collapse of palace societies around 1200, by which the Greeks "offloaded" their own catastrophe onto a city across the sea and remembered it as a conquest (ch. 4). He quotes Finley's alternative: "We may take it for granted that there was a Trojan War in Mycenaean times; more correctly, that there were many Trojan Wars."
- **Lane Fox** thinks the war as Homer tells it "manifestly fiction", and suggests that East Greeks settling Asia Minor between the eleventh and ninth centuries invented it to explain the ruins they found, much as later Greeks explained Knossos with the Minotaur (ch. 13). He quotes Oliver Dickinson: even if there is smoke, "it may simply be mist."

Page himself warned against the wrong kind of credulity. He thought the war and the chief names historical, but "the wrath of Achilles, the death of Patroclus, the combat with Hector… let all this be imaginary, as most of it surely is" (p. 253). The wrath of a hero was a common story motif — Homer also tells of the wraths of Meleager, Aeneas and Paris — and the stolen princess was "the common story of the beautiful princess stolen from her husband or lover" (pp. 256–57).

### 9. Helen and the Horse

Most writers treat Helen as the occasion of the war rather than its cause. Herodotus thought she was never at Troy. Strauss, drawing on Hittite and Near Eastern texts in which wars are framed as personal wrongs, writes that "Helen was not the cause but merely the occasion of the war", and that the real motives were "resentment, greed, and power lust" (p. 28). He notes that Bronze Age diplomacy treated a royal marriage as a treaty and a royal seduction as an act of war; Hittite law told the man who ran off with a woman, "You have become a wolf" (p. 26).

The Wooden Horse is not in the *Iliad*. The *Odyssey* has the singer Demodocus tell the story at Odysseus' request (8.492–520). Explanations have ranged from a siege engine (the Assyrians named siege towers after animals) to a painted horse on a gate opened by the traitor Antenor, to a metaphor for ships, "horses of the sea" (Luce, pp. 137–39; Strauss, p. 172). Luce rejects the siege engine because no such machines are known in the Bronze Age, and accepts W. B. Stanford's verdict that "the origin of the stratagem of the Horse is obscure" (p. 139). Strauss thinks a horse may have existed as a decoy, probably empty: "The Trojan Horse might be better known as the Trojan Red Herring" (p. 173).

### 10. Real People?

Page argued that the chief names must be historical because the poetry began so soon after the event: "who can imagine a poet at the court of Mycenae reciting to the son or grandson of an earlier king the story of an expedition conducted by that king, when his audience knew very well that there was no such expedition and no king so named? … The Achaeans did fight the Trojans, and Agamemnon was the name of Mycenae's king. Achilles is certainly not less historical" (pp. 253–54). He thought the original poem was composed in northern Greece to glorify Achilles, since Mycenae's own court poets would not have made Agamemnon look so bad (p. 254). Latacz criticises Page here: he was "so filled with euphoria" that he declared the leading figures historical, and so far nothing proves that they were (pp. 169–70). Strauss treats the characters as real for the purposes of his narrative but describes their existence as "plausible but unproven". He adds a qualification: "Priam may never have ruled Troy, but Kings Alaksandu and Walmu did" (p. 11). Lane Fox notes that real men were later named after the heroes: a King Agamemnon of Cyme whose daughter married Midas, and a King Hector of Chios (ch. 13).

| Thucydides | Yes | Homer, read critically | Long, because the army scattered to farm and raid |
| Herodotus | Yes, but Helen was in Egypt | Egyptian priests; probability | A war fought for nothing |
| Page (1959) | Yes, c. 1230 | Hittite texts (Assuwa), Catalogue, formulas | Achaeans vs League of Assuwa after Hittite withdrawal |
| Finley (1954/1977) | Unproven; "must be evicted" from Bronze Age history | Absence of documents; Linear B | If anything, a Sea Peoples context |
| Luce (1975) | Yes, "a generous measure of historicity" | Topography, epithets, walls | Strategic attack on Troad and Hellespont |
| Bennet (1997) | Undecided; poetry Bronze Age, society not | Linguistic archaisms | — |
| Latacz (2004) | "There probably was a war over Troy" | Wilusa, Ahhiyawa, Danaja, Theban tablets | Ahhiyawan strike after loss of Miletos |
| Strauss (2006) | "We can presume that the Trojan War indeed happened" | Latacz, excavations, Near Eastern parallels | Raids and low-intensity war, c. 1210–1180 |
| Porter (2021) | Probably an amalgam | Systems collapse c. 1200 | A displaced memory of general catastrophe |
| Lane Fox (2023) | Homer's war "manifestly fiction" | Destruction of Troy VIi; attackers unknown | Invented by settlers to explain the ruins |

### 12. Assessment

Several things are now established. Hisarlık is Homer's Troy, and in the Late Bronze Age it was a substantial Anatolian city, not the hilltop "nest of pirates" of earlier sceptics. Its own Hittite name was Wilusa, which Greeks heard as Wilios. Mycenaean Greeks, called Ahhiyawa by the Hittites, were active in western Asia Minor, held Miletus for a time, raided the coast and islands for captives, and quarrelled with the Hittite king about the region; Linear B tablets at Pylos and Thebes list women from Miletus, Knidos, Lemnos and perhaps Troy. Troy VIIa/VIi was destroyed by fire around 1200. Greek hexameter poetry, on the linguistic evidence collected by Bennet and Latacz, goes back into the Mycenaean period and preserves some names and objects from it.

What is not established is the link between these facts. No contemporary text records a Greek attack on Wilusa. The archaeological traces of a sack at Troy are few and ambiguous, and the city was attacked more than once — the Alaksandu treaty mentions a war with Masa in which the Hittite king came to Wilusa's aid. Finley's argument from method still stands: archaeology without documents cannot show who burned a city, let alone who led the attack, and oral tradition is unreliable about dates. The Catalogue argument is stronger than Finley allowed, especially after the Theban tablets, but it shows at most that a Mycenaean list of contingents for some overseas expedition survived into Homer. The Trojan Catalogue offers a stronger link to an expedition to north-west Asia Minor, but it rests on two or three lost names.

Latacz's own distinction is the most useful. The Iliad is a source for the setting and framework of a story, not for its events. He accepts, with Franz Hampl, that "a story may be historically untrue and geographically fully precise" (p. 91). On the balance of the evidence, the most defensible position is something like Bryce's: Mycenaean Greeks probably fought at and around Wilusa in the thirteenth century, perhaps more than once, and one such conflict — possibly the destruction of Troy VIIa/VIi — became the subject of poetry. Everything Homer adds — ten years, a thousand ships, Helen, the wrath of Achilles, the Horse — belongs to the poem. Thucydides' own verdict fits this: the war was real but "inferior to its renown… formed under the tuition of the poets."

### Conclusion

The question "Was there a Trojan War?" has moved since Finley's day from "probably not provable" to "probably something". The site is right, the city was important, its name appears in Hittite archives, Greeks were active on that coast, the city burned around 1200, and the poetry that tells the story has roots in the age it describes. What has not been found is any document that says Greeks destroyed Wilusa. Page, Luce, Latacz and Strauss conclude that there was a war, though they describe it differently; Finley, Lane Fox and Porter think the war Homer describes is either unprovable or an invention. The ruins at Hisarlık neither prove nor disprove the *Iliad*'s story. What they show is that the poets set it in a real place, in a real world of rival powers.
