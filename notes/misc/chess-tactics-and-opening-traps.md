> "Surely in vain the net is spread in the sight of any bird." (Proverbs 1:17)

> "He made a pit, and digged it, and is fallen into the ditch which he made." (Psalm 7:15)

> "For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost?" (Luke 14:28)

Seirawan's **first principle** is **force**: who has more fighting material, overall or in one part of the board. You get it two ways: by massing your pieces where the enemy's aren't, or by winning his pieces outright. The second is what **tactics** are for: short forcing sequences (pins, forks, discoveries) that win material because the opponent can't answer two threats at once. **Traps** are tactics that wait for the opponent to blunder into them. Here's how it works:

## 1. Counting Force: The Point Values

Seirawan's table:

| Piece | Points |
|---|---|
| Pawn | 1 |
| Knight | 3 |
| Bishop | 3 |
| Rook | 5 |
| Queen | 9 |
| King | Infinite: losing it loses the game |

- Knights and bishops are **minor pieces**. Rooks and queens are **major pieces**.
- **Typical imbalances** (his examples):
  - Queen (9) vs two rooks (10): the rooks are favoured.
  - Knight (3) vs three pawns (3): even.
  - Rook and pawn (6) vs bishop and knight (6): even on points, but the two minor pieces are usually better, "especially when many other pieces are on the board", because they're more active.
  - Rook (5) vs bishop and two pawns (5): about equal.
- **The warning.** The numbers are "a guide, but use your own eyes as well". In one position three pawns beat a minor piece. In another the piece crushes them. He calls blind counting the "numbers racket".
- **Janowski's two bishops.** Seirawan's cautionary tale: Grandmaster David Janowski (1868–1927) loved the bishop pair so much that opponents learned to *offer* it to him at a price. Frank Marshall said he "could follow the wrong path with more determination than any man I met". Americans took to calling the two bishops "the two Jans". Lesson: prefer principles to preferences.

## 2. The Pin

A **pin** is when a piece can't move (or shouldn't) because it would expose a more valuable piece behind it.

1. **Relative pin.** The pinned piece *can* legally move but loses something behind it. After 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Nc3 Bg4, the knight on f3 is pinned to the queen on d1. If White plays 4.d4? exd4 5.Nxd4??, then 5...Bxd1 wins the queen. Better is 4.h3, "putting the question" to the bishop: 4...Bxf3 5.Qxf3 trades and ends the pin.
2. **Absolute pin.** The pin is against the **king**, so the pinned piece *can't legally* move at all. This is the strongest kind. The pinned piece is a sitting duck: attack it again (often with a pawn) and it falls.
3. **Pinning the queen.** After 1.e4 d5 2.exd5 Qxd5 3.Nc3 (hitting the queen), the worst retreat is 3...Qc6??, because 4.Bb5 pins the queen to the king along b5–c6–d7–e8. The queen (9) goes for a bishop (3), "a gain of 6 points!"
4. **Greed punished.** In an endgame where Black has a rook against White's bishop and pawn (and should just activate his king and rook), he grabs a pawn with ...Rxd5??. Then Bb3 pins the rook to his king, and White wins it. Seirawan's moral: when someone offers you a free pawn, "study the board carefully to figure out what trick he has in mind."

## 3. The Fork

A **fork** (or **double attack**) is one piece attacking two or more enemy pieces at once. The opponent can save only one.
- Any piece can fork. The queen is the best, since she attacks in eight directions. A pawn fork on two pieces is the cheapest.
- But the **knight** is so good at it that Seirawan nicknames it "Mr. Fork". It jumps, so it can't be blocked, and its odd L-shaped move lets it attack pieces that aren't on a common line. The classic is a **royal fork**: a knight checking the king while hitting the queen or a rook. The king must move, and the queen is lost for a knight.
- Beginners who suffer this, he says, often start fearing knights and swapping them off at any price.

## 4. Discovered Attacks and Other Tactics

**Discovered attack.** One piece moves out of the way and **uncovers** an attack by a piece behind it. The moving piece can make a threat of its own, so you get two threats at once.
- **Discovered check**: the uncovered attack is on the king.
- **Double check**: the moving piece *and* the uncovered piece both give check. No block or capture can stop both, so **the king must move**. If it can't, it's mate. (Chernev's games use this several times.)

Other standard tactics (not in Seirawan's beginner chapter, but every tactics book has them):
- **Skewer**: the reverse of a pin. A valuable piece in front is attacked, moves away, and the piece behind it is taken. A rook checking a king with the queen behind it on the same line is the classic.
- **Removing the defender / deflection**: capture or lure away the piece guarding a key square. (Fischer's book calls this "displacing defenders".)
- **Decoy**: force an enemy piece *onto* a fatal square, often with a sacrifice.
- **Overloading**: one defender has two jobs, so it can't do both.
- **In-between move (zwischenzug)**: instead of the expected recapture, a forcing move first (a check or a bigger threat), then the recapture. Chernev flags several as "a fine 'in-between' move".

## 5. Playing for Traps (Don't)

Seirawan is blunt: **"Don't play for traps!"** Every move should improve your position anyway. Never play a move that leaves only two outcomes: "Your opponent doesn't see your trap and loses horribly", or "He sees your trap and counters in such a way that your position falls apart."

Chernev makes the deeper point in his introduction: "The master does not search for combinations. He creates the conditions that make it possible for them to appear!" Tactics grow out of good positions: development, central control, open lines and a safe king.

Seirawan sorts traps into three types: those aimed at **weak pawns**, at **undefended pieces**, and those that **batter the king's bodyguards**.

## 6. Trap Type 1: The Weak f7 and f2 Pawns

In the opening, the f7-pawn (f2 for White) is protected **only by the king**, while the pawns in front of it (d7, e7) are protected by several pieces. So it's the natural target.

**Scholar's mate**
- 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5 Nf6?? 4.Qxf7#. The queen and bishop combine on f7.
- **Defence:** 3...g6 (not ...Nf6). If White keeps going with 4.Qf3, threatening f7 again, 4...Nf6 blocks.
- **Seirawan won't recommend it.** Against an opponent who sees it (most will), the queen has come out too early and will lose time running from Black's developing pieces (his second principle, time).

**Legall's mate**
- 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Bc4 Bg4 (pinning the f3-knight) 4.Nc3 g6? 5.Nxe5!! "White seems to have gone berserk!" The pin is fake: if 5...Bxd1?, then 6.Bxf7+ Ke7 7.Nd5#.
- The mating net: the d5-knight checks; d7 is covered by the e5-knight, e6 and e8 by the f7-bishop, f6 by the d5-knight, and d8 and f8 are blocked by Black's own queen and bishop.
- Black's best after 5.Nxe5 is to "limit his losses" with 5...dxe5 6.Qxg4, just a pawn down. "Black ate too much and got heartburn."
- **Lesson:** a pin on a knight against the queen is only a relative pin. If the knight's move creates a bigger threat, the pin doesn't hold.

## 7. Trap Type 2: Undefended Pieces

**The Excommunicated Bishop**
- 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 d6 4.Nc3 Bg4 5.O-O. (Legall's 5.Nxe5 wouldn't work here because 5...Nxe5 both stops the mate and defends the g4-bishop.)
- 5...Nge7?? (5...Nf6 was better, defending g4). Then 6.Bxf7+! Kxf7 7.Ng5+. The knight checks and **uncovers** the queen's attack on the undefended g4-bishop down the d1–g4 diagonal: "a perfect example of a discovered attack". 7...Ke8 8.Qxg4, and White is a pawn up.

**The Queen That Never Came Home**
- 1.e4 d5 2.exd5 Qxd5 3.Nc3 Qd8 4.d4 e6 5.Bd3 Qxd4?? Black thinks White has overlooked a pawn.
- 6.Bb5+! The bishop gives check and uncovers the d1-queen's attack on d4. Black is in check, so he can't save his queen. And 6...Nc6 doesn't help: after 7.Qxd4 the knight is pinned to the king and can't recapture.
- **Lesson:** be very careful about snatching pawns with the queen in the opening. She can get "caught dipping her hand into the cookie jar".

## 8. Trap Type 3: The Battering Ram

These open up the king's cover.

**Fool's mate** (the fastest mate possible)
- Seirawan's version: 1.f4 (the Bird Opening) e5 2.g4?? Qh4#. "For no reason whatsoever White has opened up his own King to attack on the e1–h4 diagonal." The better-known version is 1.f3 e5 2.g4?? Qh4#.

**The From's Gambit trap**
- 1.f4 e5 2.fxe5 d6 3.exd6 Bxd6. Black has given a pawn for development (a **gambit**). Now 4.Nf3 would develop and stop ...Qh4+. Instead 4.Nc3?? Qh4+ 5.g3 (forced) Qxg3+!! 6.hxg3 Bxg3#. The bishop mates on the g3–e1 diagonal. Every escape square is either covered (f2) or blocked by White's own pieces.
- **Lesson:** "the King is not safe in his starting position", so castle early.

**The cheap check: the Caro-Kann smothered mate**
- 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Nd7 5.Qe2 Ngf6?? 6.Nd6#. The knight can't be taken because the e7-pawn is **pinned** by the queen down the e-file, and the king is hemmed in by its own pieces: a **smothered mate**.
- **Why "cheap"?** If Black sees it and plays 5...Ndf6, White's 5.Qe2 is just a bad move. It took a defender away from d4 and blocked the f1-bishop. Seirawan's moral: **"Never make a move with the thought, 'I hope he doesn't see it.'"**

## 9. The Traps at a Glance

| Trap | Moves | Tactic | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scholar's mate | 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5 Nf6?? 4.Qxf7# | Attack on f7 | Defend f7; don't bring the queen out early |
| Legall's mate | 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Bc4 Bg4 4.Nc3 g6? 5.Nxe5!! Bxd1? 6.Bxf7+ Ke7 7.Nd5# | Breaking a relative pin | A pin on a knight can be ignored if the threat is bigger |
| Excommunicated Bishop | ...5.O-O Nge7?? 6.Bxf7+ Kxf7 7.Ng5+ Ke8 8.Qxg4 | Discovered attack | Don't leave pieces undefended |
| Queen That Never Came Home | ...5.Bd3 Qxd4?? 6.Bb5+ | Discovered attack plus pin | Don't grab pawns with the queen |
| Fool's mate | 1.f4 e5 2.g4?? Qh4# | Open diagonal to the king | Don't weaken the king's pawns |
| From's Gambit trap | 1.f4 e5 2.fxe5 d6 3.exd6 Bxd6 4.Nc3?? Qh4+ 5.g3 Qxg3+ 6.hxg3 Bxg3# | Queen sacrifice | Develop with tempo; block threats |
| Caro-Kann smothered mate | 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Nd7 5.Qe2 Ngf6?? 6.Nd6# | Pin plus smothered mate | Don't play "hope chess" |

## 10. Assessment

*Mine.*

- **The real lesson is calculation, not memorising traps.** Every trap here works because one side *didn't look*: at f7, at an undefended bishop, at what a check uncovers. The habit to build is a blunder check before every move: what does my move leave hanging, what checks and captures does he have, what's he threatening? "Counteth the cost" before building the tower (Luke 14:28).
- **Chernev's line is the key one.** Tactics aren't separate from strategy. A well-developed position *produces* combinations. The beginner hunts for tricks. The master builds the conditions and the tricks appear.
- **Greed is the common thread.** Black takes the queen in Legall's mate, the queen takes the d4-pawn, the rook takes d5. Each time the player who grabs gets caught: "he made a pit... and is fallen into the ditch which he made" (Ps 7:15). And "in vain the net is spread in the sight of any bird" (Prov 1:17): a trap only works on someone who isn't watching.
- **Seirawan's moral is a good one off the board too.** "Never make a move with the thought, 'I hope he doesn't see it.'" Hope chess is the chess version of acting on what you hope no one will notice. The honest move, good even if the opponent sees everything, is almost always the better move.
