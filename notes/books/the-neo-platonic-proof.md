## The Starting Point

- Plotinus: a thing is a being only insofar as it is **one** (Enneads VI.9).
	- An army, a house, a body = many parts made one. Take the unity away and the thing is gone.
- Feser: everything in our experience is **composite**.
	- Physical parts: tree → roots, trunk, leaves → cells → molecules → atoms.
	- A thing with no physical parts would still be composite in other ways: its distinct properties, what it is vs. that it is (essence/existence), what it is vs. what it could become (act/potency).
- Keep in mind: composite ≠ "made of bits you can pull apart." Any real distinction inside a thing counts.

## The Argument

1. The things of our experience are composite.
2. A composite thing exists only as long as its parts are **combined**.
3. The parts don't combine themselves, so something accounts for their combination **here and now**.
4. If that cause is itself composite, it needs a combiner too.
5. The series can't go on forever: each member only passes on what it receives from the one above.
6. So there is a cause with **no parts at all** = absolutely simple = the One.

- Step 3 = a concurrent cause, not a cause back at the start. Holding together, not getting started.
- Step 5 is the hierarchical series again: hand moves stick moves stone. Same structure as the Aristotelian proof in [[The Unmoved Mover]].
- Note: nothing here needs the universe to have a beginning. In Pruss's three families ([[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]]) this sits on the Thomistic side, not the kalam.

## Only One

- Two simple things would have to differ in something.
- Differing = one has a feature the other lacks.
- A feature distinct from the rest of a thing = a part.
- So a second "simple" thing would be composite. **There can only be one One.**

## What Else Follows

- No act/potency in it → **purely actual** → can't change → **immutable**, **eternal** (no before and after).
- No physical parts → **immaterial**, **incorporeal**.
- Cause of every composite, so of everything else → whatever is real in its effects is in it in some way → **perfect**, **good**.
- Holds the intelligible natures of all its effects → **intellect**, in an analogical sense.
	- Keep in mind: Plotinus put Intellect *below* the One, as its first emanation. Calling the One an intellect is the Christian adjustment (Augustine, Dionysius, Aquinas). See [[Emanationism]].
- Mirrors the end of the Aristotelian proof: different road, same destination. Purely actual = absolutely simple.

## Objections

- **"Why not the universe, the laws of physics, or the basic particles?"**
	- Universe = all its parts → composite.
	- Particles have mass, charge, spin → composite in the sense above.
	- Laws describe how things behave. They don't hold anything together.
- **"Simplicity is incoherent."** Plantinga's version: if God = His power and God = His knowledge, then power = knowledge, and God turns out to be a property.
	- The distinctions are in our concepts, not in God. Morning star / evening star = two descriptions, one planet.
	- "Then God is a property" assumes properties are abstract Platonic objects. The classical view never said that.
- **"Why call it God?"** = Pruss's Gap problem. Answered by the attributes above, not by the bare conclusion of step 6.

## The Orthodox Side

- The Fathers affirm simplicity too. St. John of Damascus: God is simple and uncompounded.
- The disagreement is over what simplicity rules out.
	- Thomist: any real distinction in God, so essence = existence = every attribute.
	- Palamas: essence and energies are really distinct **without** composition, because the energies aren't parts.
	- See [[Essence-Energies Distinction]] and [[Implications of No Essence-Energies Distinction]].
- **The proof gets you a simple God. It doesn't settle the Palamite question.** Steps 1-6 work for both sides; the fight starts at "What Else Follows."
