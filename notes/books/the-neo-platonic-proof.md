Feser's **Neo-Platonic proof** argues from the fact that everything we experience is **composite**, made up of parts, to the existence of a cause that has **no parts at all**. It takes its name from Plotinus, for whom a thing is a being only insofar as it is **one**. Here's how it works:

### 1. Everything We Experience Is Composite

Everything in our experience has parts, and those parts are held together into one thing:

- **Physical parts**: a tree is made of roots, trunk and leaves, which are made of cells, which are made of molecules and atoms.
- **Metaphysical parts**: even something without physical parts would still be composite in other ways, through its distinct properties, its essence and its existence, and what it is versus what it could become (act and potency).

Composition does not just mean "made of pieces you can take apart." Any real distinction within a thing counts. Plotinus' point (Enneads VI.9) is that **unity is what makes a thing a being at all**: an army, a house or a body is many parts made one, and without that unity it would not exist.

### 2. The Argument

1. The things of our experience are composite.
2. A composite thing exists only as long as its parts are combined.
3. The parts do not combine themselves, so something must account for their combination here and now.
4. If that cause is itself composite, it needs a cause of its own.
5. This series of causes cannot go on forever.
6. Therefore there is a cause with no parts at all: an **absolutely simple** cause, the **One**.

- **Significance of step 3:** the cause is a **concurrent** cause, one that holds the parts together now, not merely one that brought them together in the past.
- **Significance of step 5:** the series is **hierarchical**, like a hand moving a stick that moves a stone. Each member only passes on causal power it receives from the one above it, so without a first member nothing would happen at all. This is the same structure as the Aristotelian proof (see [[The Unmoved Mover]]).

The argument does not require the universe to have a beginning. It concerns what holds things in existence right now, which places it with the Thomistic family of cosmological arguments rather than the kalam (see [[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]]).

### 3. There Can Only Be One

- If there were two absolutely simple beings, they would have to differ in some way.
- To differ, one would need a feature the other lacks.
- But a feature distinct from the rest of a thing is a **part**, so that being would be composite after all.
- Therefore there can be **only one** absolutely simple being.

### 4. The Divine Attributes

From its simplicity, the other divine attributes follow:

1. **Pure Actuality**: with no distinction of act and potency in it, the One has no unrealised potential. It is therefore **immutable**, and **eternal**, without before and after.
2. **Immateriality**: matter has parts, so the One is **immaterial** and **incorporeal**.
3. **Perfection and Goodness**: as the cause of every composite thing, whatever is real in its effects must exist in it in some way, so it is **perfect** and **good**.
4. **Intellect**: it contains the intelligible natures of everything it causes, so it is intellect, in an analogical sense. Plotinus himself placed Intellect *below* the One, as its first emanation (see [[Emanationism]]). Identifying the One with intellect is the Christian adjustment made by Augustine, Dionysius and Aquinas.

The Neo-Platonic proof therefore arrives where the Aristotelian proof does: what is purely actual is absolutely simple, and what is absolutely simple is purely actual.

### 5. Objections

- **"Why not the universe, the laws of physics or the basic particles?"** The universe is the sum of its parts, so it is composite. Particles have mass, charge and spin, so they are composite in the sense above. Laws of physics describe how things behave; they do not hold anything together.
- **"Divine simplicity is incoherent."** Plantinga objects that if God is identical to His power and to His knowledge, then power and knowledge are identical, and God turns out to be a property. The reply is that the distinctions are in our concepts, not in God, just as "the morning star" and "the evening star" are two descriptions of one planet. The claim that God would be a property assumes that properties are abstract Platonic objects, which the classical view never held.
- **"Why call the One God?"** This is Pruss's "Gap" problem. It is answered by the attributes in section 4, not by the bare conclusion of the argument.

### 6. The Orthodox View

- **Simplicity Affirmed**: the Fathers also teach that God is simple. St. John of Damascus calls Him simple and uncompounded.
- **Where Orthodoxy and Thomism Differ**: the Thomist holds that simplicity rules out any real distinction in God, so His essence, His existence and every attribute are identical. St. Gregory Palamas holds that the essence and the energies are really distinct **without** composition, because the energies are not parts (see [[Essence-Energies Distinction]] and [[Implications of No Essence-Energies Distinction]]).
- **Significance:** the proof establishes a simple God, but it does not settle the Palamite question. Both sides can accept the argument; the disagreement begins over what simplicity implies.

For Plotinus' own account of the One's simplicity, see [[Neoplatonic Idea of Divine Simplicity]].

### Conclusion

The Neo-Platonic proof moves from the ordinary fact that things are made of parts to a cause that has no parts at all. Because composite things need something to hold them together here and now, and that series cannot regress forever, there must be an absolutely simple cause. Such a cause must be unique, purely actual, immaterial, perfect and intelligent, which is what is meant by God. For the Orthodox, the argument confirms the divine simplicity the Fathers taught, while leaving open how that simplicity relates to the distinction between God's essence and His energies.
