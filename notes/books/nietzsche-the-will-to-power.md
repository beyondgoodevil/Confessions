Will to power is N.'s name for what every living thing, and in the notebooks every thing, is doing: not keeping itself alive but trying to grow, to master what resists it, to become more. It is the most famous idea he has and the least settled. The book called *The Will to Power* is not his, the published passages are short and often hedged, and the notebooks go much further than anything he printed. So there are two questions running together. What did he mean by it? And did he mean it as a claim about the world, a psychology, or an expression of his own values? My view after this reading is that it starts as psychology, becomes a way of reading life, and turns into a metaphysics he never quite committed to in public. Power in his sense is not force and not domination. It needs resistance and keeps the opponent standing. Here's how it works:

N. = Nietzsche, WtP = will to power, WP = the posthumous *Will to Power* collection (also WM, *Wille zur Macht*, in Kaufmann and Heidegger), BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, GM = *On the Genealogy of Morality*, GS = *The Gay Science*, Z = *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (by part and chapter), TI = *Twilight of the Idols*, A = *The Antichrist*, D = *Daybreak* (Kaufmann's *Dawn*), HH = *Human, All Too Human*, KSA = the critical edition. Z is quoted in Parkes's translation, BGE and GM in Del Caro's, WP, GS, TI and A in Kaufmann's, unless I say otherwise.

### 1. The Text Problem (Read This First)

- **Not a book N. wrote.** *The Will to Power* was put together by his sister Elisabeth and Peter Gast from the notebooks after his collapse: a first edition in 1901 and a bigger one, 1,067 sections, in 1906. Hatab calls it a "non-book". Heidegger, who built his whole reading on these notes, still calls it a book "falsely ascribed to Nietzsche".
- **The plan was real.** N. did plan a big work with that title. It is announced in GM III 27, and the notebooks hold plans for it from 1885 to 1888. Heidegger lists the subtitles: "Attempt at an Interpretation of All Occurrence" (1885), then "Attempt at a Revaluation of All Values" (1886). In late 1888 the plan turns into *The Antichrist*, and whether he dropped the rest is disputed. The usual story relies on one letter from late 1888. Hatab doesn't think one letter from a shaky period settles it.
- **The editing.** The editors arranged the notes by theme, not date, and sometimes added words. Two examples I checked in Kaufmann's notes:
    - WP 688 begins "My theory would be: that the will to power is the primitive form of affect". Kaufmann marks "My theory would be" as the editors' words, not N.'s, and Heidegger, quoting the passage, repeats the warning.
    - WP 702 has the protoplasm stretching out "not from hunger but from will to power". Every edition dropped the next sentence in the manuscript: "Duality as the consequence of too weak a unity."
- **WP 1067, the famous ending,** has two versions. The earlier one ends with a "ring of good will" and asks whether one could will back everything that has been and will forward everything that must be. N. crossed that out and wrote "This world is the will to power--and nothing besides! And you yourselves are also this will to power--and nothing besides!" So the line most often quoted as N.'s view of the world is a revision of a recurrence passage (see [[Nietzsche: Eternal Recurrence]]).
- **How much published text is there?** Hatab counts 32 aphorisms in the books and 147 notebook entries, less than 5% of everything N. wrote. Smaller than I expected for something this famous.
- **Elisabeth's legend.** She told a story that N. got the idea in 1870 as a medical orderly, watching a tired cavalry regiment ride into battle: life, he saw, is not a struggle to survive but a will to power. Kaufmann says this is "remarkably at variance" with the books. N. didn't write about WtP for nearly ten years after that, and when he did it was in a psychological note.
- **Who trusts the notebooks?**
    - Trust them and you get N. the metaphysician (Heidegger, Schacht, Richardson).
    - Trust only the books and you mostly don't (Jaspers, Nehamas, Clark).
    - Golomb calls the notebooks a "thought laboratory" (Kaufmann's phrase), to be used with care.
    - Hatab: you can't settle N.'s intentions, so treat it as a question of interpretation, and on that basis WtP is central.
- Hatab also makes a slightly cynical point I liked: how much weight a scholar gives WtP tends to track how comfortable they are with the idea.

### 2. Where It Came From: The Feeling of Power

Kaufmann's ch. 6 is the best account of how the idea grew. It started as psychology, and not as praise of power.

- **Power as evil.** The young N. took the old view that power corrupts. Kaufmann quotes the end of *Richard Wagner in Bayreuth* (1876): "Who of you will renounce power, knowing and experiencing that power is evil?" Wagner himself was N.'s first close study of a man driven by power and of how that drive could turn into art.
- **The first note,** from the late 1870s (in Kaufmann's translation): "Fear (negative) and will to power (positive) explain our strong consideration for the opinions of men." Power here is one motive alongside fear.
- **HH: unmasking.** In HH N. starts tracing "good" motives back to wanting power. Gratitude is "a milder form of revenge" (HH 44). People who seek pity want "the power to hurt" (HH 50). HH 87 rewrites the Gospel: "Luke 18:14 corrected. He that humbleth himself wills to be exalted." Kaufmann sees here the first hint of a monism, everything explained by one drive.
- **A note from the same years:** "One strives for independence (freedom) for the sake of power, not the other way around."
- **D (1881) is the experiment.** Gast confirms N. was consciously trying out fear and power as explanations. Kaufmann's key passages:
    - D 113, "The Striving for Excellence": the striving "to overwhelm one's neighbor", which runs from the barbarian who tortures to the ascetic who torments himself. N. even thinks happiness, "taken as the most alive feeling of power", may have been greatest in the souls of superstitious ascetics.
    - D 348: whoever lacks the feeling of power will use any means to get it, but whoever has it "has become very choosy and noble in his tastes".
    - D 371: only the weak man wishes to hurt.
    - D 262, "The Demon of Power": the love of power is "the demon of man".
    - D 548: in a contest of strengths "only the degree of reason in strength is decisive".
- **The Greeks.** Kaufmann calls "one of the most decisive steps" N.'s realization that the Greeks admitted their WtP openly, and that their contest, the agon, was itself a form of it. The early essay "Homer's Contest" says that man "in his highest and most noble capacities, is wholly nature and embodies its uncanny dual character." (More in [[Nietzsche and the Greeks]] and section 13 below.)
- **The tension Kaufmann spots.** D 262 sets power against reason, a dualism. D 548 makes reason a degree of power, a monism. N. ends up choosing the monism: rational self-control is the highest power, not its opponent. That choice is what makes WtP a single principle.

### 3. What the Published Books Say

Laid out in order, because the published claims are much narrower than the notebook ones.

- **Z I 15, "On the Thousand and One Goals"** is the first public appearance. "A tablet of things held to be good hangs over every people. Behold, it is the tablet of its overcomings; behold, it is the voice of its will to power." Kaufmann's point: WtP enters N.'s published work as the will to *overcome oneself*. A people's morality records what it found hard and mastered.
- **Z I 11, "On the New Idol":** "State is the name for the coldest of all cold monsters." This comes before WtP is even named, and it matters for Golomb (section 8).
- **Z II 12, "On Self-Overcoming"** is the main statement.
    - "Where I found the living, there I found will to power; and even in the will of one who serves I found a will to be master."
    - Life tells Zarathustra: "I am that which must always overcome itself."
    - "Only, where Life is, there too is will: though not will to life, but--thus I teach you--will to power! Much is valued by the living more highly than life itself".
    - The will to truth of the "wisest" is itself a will to make all being thinkable, so even philosophy is WtP.
    - Kaufmann stresses that this is offered as something learned from experience ("Thus did Life once teach me"), not deduced.
- **BGE 13:** "Anything that lives wants above all to discharge its strength--life itself is will to power--: self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent consequences of this." Aimed at Spinoza and at physiologists who take self-preservation as the basic drive.
- **GS 349:** "The wish to preserve oneself is the symptom of a condition of distress, of a limitation of the really fundamental instinct of life which aims at the expansion of power". N. says Darwinism's struggle for existence is the exception, not the rule. Kaufmann's note links this to TI: "One should not mistake Malthus for nature."
- **GM II 12:** modern biology (Herbert Spencer) has put "adaptation" in front, "a second-class activity, a mere reactivity", and so missed "the essence of life, its will to power", the "spontaneous, attacking, infringing, reinterpreting, reordering and shaping powers, upon whose effect 'adaptation' first follows". Johnson's *Anti-Darwinism* builds on this: N.'s quarrel with Darwin isn't over evolution but over whether life is passive (selected) or active (forming).
- **BGE 6:** "For every drive is bent on ruling: and as such it attempts to philosophize." Each drive wants to be "the rightful master of all other drives".
- **BGE 9:** philosophy "always creates the world in its own image... philosophy is this tyrannical drive itself, the most spiritual will to power".
- **BGE 19,** the analysis of willing (section 9).
- **BGE 22,** physicists' "laws of nature" reread as power: "every power draws its final consequence in every moment". Then: "Supposing this too is only interpretation--and you will be eager enough to make this objection?--well then, so much the better."
- **BGE 23:** psychology should become "morphology and doctrine of the development of the will to power".
- **BGE 36,** the one published piece of cosmology, hedged at every step (section 11).
- **BGE 259:** exploitation "belongs to the essence of what lives, as organic basic function, it is a consequence of the actual will to power, which is simply the will of life."
- **A 2 (1888):** "What is good? Everything that heightens the feeling of power in man, the will to power, power itself. What is bad? Everything that is born of weakness. What is happiness? The feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome."
- **A 6:** "Life itself is to my mind the instinct for growth, for durability, for an accumulation of forces, for power: where the will to power is lacking there is decline."

So in public, WtP is a claim about *life* (Z, BGE 13, GS 349, GM II 12, BGE 259), about *psychology and morality* (Z I 15, BGE 6, 9, 23) and about *value* (A 2, 6). The claim about all being appears only in BGE 36, as a "supposing", and BGE 22 calls it an interpretation.

### 4. Hatab's Map of Readings

Useful as a map. WtP has been read as:

1. Celebration of force and domination (the fascist appropriation)
2. A metaphysical thesis, the end point of Western metaphysics (Heidegger)
3. A theory about drives, not persons
4. Psychology of motivation and self-control
5. Something about agency and norms
6. Self-creation
7. A non-metaphysical plurality of force relations
8. Sublimation, not domination
9. Naturalistic empirical theory
10. Tied to valuing and life affirmation
11. Dispensable

### 5. Why "to Power"? Drives and Their Ends

The obvious objection: nobody wants "power" as such. We want food, love, a finished book. So in what sense is all willing will to power?

| Who | WtP is |
|---|---|
| Kaufmann | self-overcoming; whatever is wanted is wanted as a way of growing |
| Clark | a second-order desire: wanting to be able to satisfy your other desires |
| Richardson | every *drive* tends to grow, to raise its own activity to a higher level |
| Reginster | desire to overcome **resistance** in pursuing something, and to feel yourself doing it |

- **Clark's version** is the most sensible but misses something. Power as capacity could be satisfied once you have enough. N. insists it never is. WP 689 asks whether "the only reality is the will to grow stronger of every center of force--not self-preservation, but the will to appropriate, dominate, increase, grow stronger".
- **Richardson** makes the key move: "To be a will to power, it must already want something other than power." Drives come with their own ends (sex, eating, knowing). Power is not a further end but the drive's tendency to enhance that very activity. So power "lacks content" of its own, and every will to power is specific. A 11 follows: everyone must invent "his virtue, his categorical imperative".
    - Growth comes in two kinds: inside one episode (doing it more strongly) and across episodes, which is sublimation. WP 312 has cruelty turning into tragic pity and sexual love into *amour-passion*.
    - WP 488: no "substance", "rather something that in itself strives after strengthening, and that wants to 'preserve' itself only indirectly (it wants to surpass itself--)".
    - BGE 175, which Richardson quotes: "Ultimately we love our desire, and not the desired."
- **Reginster** explains why it can never be satisfied: it needs new resistance. WP 696 is his proof-text: "It is not the satisfaction of the will that causes pleasure... but rather the will's forward thrust and again and again becoming master over that which stands in its way. The feeling of pleasure lies precisely in the dissatisfaction of the will". His athlete, mathematician and novelist all want an end, but what they want in wanting it is the overcoming.
- **All of them agree** that power can't be the *only* thing wanted. You need some other end to want power in pursuing it. Reginster: the WtP "cannot be satisfied unless the agent has a desire for something else than power".
- **Poellner on the strong claim.** In the late notes N. holds what Poellner calls the reducibility thesis. WP 663: "All events that result from intention are reducible to the intention to increase power." Poellner thinks this is not incoherent (the pleasure of music isn't a separate thing you add to hearing it) but probably false: people want too many different things. And if it fails for humans, extending it to all of nature is on thin ice.
- **Power over others?** Richardson separates four senses:
    1. others' views of you (fame, love)
    2. some standard others' losses help you reach (wealth, territory)
    3. contest comparison (the athlete, the agon, rank order)
    4. mastery, actually subduing another (wrestling, BGE 259)
    - He thinks N. means (4), but then reads mastery as **incorporation** (*Einverleibung*): drive A rules B when it has turned B toward A's end, so that B now takes part in A's activity. The best growth keeps the ruled forces' own character, which enriches the ruler. BGE 230 calls spirit most like a stomach, and the spirit wants "to be master in and around itself".
    - Kaufmann's tamer reading he compares to a Greek gymnasium. Richardson thinks it rounds off N.'s hard edges too neatly, but agrees that the agon and spirit soften the brutality.

### 6. Power against Pleasure

Kaufmann's ch. 9 sets WtP against hedonism, and against the English.

- **The slogan.** TI Maxims 12: "Man does not strive for pleasure; only the Englishman does." WM 704 (Kaufmann): "Not for pleasure does man strive: but for power".
- **Pleasure is a symptom, not a goal.** A 2 defines happiness as "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome". WP 688 says it is "notably enlightening to posit power in place of individual 'happiness'"; pleasure is only a symptom of the feeling of power achieved.
- **Displeasure is built in.** WP 702: "every victory, every feeling of pleasure, every event, presupposes a resistance overcome." The protoplasm stretches out its pseudopodia "in search of something that resists it--not from hunger but from will to power... what one calls 'nourishment' is merely a derivative phenomenon, an application of the original will to become stronger." Displeasure isn't the enemy of power but its stimulant.
- **Kaufmann's three terms.** He distinguishes happiness (the state desired), joy (its conscious side) and pleasure (absence of pain). "Happiness is the fusion of power and joy--and joy contains not only ingredients of pleasure but also a component of pain." WM 699: a kind of joy comes from a rhythm of small displeasures overcome, "tickling", even coitus, "this play of resistance and victory". Compare the midnight song in Z IV: joy is "deeper yet than agony".
- **Against Schopenhauer.** GS 127: "When Schopenhauer assumed that all that has being is only a willing, he enthroned a primeval mythology." He never analyzed will. N. says that will proper needs an idea of pleasure and displeasure, which "the vast majority of organisms" lack. Kaufmann notes the irony: N. later says much the same kind of thing about all being in the notebooks.
- **Goethe as the picture.** TI Skirmishes 49: Goethe "disciplined himself into wholeness, he created himself", and N. baptizes this faith "with the name of Dionysus". For Kaufmann this is the end point: the Dionysian is no longer frenzy but "a creative striving that gives form to itself", passion mastered rather than cut out.
- **Z IV 1:** "Do I strive for happiness? I strive for my work." That sums up the chapter for me.

### 7. Self-Overcoming and Sublimation

Kaufmann's ch. 7. His epigraph is WM 382: "I assess the power of a will by how much resistance, pain, and torture it endures and knows how to turn to its advantage."

- **Z I 15 as a definition of morality.** Every morality is a table of overcomings. Not Mill's expediency, not Kant's reason: a morality is what a people made itself become against its own nature.
- **Klages's objection.** If everything is WtP, what overcomes what? Self-overcoming looks like a Christian motif (spirit against flesh) slipped into a monism. Kaufmann's answer is sublimation. The drive isn't defeated by something outside it. It redirects itself.
- **Sublimation (*sublimieren*).** Kaufmann says N. first gave the word its modern sense, before Freud. HH II speaks of sublimated sexuality. BGE 75: "The degree and kind of a person's sexuality reaches up into the ultimate pinnacle of their spirit."
- **D 109 lists six ways to fight a drive:** avoid the occasions; impose regularity; indulge it to satiety and disgust; link it with a painful thought; shift your energies elsewhere; weaken yourself in general, as the ascetic does. Only the last kills the drive, and N. thinks it the worst. D 560: one can dispose of one's drives "like a gardener".
- **Against extirpation.** TI "Morality as Anti-Nature": Christianity cuts out the passions ("if thine eye offend thee, pluck it out"). N. wants them spiritualized, used, not castrated.
- **The hard cases.** Kaufmann deals with Cesare Borgia (BGE 197) and the blond beast. He shows the blond beast is not racial: GM I 11 lists Roman, Arabian, Germanic and Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes and Scandinavian Vikings. Still, I find N. means the admiration for Borgia more than Kaufmann lets on. Sublimation is the goal, but N. prefers an unsublimated strong drive to a weak one. (See [[Nietzsche's Bestiary: Camel, Lion, Ape, Ass and Blond Beast]].)

### 8. Golomb: Kraft, Macht, Gewalt

Golomb's target is the Nazi reading (Bäumler). His move is that N. has three words and the Nazis ran them together.

| German | English | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| *Kraft* | force | raw energy, a potential, quantitative |
| *Macht* | power | force that has been shaped or sublimated, qualitative |
| *Gewalt* | violence | force used to crush, exploit, destroy |

- **Power = sublimated force.** It goes back to *The Birth of Tragedy*: Apollonian form shaping Dionysian drive.
- The key idea is **self-overcoming** (*Selbstüberwindung*): the will keeps negating what it has made and going beyond it.
- What's it for? Maturity, selfhood, being able to make your own values. The ideal case is the [[Nietzsche: The Overman|overman]].
- So what the Nazis called *Macht* is really *Kraft* and *Gewalt*.

Why "force" won't do as the concept (Golomb's three reasons):

1. It's quantitative (from mechanics). Sublimation is a change in quality.
2. Force is conserved. WtP grows.
3. Force follows "nothing from nothing". Self-overcoming gets out more than went in.

My note on (2): in WP 1067 the world as a whole is "a firm, iron magnitude of force that does not grow bigger or smaller". So in the cosmology total force *is* conserved, and what grows is each centre's power relative to the others. That fits Golomb better than it first looks: growth is redistribution and form, not more stuff.

**Limits on violence.** Three limits on power directed outward:

1. It assimilates and transforms. It doesn't wipe out.
2. What gets "taken in" is your experience of the thing, not the thing by conquest.
3. The aim is to change the *agent*, not to remake the other.

- Exploiting people is a sign you **lack** power. It is what insecure people do, out of revenge (compare D 371).
- BGE 259: among equals a healthy aristocracy holds back from injury and exploitation. GM II 8: justice is a settlement between roughly equal powers.

**Positive and negative power**

- Everybody has power. Strong and weak differ in *how* it comes out, not how much.
- Positive: autonomous, spontaneous, doesn't need to dominate.
- Negative: a weak personality that needs the feeling of power and gets it from other people (pity, guilt, ressentiment).
- The ascetic does *Gewalt* to himself, one drive tyrannizing the others. The overman sublimates, the ascetic represses.
- This answers the obvious objection (if slave morality is weak, how did it win?). Real power is spiritual, and spirit loses to brute force and to manipulation. N. separates the *history of power* (culture) from the *history of force*. GM III 15 calls the priest's war "a war of cunning (of the 'spirit') more than of force".
- A fully autonomous WtP is only a regulative ideal, never finished.
- "Coldest of all cold monsters" is the state (Z I 11). Golomb's answer to his title question, whether WtP leads there: no.

My worry: Golomb's N. comes out very tidy. The *Kraft*/*Macht*/*Gewalt* scheme is neat, but Hatab admits N. "does not always distinguish" them, and BGE 259 calls exploitation the essence of life, not a failure of it.

### 9. Poellner: The Argument from Causation

A different question: what could *justify* saying the world is WtP? Poellner starts from causation, not psychology.

1. The ordinary idea of a cause includes **compulsion**: the cause *makes* the effect happen.
2. Hume: we never perceive that. We get used to sequences and project our expectation.
3. N. agrees we have no "sense" for efficient causes. But what we project is **agency**, not habit. We can't help seeing every event as a deed with a doer (WP 550).
4. Against regularity theories: if causation is only regular succession between unconnected things, the order of the world is a brute fact, basically a miracle. N.: science describes better and better but explains nothing (GS 112).
5. Against mechanism: after Boscovich (and Lange), matter is centres of force, not solid lumps. So bumping into things doesn't show you what force *is*. We only get effects "translated into a completely foreign language" (WP 620).
6. So the only place we could be acquainted with force is **our own willing**. Things count as real for us because they *resist*, and that presupposes we experience ourselves as agents.

The conclusion is a fork. Either the inside of causation is a total mystery ("force we cannot imagine is an empty word", WP 621), or it is something like will. The notebooks take the second path.

**Where in willing?** BGE 19 says: "Willing to me seems above all to be something complicated, something that is a unity only in word". It breaks an act of will into a plurality of feelings, a commanding thought, and "above all an affect: and specifically that affect of command". Freedom of the will is "essentially the affect of superiority with respect to the one who must obey". GS 347 adds: "will, as the affect of command, is the decisive sign of sovereignty and strength." The force is supposed to be in the commanding. Poellner finds three models of it:

- being bound by a norm you accept ("I must")
- mental effort, holding to a purpose against other pulls
- resistance of objects to what we want

**The problem.** N. isn't consistent. TI "Four Great Errors" 3: "The 'inner world' is full of phantoms and will-o'-the-wisps: the will is one of them. The will no longer moves anything, hence does not explain anything either--it merely accompanies events; it can also be absent." So the published N. says we only *believe* the will is effective, while the notebook N. builds a cosmology on it. Poellner thinks this wobble is why interpreters disagree so much.

### 10. The Notebook Cosmology: Force Quanta and the Monster of Energy

This is the part the published books only hint at. Poellner's sec. 4, Kaufmann's "physics" and "monadology" (WM 618–39), and Lightbody's ch. 3 cover it.

- **Force needs an inside.** WP 619: "The victorious concept 'force', by means of which our physicists have created God and the world, still needs to be completed: an inner will must be ascribed to it, which I designate as 'will to power,' i.e., as an insatiable desire to manifest power". All motion and all "laws" become symptoms of an inner event, with man as the analogy.
- **No things, only quanta.** WP 635: remove the added ideas (thing, subject, atom) and "no things remain but only dynamic quanta, in a relation of tension to all other dynamic quanta: their essence lies in their relation to all other quanta". Then the line: "The will to power not a being, not a becoming, but a pathos--the most elemental fact from which a becoming and effecting first emerge". (Kaufmann's note: *pathos* covers occasion, event, passion, suffering, destiny.)
- **A thing is what it does.** WP 634: "A quantum of power is designated by the effect it produces and that which it resists. The adiaphorous state is missing, though it is thinkable." Nothing is indifferent to anything else. Every atom "affects the whole of being". Also: "There is no law: every power draws its ultimate consequence at every moment." That is BGE 22 again.
- **Resistance.** WP 656: "The will to power can manifest itself only against resistances; therefore it seeks that which resists it--this is the primeval tendency of the protoplasm when it extends pseudopodia and feels about." Appropriation is "a forming, shaping and reshaping" until the overwhelmed thing has gone over into the aggressor's domain.
- **Perspective all the way down.** WP 636: physicists forgot "this necessary perspectivism by virtue of which every center of force--and not only man--construes all the rest of the world from its own viewpoint, i.e., measures, feels, forms, according to its own force".
- **Organisms as societies.** The body is a "sociality" of drives commanding and obeying (BGE 19: "our body is after all only a society constructed of many souls"). In a note Poellner quotes, "the whole organism thinks", and consciousness becomes something like a figurehead monarch.
- **The world as a whole.** WP 1067: "a monster of energy, without beginning, without end; a firm, iron magnitude of force that does not grow bigger or smaller", "a sea of forces flowing and rushing together, eternally changing, eternally flooding back, with tremendous years of recurrence". The cosmology of WtP and the cosmology of recurrence are the same cosmology.

**What it amounts to (Poellner).** An inference to the best explanation, and panpsychist: mentality is the basic inside of the real, as in Schopenhauer and later Eddington. Its consequences:

- reality is perspectival (WP 556, 560, 567)
- no thing in itself: "The properties of a thing are effects on other 'things'... there is no thing without other things" (WP 557)
- a strong becoming thesis: no enduring entities at all

Poellner's objection: even granting that force must have an inside like will, it doesn't follow that it is a will to *power*. We can imagine a will with different ends.

**Porter's objection (via Lightbody).** "A thing is the sum of its effects" already presupposes grouping effects together, which needs a subject. And WtP totalizes the world ("and nothing besides!") while denying any totality can be grasped. So Porter reads it as a "parable" alerting us to our own totalizing instinct. Lightbody calls this a false dilemma. "This world is the will to power" is not a totalization but an *announcement*, a way of telling the reader what the world looks like "from inside" (BGE 36).

### 11. Is the Metaphysics Meant?

The central quarrel. BGE 36 is the key text, so here it is in full outline (Del Caro):

- "Supposing nothing else is 'given' as real except our world of desires and passions"
- then try whether this given is enough to understand the mechanistic world too, and whether causality of the will is the only kind
- "wherever 'effects' are recognized, will is affecting will"
- "Supposing finally that we were to succeed in explaining our entire life of drives as the taking shape and ramification of a basic form of the will--namely of the will to power, as my proposition has it"
- then "The world seen from inside, the world determined and characterized on the basis of its 'intelligible character'--it would be precisely 'will to power' and nothing else."

Every clause is a supposition. Four readings of what that means:

1. **Clark: a reductio.** N. rejects both premises: that only desires are "given" (BGE 16, no immediate certainties) and that the will is causal (TI Errors 3). So BGE 36 shows what you'd get *if* you thought like a metaphysician. BGE 5 says metaphysicians are defending "a fervent desire rendered abstract and sifted for reasons they seek after the fact". WtP is N.'s own fervent desire, a "construction of the world from the viewpoint of his moral values", and BGE 22's "so much the better" is a confession.
    - Lightbody's objections: Clark runs will and desire together. Desire doesn't need certainty. And if the will isn't effective at all, N.'s ethics (GS 290, "To 'give style' to one's character--a great and rare art!") collapses.
2. **Poellner: a real conflict.** Why would N. fill private notebooks with detailed cosmology if it was only a performance? Poellner thinks N. officially holds "metaphysical indifferentism":
    - BGE 4: the falsity of a judgment is no objection to it. The question is how far it advances life.
    - BGE 34: "Why shouldn't the world that concerns us--be a fiction?"
    - But N. also has a residual desire that what he values should be written into "the nature of things". His metaphysics "inscribes in the nature of the world at large that which he... values above all: the conscious will to power".
3. **Richardson: two stages.** In *System* (1996) WtP is foundational, a real ontology of drives. In *Nietzsche's New Darwinism* (2004) he deflates it. WtP is embarrassing because mentalist (it reads mind into all things) and unlikely true. What survives is power *biology*: drives aimed at control are what selection has favoured.
4. **Kaufmann: psychology first.** WtP is "first and foremost the key concept of a psychological hypothesis". He admits the objection to the cosmic extension is "in the end, unanswerable".

My view: Clark is right that BGE 36 is hedged and Poellner is right that the hedging isn't the whole story. The notebooks read like someone trying to make it work, not someone mocking metaphysics. But N. never let it into print except as a supposition, and that restraint is also part of his view.

### 12. Heidegger: Will to Will

Heidegger reads WtP as the last word of Western metaphysics. Vol. I, §§7 and 10.

- **One question, not two.** "What does 'will' mean? What does 'will to power' mean? For Nietzsche these two questions are but one." Will is nothing but WtP, and power nothing but the essence of will. "Hence, will to power is will to will, which is to say, willing is self-willing."
- **Not a faculty, not a cause.** If will is the being of everything, it can't be explained by something more basic. It is not a faculty of the soul (the soul is itself will) and not a kind of cause (causing is grounded in will). Hence N. can say "Today we know that it is merely a word" and, in a Zarathustra-period note, "what you call will is to me an illusion; there is no will". He means there is no will of the old kind.
- **Not wishing, not striving.** A boy who wants a motorbike *wishes*. Whoever only wishes "hopes that his wish will come true without his having to do anything about it". Willing "is the submission of ourselves to our own command", and it is always determinate: "Someone who does not know what he wants does not want anything". Striving can be vague. Will is "resolute openness to oneself" and so always "a willing out beyond oneself".
- **Command.** Only he can truly command "who is always ready and able to place himself under command". This is Z II 12 again: whoever cannot obey himself is commanded.
- **So "to power" isn't a goal.** "Will is power; power is will." Power is not something set outside the will for it to aim at. The phrase "to power" spells out what will already is. When N. says power rather than pleasure is the goal, he is changing the definition of will, not just its target.
- **"Every willing is a willing to be more."** Power is only power so long as it wills more power. "Simply holding onto the position already attained will not do". So WtP is creative, and creation includes destruction: "the need to destroy belongs essentially to creation". Heidegger brings in Hegel's "monstrous power of the negative" and WM 416, where N. praises Hegel's pantheism, "in which evil, error, and suffering are not felt to be arguments against divinity".
- **Dynamis, energeia, entelecheia.** Power in N. means all three of Aristotle's terms at once: capacity, being-at-work and reaching one's end. N. didn't see the link, but his doctrine "has more to do with" Aristotle's *Metaphysics* Θ than with any modern theory of categories. Compare [[Aristotle and God as Pure Act]]: Aristotle's god is pure *energeia* with no potential left, and for N. that would be the end of power, since power needs resistance (section 13).
- **The hammer.** "Philosophizing with the hammer" means tapping things to hear whether they ring hollow, and also sculpting, "to give things weight and importance again". Not wrecking.
- **Vol. II: WtP and ER.** WtP names what beings are (*essentia*). Eternal recurrence names how they are (*existentia*). A note of 1885: "Will to power is the ultimate fact we come down to". WM 617: "To stamp Becoming with the character of Being--that is the supreme will to power." So the two doctrines are one metaphysics and it is a mistake to play one off against the other. Heidegger's verdict is that N. inverts Platonism and stays inside it (see [[Nietzsche: Eternal Recurrence]] §15).

What I take from Heidegger: power as self-surpassing rather than as a state you have. What I distrust: he makes N. a systematic metaphysician mainly on the strength of the notebooks, and his N. ends up as the thinker of technology's will to will, which flattens the psychology.

### 13. Hatab: Resistance and the Agon

This was the chapter that made the most sense to me.

**Starting point.** The death of God (GS 125) is addressed to *unbelievers* who haven't seen what follows. Morality, truth, even science were underwritten by God, so they are his "shadows" (GS 108). Metaphysics lives on "the faith in the opposition of values" (BGE 2): being/becoming, reason/passion, good/evil. WtP is N.'s way of redescribing meaning and value inside natural life, with the opposites tied together.

**WtP is a relation, not a thing.**

- Not a substance, not Schopenhauer's one Will. "There is no will", only "will-points" gaining and losing power. Will is "a unity only in word" (BGE 19).
- WP 635: "not a being, not a becoming, but a pathos".
- Hatab: the term has a *nominal* function. It points at a network of tensions, and you shouldn't turn it into an entity.

**Resistance.** This is the main thing.

- WtP "can manifest itself only against resistances; therefore it seeks that which resists it" (WP 656).
- So if you destroy what resists you, your power evaporates. **Total domination defeats itself.**
- It's will *toward* power: aimed at the activity of overcoming, not an end state.
- Hence N. against "happiness" as the measure of life. Displeasure is built into overcoming. Pleasure is a symptom of power achieved, not the goal.
- Even pacifism counts as WtP (overcoming one's own savagery).

**The agon.** The source is the early essay "Homer's Contest".

- Greek contest culture: athletics, drama, rhetoric, politics. Competition produces excellence because you *don't* annihilate the opponent.
- Ostracism was the removal of someone too dominant, to keep the contest going.
- So WtP has a kind of **measure** in it. Not a fixed rule, but "laws and measures immanent in the contest".
- Agon against violence: violence wants to *end* the conflict by removing the other side. That's anti-agonistic.
- N. applies this to himself. He fights the Christian ideal to end its tyranny, not to destroy it. New ideals need strong opponents. "we immoralists and Antichristians, find our advantage in this, that the church exists" (TI, "Morality as Anti-Nature" 3).
- Hatab maps this onto the same words as Golomb: natural WtP = *Kraft*/*Gewalt*, cultural WtP = *Macht*. Also *Macht* is related to *machen* (to make): power-for more than power-over. Zarathustra's "bestowing virtue" (Z I 22) is the image: "a bestowing virtue is the highest virtue", gold "always bestows itself".

A nice line of thought: if power became pure actuality with no resistance (the old dream of "being"), it would stop being power. Compare [[The Unmoved Mover]], which is exactly pure act.

**Affirmation.** Why Z ties willing recurrence to WtP: if meaning always involves what you oppose, then to affirm your life you must affirm the return of what you oppose too. But affirming isn't approving. Zarathustra wills the return of the small man *and* of his fight against him.

### 14. Interpretation and Perspective

- **"No facts."** WP 481: "Against positivism, which halts at phenomena--'There are only facts'--I would say: No, facts is precisely what there is not, only interpretations." And: "'Everything is subjective,' you say; but even this is interpretation."
- **Interpreting is a form of WtP.** BGE 6, 9 and WP 636: each drive, each philosophy, each centre of force construes the world from its own position and wants that view to rule. GM II 12 makes interpretation part of mastery: reinterpreting is one of the "shaping powers".
- **Not relativism, though.** Hatab: absolutism = one truth nobody may contest; relativism = many truths nobody may contest. Both are non-agonistic. Perspectives *fight*.
- **More eyes.** GM III 12: "the more eyes, different eyes we know how to engage for the same thing, the more perfect will be our 'concept' of this thing, our 'objectivity.'" Objectivity is not disinterested looking but the ability to control your pro and con and use them.
- **Self-refutation.** Isn't WtP itself only an interpretation? BGE 22: "so much the better". On the agon model that makes sense: he wants opposition. On Clark's reading it is a confession. I lean to Hatab here, but I think it does partly dodge the question.
- **Lightbody's version.** Following Hales and Welshon, he holds a "weak perspectivism": some claims are true across perspectives, and WtP is one. "We do not have perspectives... rather we are in them." Error comes from within, from how our drives space out the world, not from a gap between mind and world. See [[Relativism]] and [[Zhuangzi: Perspectives, the Butterfly Dream and the Useless Tree]] for the comparison.

### 15. Lightbody: Will to Power Naturalized

Lightbody's whole book argues that WtP is what makes N. a naturalist without making him a scientist.

- **The problem.** N. wants to "translate man back into nature" (BGE 230, *homo natura*). But he also attacks science: "physics too is only a world interpretation and arrangement (by us! if I may say so) and not a world explanation" (BGE 14). Leiter's methodological naturalism makes N. continuous with science. Lightbody thinks that leaves no role for WtP, and that only WtP can join causes and reasons.
- **Three rival solutions he rejects:**
    - Clark: WtP expresses N.'s values. This "neuters" N.
    - Richardson: power biology on Darwin's frame. This leaves WtP no work to do.
    - Linda Williams: WtP is a mirror in which you see whether you affirm life. This denies it any truth.
- **His ontology: "non-physical emergentism".** Power emerges from relations of force when a relation relates to itself. "Power is simply that relation which relates to itself as a force." Force describes the non-conscious, power the conscious. A thing is a real organization of relations, not a bundle we make up. His example is acid rain, which needs both chemistry and politics to describe.
- **His epistemology: "virtue foundherentism"** (after Susan Haack). Genealogy is justified by evidence plus coherence plus comprehensiveness. GM II's story of guilt explains more than the hedonist's story does.
- **His ethics: how is change possible?** If we are just drives, as on Leiter's reading, how can anyone change their character? Lightbody's answer is "character reflection", built on feelings (after Colin McGinn's "selective cognition"). The self is a permeable membrane. GM II 16 gives the image: "The whole inner world, originally thin as if stretched between two membranes" grew deeper as outward discharge was blocked. The priests changed the rules of the game from force to "cunning" (GM III 15).
- **His conclusion.** WtP translates man into nature *and* nature into man. Genealogy studies how values come to have value.

He is clearer than most and I like the reply to Porter. But "non-physical emergentism" is doing a lot of work for a phrase, and he concedes akrasia remains a problem for any drive account.

### Conclusion

- **What I now hold.**
  1. WtP started as psychology: the *Daybreak* experiment with fear and the feeling of power. It became the claim that life aims at growth rather than survival, and in the notebooks it became a cosmology of force quanta. The published books stop at life and value, and BGE 36 keeps even that as a supposition.
  2. Power is not a separate goal. Richardson and Heidegger agree on this from opposite ends. Every drive wants something specific, and power is its tendency to do that thing more and beyond itself. That is why it never ends and why it needs resistance.
  3. Power is not domination, but it isn't gentle either. Golomb and Hatab are right that destroying the opponent defeats power and that exploitation is a sign of weakness. But BGE 259 and Richardson's mastery reading keep the hard edge. Sublimation is the goal, not a softening.
  4. The metaphysics is half-meant. N. wanted the world to be what he valued (Poellner) and knew this was an interpretation (BGE 22). Clark's reductio is too neat for the notebooks, and Heidegger's systematic N. is too neat for the books.
- **Revisions to my earlier view.** I used to treat the *Kraft*/*Macht*/*Gewalt* scheme as N.'s own. It is a commentator's tidying, useful but not exact. And I hadn't seen how much of the famous "this world is the will to power" comes from the editors' choices: a crossed-out recurrence passage, and a "My theory would be" that N. never wrote.
- **What stays open.**
    - Whether the strong claim (all willing is will to power) can be anything but true by definition. If every want counts as power because every want overcomes something, the thesis tells us nothing. Kaufmann sees this, and his answer is that the qualitative ranks of power do the work.
    - How the cosmology squares with TI's "the will no longer moves anything".
    - Whether the agon can really keep its measure. Hatab's ostracism keeps the contest going, but someone has to decide who is too strong.
