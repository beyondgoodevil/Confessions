Ouspensky was an iconographer as well as a theologian (Paris, mid-20th c., with Lossky). The site's existing icon notes defend icons from Scripture; this is about what icons **are** and how to **read** them.

The thesis: the icon is not religious art in the Western sense (illustration, decoration, devotion). It's **theology in colour**, as Scripture is theology in words, and it carries the same dogmatic content.

## 1. The Church Building

- The temple is an image of the **new creation**, the Kingdom. St. Maximus's *Mystagogy*: the nave is the earth, the sanctuary heaven; or the nave the body, the sanctuary the soul.
- Icons fill it because the whole cosmos is to be transfigured. See [[The Temple and Its Fulfilment]].

## 2. Origins

- The OT forbids idols (Exod 20:4), yet commands images in the tabernacle (cherubim, Exod 25:18). See [[Icons in the OT]].
- The **Incarnation** makes images of God possible: the invisible God has become visible. "He that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (John 14:9). Christ is "the image (*eikōn*) of the invisible God" (Col 1:15).
- **Traditions of the first icons**:
    - the **Mandylion** (Image Not Made by Hands): Christ pressed His face on a cloth for King Abgar of Edessa
    - **St. Luke** painted the Theotokos
- Whatever their history, Ouspensky treats them as expressing a truth: the icon comes from Christ's own self-revelation, not from human invention.

## 3. Early Art

- **Catacombs** (2nd–4th c.): mostly **symbols**: fish (*ichthys*), anchor, the Good Shepherd, Jonah, Daniel, the orant. Already "symbolic" rather than naturalistic: they show meaning, not appearance.
- **Constantinian** era: art goes public, borrowing imperial forms (Christ as emperor-like Pantocrator, mosaics).

## 4. Quinisext Council (692), Canon 82

- Forbade depicting Christ as a **lamb** (the symbol from John the Baptist) and required depicting Him **in human form**. Symbols belonged to the age of shadows; now the reality has come and must be shown directly.
- A key moment: the move from **symbol** to **icon**. See [[Canons of the Sixth Ecumenical Council]].

## 5. Iconoclasm (726–843)

- See [[Iconoclast Emperors]] for the history.
- **Iconoclast argument** (Constantine V, Council of Hieria 754): an image of Christ either depicts His divinity (impossible, uncircumscribable) or His humanity alone (Nestorian division). The only true image is the **Eucharist**.
- **Orthodox reply** (St. John of Damascus, St. Theodore the Studite, Nicaea II 787):
    - the icon depicts the **person** (hypostasis) of Christ, not a nature. The person who is God became circumscribable in the flesh. See [[Nature and Person Distinction in Christology]].
    - the honour passes to the **prototype** (St. Basil): we venerate the person shown, not wood and paint.
    - **veneration** (*proskynesis*) vs **worship** (*latreia*): latreia to God alone.
    - denying the icon denies the reality of the Incarnation.
- **Triumph of Orthodoxy** (843): celebrated on the first Sunday of Lent.

## 6. Meaning and Content of the Icon

The icon shows the world **transfigured**, not the world as it looks to ordinary eyes. Its features follow from that:

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Inverse perspective** | lines converge toward the viewer, not toward a vanishing point behind. The space of the icon opens toward us; we are drawn into it |
| **Light** | no single light source and no cast shadows. Light comes from within: the uncreated light. The gold background is that light (the "Tabor light") |
| **Faces** | large eyes (seeing spiritually), small mouth (silence), thin nose, elongated features: the senses transfigured. Ears often hidden or small |
| **Bodies** | elongated, stylised; not anatomically "realistic", since this is the deified body |
| **Buildings** | shown from several sides at once, often behind the figures; a red cloth (veil) over them shows the scene is inside |
| **Time** | several moments in one picture (the Nativity: birth, shepherds, Magi, the washing) |
| **Inscriptions** | every icon is named; the name links it to the prototype. *ICXC*, *ὁ ὤν* in Christ's halo, *ΜΡ ΘΥ* for the Mother of God. See [[The Name of God]] |
| **Colours** | symbolic: gold (divinity), blue (heaven, the human), red (life, divinity in the Theotokos' cloak), white (the Transfiguration, Resurrection) |
| **No naturalism** | the Renaissance shift to natural perspective and emotion (Western religious painting, and Russian painting after the 17th c.) loses the icon's theology |

- The icon is made in **prayer and fasting**, following a **canon** (prototypes, painters' manuals). Not self-expression.
- It's part of the **liturgy**: venerated, censed, carried in procession, painted for feasts.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The iconoclast controversy was Christological, not about art. That's the strongest point against Protestant iconoclasm: if Christ can be seen, He can be painted.
- Inverse perspective is a good image for the whole Orthodox outlook: the viewer is not the measure of the picture; the picture looks at you.
- See [[The Descent into Hades]] sec. 5 for one icon read in detail.

## Chapter by Chapter

**Introduction**
- Of all the Church's victories over heresy, only the victory of the icon was proclaimed as "the Triumph of Orthodoxy", celebrated every first Sunday of Lent.
- "The icon corresponds entirely to the word of Scripture": the Church's teaching "can be falsified by the image as much as by word".
- Against the Roman practice (Pius XII's "wonderful concert of famous men") of commissioning famous painters, believers or not, to decorate churches. And against Orthodox churches full of "paintings in the Italian style", even Masonic symbols like the "all-seeing eye" in a triangle. Taste is "subjective and changeable"; the icon isn't a matter of taste.

**1. The Symbolism of the Church**
- *Ekklēsia* = convocation, assembly. The consecration prayers call the church "a house comparable to the heavens"; St. Symeon of Thessalonica: "a mysterious heaven and the Church of the first-born".
- The **nave** (from *navis*, ship): the Church as the ark of salvation.
- The building points to the coming **Kingdom**, whose first-fruits are present in the Eucharist. The nave, where the "royal priesthood" (the laity) prays, is at the west; the sanctuary at the east.
- The church's beauty "reflects the harmony of the age to come", different from worldly beauty.

**2. Origins of the Christian Image**
- A distinction between a **wall-painting** and an **icon**.
- Modern scholars claimed the early Christians were hostile to images, citing Fathers who attacked art. Ouspensky: they attacked **pagan** and deceiving art. Clement opposes only images that "fascinate and deceive", "pretending to be truth".
- The OT prohibition: St. John of Damascus explains with Ezek 20:25 ("I gave them statutes that were not good") because of their tendency to idolatry. "You have seen no form; hence make none" means: make no image of God **as long as you have not seen Him** (Deut 4:15-16).
- Now He has been seen: "That which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled" (1 John 1:1).

**3. The First Icons of Christ and the Virgin**
- The **Image Not Made by Hands** (*acheiropoiētos*), the "Holy Face" in the West: King Abgar's painter couldn't paint Christ "because of the indescribable glory of His face", so Christ pressed it on a cloth. Troparion: "Formerly Thou wast seen by men, and now Thou appearest in Thy image not made by human hand."
- The Theotokos "presides with Christ over the destiny of the world" since Ephesus (431).
- Icon types: **Umilenie / Eleousa** ("Our Lady of Tenderness"); "Our Lady of Lydda", also "of Rome" (June 26).
- **St. Luke's** icons: tradition attributed by Theodore the Lector (c. 530).

**4. The Art of the First Centuries**
- Catacomb themes from the OT and NT appear already in the 2nd century.
- The **fish**: first and essentially Christ Himself ("fishers of men"). Tertullian: "we are born in the water like our fish (*ichthys*) Jesus Christ, and we can only be saved by staying in the water" = baptism.
- Even secular scholars admit a **new style** appears already in the catacombs, with the traits of later Church art: the Christian painter **renounces naturalistic representation of space**.

**5. Sacred Art in the Constantinian Epoch**
- Mass conversions needed more direct teaching: the old symbols, meant for initiates, were incomprehensible. St. Basil thought painting more convincing than his own words.
- Contemplation "of the visible Word" isn't passive or aesthetic but contemplation of the Word.
- The Fathers used ancient philosophy for theology; artists used ancient art for the Church. The Didache prayer: "As this bread was scattered over the hills and became one, so let Thy Church be gathered into Thy Kingdom": catholicity in culture.
- Not "art for art's sake".

**6. The Quinisext Council**
- Called "Quinisext" (fifth-sixth) because it completed the disciplinary work of the 5th and 6th councils (held 691-2 in the Trullo hall).
- **Canon 82**: depict Christ in **human form**, not as a lamb. The old symbols are "Jewish hesitancy" now that the reality has come: truth "had to be shown".
- **For the first time** a council links the icon to the dogma of the Incarnation. Canon 82 establishes the "**iconographic canon**", a criterion for the liturgical quality of an image, as the canon of Scripture does for texts. "That this fulfilment might be seen by all."

**7. The Pre-Iconoclastic Period**
- Secular art "lowered" revelation to make it accessible, corrupting it.
- Icons were so honoured they sometimes served as **godparents** at baptism and monastic tonsure.
- Augustine already complains of artists painting Christ according to their imagination. Some argued: how dare one represent the Theotokos "by means of a vile Greek art"?
- Pressures toward iconoclasm: **Islam**, **Judaism**, and **docetic** Christian sects (the Incarnation unreal).

**8. The Iconoclastic Period: A Synopsis**
- Leo III's decrees (726, 730; Ostrogorsky thinks only one). Iconoclasm was partly a tool of **imperial absolutism** against the monasteries.
- First act: destroying the icon of Christ over the palace's Chalke gate.
- Constantine V wrote a treatise and called the council of Hieria (754). The **monks** led the resistance: "idolaters and worshippers of the shadows", Constantine called them.
- The second wave (815–843) was weaker doctrinally; both were the state violating the Church's sovereignty.
- When the Lombards threatened Rome, the Pope turned not to the iconoclast emperor but to **Pepin the Short**, who in 756 created the **Papal States**, making the Pope a temporal ruler.

**9. The Teaching of the Iconoclasts and the Orthodox Response**
- The real difference was the **definition of "icon"**: for the iconoclasts a true image must be **identical** to what it represents (so only the Eucharist qualifies). For the Orthodox an image is essentially **different** from its prototype, connected by likeness and name; so idolatry doesn't arise.
- "The honour rendered to the image passes to its prototype" (St. Basil, *On the Holy Spirit* 18, quoted by Nicaea II).
- Icons are "full of grace": holy objects.
- Iconoclast patristic citations: **Eusebius** (rejected for Arian leanings), **Epiphanius** (John of Damascus: maybe written by someone else using his name, "which many people are in the habit of doing").
- Hieria couldn't be "the Seventh Council" since it disagreed with the six others, especially Quinisext.
- The **Libri Carolini** (Charlemagne's court): "They [the Greeks] place almost all their hope in icons, while we venerate the saints in their body, relics or clothing." The distinction "was never well understood in the West".
- Iconoclasm "undermined the entire economy of salvation" by refusing the sanctification of the visible, material world that follows from the Incarnation.

**10. The Meaning and Content of the Icon**
- The **kontakion of the Triumph of Orthodoxy** sums up the teaching: "No one could describe the Word of the Father; but when He took flesh from you, O Theotokos, He accepted to be described, and restored the fallen image to its former state by uniting it to divine beauty."
- Saints of monastic holiness are called *prepodobnyi* in Slavonic: "very similar" (to God). Gregory the Theologian: "Man is a creature, but he is commanded to become God."
- Dionysius calls God **beauty**: He gives each creature its own beauty and adorns it with divine beauty. True beauty is the radiance of the Holy Spirit.
- **Western art is "Nestorian"**: it shows only the human, earthly side. The icon isn't Monophysite either: it shows neither the divinity alone nor humanity absorbed.
- The **Transfiguration**: seen only by three apostles "capable of receiving" it, and only to the extent they could. Dionysius: "the disorderly is set in order, the formless takes on form, and the man is radiant with a life full of light."
- The icon's aim isn't to provoke natural feeling but to convey grace, which (Palamas) "paints in us... that which is in the divine likeness".
- The fool-for-Christ: "without madness one does not enter into the Kingdom".
- Icon manuals (*podlinniki*) appear later, after the decline.

## Volume 2 (Chapters 11–18)

**11. The Post-Iconoclastic Period**
- After the icon controversy, the central theological question became the **Holy Spirit** and His work in man: the fruit of the Incarnation. Art "spiritualises" (11th c.).
- **St. Photius** fought the remnants of iconoclasm; the council of 879–880 recognised Nicaea II as the **Seventh Ecumenical Council**.
- The Byzantine rite took its definitive form after the Triumph of Orthodoxy.
- An Orthodox church isn't for "solitary meditation" but to include man in the Church's unity, "with one mouth and one heart". Exported Byzantine art was "a preaching of Orthodoxy", and each nation made its own artistic language: unity in diversity.

**12. Hesychasm and Humanism: The Paleologan Renaissance**
- The last Byzantine flowering (14th c.). Two currents: **humanism** (natural reason explaining the faith; Barlaam) and **hesychasm** (Palamas).
- "He that is born of the Spirit is spirit" (John 3:6): Orthodox experience overcomes the dualism of matter and spirit.
- Humanist tendencies appear in art: depth, volume, personified Wisdom in the antique style. Learning can't give "precise information about things divine" and misleads outside its domain.
- The 2nd Sunday of Lent honours Palamas, "the preacher of divine light".

**13. Hesychasm and the Flowering of Russian Art**
- Hesychast influence in Russia (St. Sergius, St. Nilus of Sora); Princes Boris and Gleb as a typically Russian holiness (non-resistance).
- The heresy of the **Strigolniki** (14th c., Pskov/Novgorod), with iconoclastic tendencies, provoked a theology of the icon in reply (Joseph of Volokolamsk's treatises).
- **Andrei Rublev's Trinity**: the Old Testament Trinity (Abraham's three visitors, Gen 18), "God's first covenant with man, and the first revelation of the one triune God".
- **Dionysius**'s frescoes at Therapont: "a beauty not of this world", the visible illustration of hesychast prayer.
- **The iconostasis**: the Royal Doors with the Annunciation and the four Evangelists; Christ at the centre is the key to the whole screen. It shows "the heavenly Liturgy, the eternal eucharistic sacrifice".

**14. The Muscovite Councils of the 16th Century**
- The **Stoglav** council (1551): because "morals are wavering... everyone acts according to his own will". Demanded painting "according to the ancient models".
- The **Viskovatyi affair** (1553–54): the official Viskovatyi objected to new icons (God the Father as an old man, Christ "in the image of David", Christ covered by cherubim's wings on the cross) as painted "according to imagination". The metropolitan defended them; Viskovatyi was condemned. Ouspensky sides with Viskovatyi: he defended iconographic realism, not "the past".
- The case shows a Roman-style division of the Church into a "teaching Church" and a "learning Church": a layman shouldn't question.

**15. The Art of the 17th Century: An Art Divided**
- Western learning and **scholasticism** enter via southwest Russia: Florovsky's "**pseudomorphosis**" of Orthodoxy.
- Control of iconographers moved from spiritual formation and prayer to Church administration and then to the State (charters for "the best iconographers", 1669).
- **Joseph Vladimirov**: the aesthetic criterion is decisive; better a well-painted Christ than many bad icons; why not paint naturalistically, since God gave the Holy Face? Opponents (**Avvakum**, Patriarch Joachim) saw the new realistic faces as "German", i.e. heretical: "the image of Emmanuel... your blasphemous tongue has called it that of a German woman".
- Christ painted "after Latin and Lutheran models, holding an apple or a globe", "which the holy Fathers do not accept".

**16. The Great Council of Moscow and the Image of God the Father**
- The **1666–67 Council** forbade images of **God the Father** as an old man ("Sabaoth"): "paint nothing according to their own ideas".
- The defence: God is shown not in His nature but as in the prophets' visions (Daniel's "Ancient of Days"). Ouspensky: the Ancient of Days in Scripture is the **Son** (cf. John 12:41: Isaiah "saw his glory").
- The **"Paternity"** composition (old man with the young Christ on his knees and the dove) brings anthropomorphism into the Trinity.
- Critique of **Bulgakov**'s sophiological justification ("the Trinity is a tri-hypostatic Person"): it doesn't escape the impasse.
- Florensky: "Rublev's Trinity exists; therefore God exists." The Trinity can only be shown **symbolically** (the three angels; or a throne, book and dove), not with the Father as an old man.

**17. Art in the Russian Church during the Synodal Period**
- Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate (1721): "the Church was decapitated". A "Spiritual College" with a lay "procurator general", "the tsar's eye".
- Western religious art and statuary ("Christ in prison") took over; Catherine II (1767) forbade "scandalous" icons, but "icons in the Italian style" became the fashion.
- The **Encyclical of the Eastern Patriarchs** (1848): "with us it is the very body of the Church, that is, the people, who are the guardians of piety."
- Khomiakov (1846): imitation is sterile.
- The 19th century rediscovered old Russian art as **archaeology**, while some (Golubinskii) saw the Petersburg period as "true civilisation". Icon painting was industrialised.

**18. The Icon in the Modern World**
- Icons were cleaned and rediscovered on the eve of WWI and the Revolution: "theology in images" rediscovered in a time of catastrophe.
- The temptation of a "religious" art venerated like the icon but not Orthodox: the apostles' temptation about "the kingdom of Israel" (Acts 1:6).
- Modern civilisation began by refusing "the ineffable image of glory": a "second fall". The dogma of the icon answers all the heresies against **divine-humanity**: humanism reduces man to "a rational animal, a social being".
- **Light**: Palamas: "God is called light not according to His essence but according to His energy." The light is the basis of the icon's symbolic language.
- The **canon** can't be defined as a set of rules: it's the Church's guidance toward a faithful expression of the faith.
- Protestants, reacting against distorted Catholic images, reject the image altogether: the "image without substance". But the ascetic warning against images concerns **mental images** made by the imagination, not the icon, the image "of the true, non-fictional Incarnation of God the Word".
