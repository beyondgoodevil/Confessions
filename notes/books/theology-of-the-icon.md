Ouspensky was an iconographer as well as a theologian (Paris, mid-20th c., with Lossky). The site's existing icon notes defend icons from Scripture; this is about what icons **are** and how to **read** them.

The thesis: the icon is not religious art in the Western sense (illustration, decoration, devotion). It's **theology in colour**, as Scripture is theology in words, and it carries the same dogmatic content.

## 1. The Church Building

- The temple is an image of the **new creation**, the Kingdom. St. Maximus's *Mystagogy*: the nave is the earth, the sanctuary heaven; or the nave the body, the sanctuary the soul.
- Icons fill it because the whole cosmos is to be transfigured. See [[The Temple and Its Fulfilment]].

## 2. Origins

- The OT forbids idols (Exod 20:4), yet commands images in the tabernacle (cherubim, Exod 25:18). See [[Icons in the OT]].
- The **Incarnation** makes images of God possible: the invisible God has become visible. "He that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (John 14:9). Christ is "the image (*eikōn*) of the invisible God" (Col 1:15).
- **Traditions of the first icons**:
    - the **Mandylion** (Image Not Made by Hands): Christ pressed His face on a cloth for King Abgar of Edessa
    - **St. Luke** painted the Theotokos
- Whatever their history, Ouspensky treats them as expressing a truth: the icon comes from Christ's own self-revelation, not from human invention.

## 3. Early Art

- **Catacombs** (2nd–4th c.): mostly **symbols**: fish (*ichthys*), anchor, the Good Shepherd, Jonah, Daniel, the orant. Already "symbolic" rather than naturalistic: they show meaning, not appearance.
- **Constantinian** era: art goes public, borrowing imperial forms (Christ as emperor-like Pantocrator, mosaics).

## 4. Quinisext Council (692), Canon 82

- Forbade depicting Christ as a **lamb** (the symbol from John the Baptist) and required depicting Him **in human form**. Symbols belonged to the age of shadows; now the reality has come and must be shown directly.
- A key moment: the move from **symbol** to **icon**. See [[Canons of the Sixth Ecumenical Council]].

## 5. Iconoclasm (726–843)

- See [[Iconoclast Emperors]] for the history.
- **Iconoclast argument** (Constantine V, Council of Hieria 754): an image of Christ either depicts His divinity (impossible, uncircumscribable) or His humanity alone (Nestorian division). The only true image is the **Eucharist**.
- **Orthodox reply** (St. John of Damascus, St. Theodore the Studite, Nicaea II 787):
    - the icon depicts the **person** (hypostasis) of Christ, not a nature. The person who is God became circumscribable in the flesh. See [[Nature and Person Distinction in Christology]].
    - the honour passes to the **prototype** (St. Basil): we venerate the person shown, not wood and paint.
    - **veneration** (*proskynesis*) vs **worship** (*latreia*): latreia to God alone.
    - denying the icon denies the reality of the Incarnation.
- **Triumph of Orthodoxy** (843): celebrated on the first Sunday of Lent.

## 6. Meaning and Content of the Icon

The icon shows the world **transfigured**, not the world as it looks to ordinary eyes. Its features follow from that:

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Inverse perspective** | lines converge toward the viewer, not toward a vanishing point behind. The space of the icon opens toward us; we are drawn into it |
| **Light** | no single light source and no cast shadows. Light comes from within: the uncreated light. The gold background is that light (the "Tabor light") |
| **Faces** | large eyes (seeing spiritually), small mouth (silence), thin nose, elongated features: the senses transfigured. Ears often hidden or small |
| **Bodies** | elongated, stylised; not anatomically "realistic", since this is the deified body |
| **Buildings** | shown from several sides at once, often behind the figures; a red cloth (veil) over them shows the scene is inside |
| **Time** | several moments in one picture (the Nativity: birth, shepherds, Magi, the washing) |
| **Inscriptions** | every icon is named; the name links it to the prototype. *ICXC*, *ὁ ὤν* in Christ's halo, *ΜΡ ΘΥ* for the Mother of God. See [[The Name of God]] |
| **Colours** | symbolic: gold (divinity), blue (heaven, the human), red (life, divinity in the Theotokos' cloak), white (the Transfiguration, Resurrection) |
| **No naturalism** | the Renaissance shift to natural perspective and emotion (Western religious painting, and Russian painting after the 17th c.) loses the icon's theology |

- The icon is made in **prayer and fasting**, following a **canon** (prototypes, painters' manuals). Not self-expression.
- It's part of the **liturgy**: venerated, censed, carried in procession, painted for feasts.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The iconoclast controversy was Christological, not about art. That's the strongest point against Protestant iconoclasm: if Christ can be seen, He can be painted.
- Inverse perspective is a good image for the whole Orthodox outlook: the viewer is not the measure of the picture; the picture looks at you.
