**Epicurus** (341–270 BC) founded his school in a **Garden** outside Athens. Only three letters and some sayings survive whole. **Lucretius** (c. 99–55 BC) put the system into a long Latin poem, *De Rerum Natura* (*On the Nature of Things*), rediscovered in 1417 and a big influence on the Renaissance and early modern materialism.

The purpose of the whole philosophy is **therapeutic**: to free people from fear (of the gods and of death) and so give them tranquillity.

## 1. The Tetrapharmakos

The "four-part cure" (Philodemus's summary):
1. **God** is nothing to fear.
2. **Death** is nothing to worry about.
3. The **good** is easy to get.
4. The **terrible** is easy to endure.

## 2. Knowledge: The Canon

- **Sensations** are always true: they report what reaches us. Errors come from **judgements** we add.
- Plus **preconceptions** (*prolepseis*: general ideas formed from repeated experience) and **feelings** (pleasure and pain).
- Things we can't see (atoms, causes of thunder) are inferred from what we can, as long as nothing we see contradicts them. Often several explanations are allowed: no need to pick one if all fit.

## 3. Physics: Atoms and Void

- "Nothing comes from nothing" and nothing passes into nothing.
- All that exists is **atoms** (indivisible, unchanging, of many shapes) and **void**. The universe is infinite, with infinitely many worlds.
- Atoms fall through the void. To explain collisions (and free will), Epicurus adds the **swerve** (*clinamen*, Lucretius's word): atoms occasionally deviate a tiny bit "at no fixed time or place". Otherwise everything would be fixed by fate. Ancient critics (Cicero) mocked it as uncaused.
- Lucretius's famous image: dust motes dancing in a sunbeam, showing the hidden motion of atoms.

## 4. The Gods

- Gods **exist** (we have a universal preconception of them), but they're blessed and immortal, and so **untroubled**: they don't create, govern or punish. They live in the spaces between worlds.
- Fear of divine punishment is the great evil of religion. Lucretius: "**tantum religio potuit suadere malorum**": "so great the evils religion could induce", on Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia.
- The world arose by atoms colliding over infinite time, not by design. Lucretius gives an early account of how animals fit for survival lasted and monsters died out (book V).
- **Epicurus's riddle** of evil (attributed by Lactantius): is God willing to prevent evil but not able? Then He's not omnipotent. Able but not willing? Then malevolent. Both? Then whence evil? See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].

## 5. The Soul and Death

- The soul is made of very fine **atoms** spread through the body. At death they scatter. No survival.
- **"Death is nothing to us"** (*Letter to Menoeceus*): "where death is, I am not; where I am, death is not". Good and evil are in sensation; death is the end of sensation.
- Lucretius's **symmetry argument**: we don't mind the infinite time before we were born; the time after death is just the same, a mirror held up to us.
- So fear of death is irrational, and with it greed, ambition, and war, which come from fear of death.

## 6. Ethics: Pleasure

- **Pleasure** is the beginning and end of the happy life. But not what people think:
    - **kinetic** pleasure: in motion (eating when hungry)
    - **katastematic** pleasure: a stable state, the **absence of pain** in the body (*aponia*) and of disturbance in the soul (***ataraxia***). This is the highest.
- "When we say pleasure is the end, we don't mean the pleasures of the profligate... but freedom from pain in the body and trouble in the soul."
- So **simple living**: bread and water give the highest pleasure to the hungry. "Send me a pot of cheese, so I can feast when I like."

**Classification of desires**

| Kind | Example | Policy |
|---|---|---|
| natural and necessary | food, shelter, friendship | satisfy: easy |
| natural but not necessary | fancy food, sex | allowed, not pursued |
| neither natural nor necessary (empty) | fame, power, wealth, immortality | eliminate: limitless and painful |

- Virtue is valued because it produces pleasure: you can't live pleasantly without living wisely, honourably and justly.
- **Justice** is a contract "neither to harm nor be harmed". Injustice is bad only because of the fear of being caught.
- "**Live unnoticed**" (*lathe biōsas*): stay out of politics.

## 7. Friendship

- "Of all the things wisdom provides for the blessedness of the whole life, by far the greatest is **friendship**."
- The Garden was a community of friends, including women and slaves. Epicurus's letters are full of affection.
- Is friendship valued for its use or for itself? Epicurus seems to say it begins from need but becomes valued for its own sake: a tension. See [[Friendship and Love in Ancient and Medieval Thought]].

## 8. The Christian Reply

*Mine.*

- Early Christians and Epicureans were **both called atheists** by pagans, but the Fathers rejected Epicurus most of all the schools: Acts 17:18 (Paul meets Epicureans and Stoics at Athens); "let us eat and drink; for to morrow we die" (1 Cor 15:32) is read as the Epicurean motto.
- Lactantius (*On the Anger of God*) answers the riddle of evil and the idle gods: a God who doesn't care about good and evil isn't God.
- **Death is nothing to us** misses the point: death is the loss of the good of life, and of the person. Christianity agrees death shouldn't be feared, but because it's **defeated**, not because it's nothing. See [[1 Corinthians 15:26: The Last Enemy That Will Be Destroyed Is Death]].
- The ascetic side (simple living, limiting desire, friendship, self-examination) is close to monastic practice. The desert Fathers and Epicurus both treat "empty desires" as the source of anxiety; but for the Fathers the cure is love of God, not tranquillity. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- Modern materialism (Gassendi, Hobbes) and the Enlightenment revived Lucretius. See [[Descartes: The Cogito and Mind-Body Dualism]].
