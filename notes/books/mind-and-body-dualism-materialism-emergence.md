Part VI of the Handbook. Three long chapters and I only read the opening and the main sections of each, so this is a map and a vocabulary list more than a full account. Interesting that two of the three authors are not materialists (Robinson is a dualist, Zimmerman says "most days of the week I am a dualist myself"), and the third, Kim, is the materialist who made life hardest for other materialists. Last section is mine.

## 1. The Positions

| View | Says | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Substance dualism** | I am (or have) an immaterial thing that thinks and feels | how do mind and body interact? |
| **Property dualism** | one kind of substance, two irreducible kinds of property | does the mental do anything, or is it along for the ride? |
| **Reductive materialism** (identity theory) | mental states *are* brain states | what it's like; multiple realizability |
| **Functionalism** | mental states are defined by what they *do*; the brain "realizes" them | same worry about what it's like |
| **Emergentism** | mind is a new kind of property arising from complex matter, not predictable from it | "arises" how? |
| **Eliminativism** | there are no beliefs or pains, strictly | hard to believe (literally) |
| **Idealism** | only minds and their contents | |

## 2. Zimmerman: If I'm Material, Which Thing Am I?

His definitions:

- **Dualism**: no human being is composed entirely of the kind of stuff rocks and trees are made of. Each of us is a substance that has experiences and thoughts.
- **Materialism**: each of us is something all of whose parts are made of that stuff.
- **"Sensible materialism"**: I'm one of the *likely candidates*, a thing with natural boundaries that includes whatever my thinking most directly depends on. The whole organism, the nervous system, the brain, the cerebrum, maybe one hemisphere.

He sets dualism aside for the chapter and asks whether any version of sensible materialism works.

**Chisholm's *entia successiva* argument**

- My body gains and loses matter constantly. So it's a "successive entity": made of different stuff at different times, with different masses of matter "standing in" for it.
- If I am my body, a different thing does my feeling sad for me today than did it yesterday.
- But when I feel sad, nothing else is doing my feeling for me. So I'm not an *ens successivum*.
- Every likely candidate (organism, brain...) *is* one. So sensible materialism is false.

Zimmerman tightens it into two premises about me and the mass of matter that makes me up right now. The materialist has to deny one. Each denial has a cost.

**Too many minds.** Say both the organism and the mass of matter are here and both have what it takes to think. Then:

- *too many thoughts*: two pains where there should be one
- *too many thinkers*: two subjects, each thinking "I'm in pain", who can't tell which one they are

Escape routes he goes through:

| Move | Idea | His complaint |
|---|---|---|
| **Temporal parts** | strictly only my *present stage* is in pain; longer things are in pain by having that stage | then I never have my experiences directly |
| **Coincidentalism** ("the new dualism") | person and matter are two things in one place, alike atom for atom, but only one thinks | how can things identical in microstructure differ in powers? |
| **Shared tropes** | one pain-instance shared by both | rejected |
| **No masses of matter** (van Inwagen) | "the matter" is just many particles, not a thing | he argues it doesn't escape |
| **Dominant kinds** (Burke) | the mass stops existing when it comes to constitute a person | masses of matter popping out of existence |

His conclusion, roughly: once you spell the materialist options out, each is strange enough that "dualism is considerably less unbelievable" than it's usually taken to be.

Note that this is an argument from **metaphysics of material objects**, not from consciousness. It's the statue-and-lump problem with a thinking statue. See [[Identity and Leibniz's Law]] sec. 4, [[Presentism vs Eternalism]] sec. 3.

## 3. Robinson: What Is a Mental State?

Three questions for an "ontology of the mental":

1. What are the contents of a mind: states, events, acts-with-objects?
2. What ties them together into *one* mind: a bundle relation, or a substance that owns them?
3. Are contents and owner material?

**Perception**

| Theory | The content of my experience is... |
|---|---|
| **Naive realism** | the external object itself |
| **Direct realism** | a way the external object *appears*; no other entity involved |
| **Sense-datum theory** | some entity other than the external object |
| **Adverbialism** | not an object at all but a way of sensing ("I sense redly") |

Argument from hallucination:

1. You could in principle trigger the brain process involved in seeing this room and get an experience exactly like it with no room.
2. Same immediate cause, same kind of effect.
3. In the hallucination the content is a subjective state.
4. So it is in real perception too.

- That goes against naive and direct realism. Then is the subjective content an *object* or just a *manner*? Depends on the **phenomenal principle**: if it sensibly appears that something is red, there is something I'm aware of that *is* red. Robinson accepts it, so: sense data.
- His final view: a sensation = subject + sense datum. In perception the sense datum is taken up into a judgement about an external thing and experienced as how that thing is presented. So he thinks sense data and common-sense realism can be reconciled.

**Sensation and thought both resist reduction**

- **Qualia / the hard problem** (Chalmers' phrase): no physical account of *what it is like*. He assumes this.
- **Thought** too. A physicalist theory of thought will be **computational**: the mind is a "syntactic engine" (Dennett). Then when I'm answering you, what drives my reply isn't my *grasp of what you meant* but a process the meaning merely rides on. Meaning becomes **epiphenomenal**. He calls this as mad as saying it isn't the pain that makes you cry out.
- Positive account: thought is the capacity to **grasp the intelligible form** of things and operate on it. It involves grasping what is "essentially graspable as universal". Universals are "Janus-faced": in things as properties, in minds as concepts. He brings in Plato explicitly.

That last bit links to [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]] and to Perl: intellect is what has the forms. See [[The Forms and the Demiurge]].

## 4. Kim: Four Relations

Kim's chapter is about the words everybody uses for how mind relates to brain.

**Layered model.** Reality in levels: particles → atoms → molecules → cells → organisms → minds. Each level made of the one below, each with its own properties and its own science. The question is how the levels relate.

### Supervenience

- A-properties supervene on B-properties = **no A-difference without a B-difference**.
- His picture: the sculptor. When the physical work on the marble is done, the work is done. There's no further job of attaching the beauty.
- Mind–body version: an exact physical duplicate of you would be an exact mental duplicate.
- Came from ethics (moral on non-moral: Moore, Hare), though Kim finds the emergentists using the word earlier.
- Strengths:
    - **weak**: within any one world, B-twins are A-twins
    - **strong**: across worlds too. This is what's needed for B to *determine* A
    - **global**: worlds alike in B are alike in A
- Key point: supervenience is "not a deep explanatory relation". It says there's dependence, not what kind or why. Dualists, emergentists and reductionists can all accept it.

### Emergence

- British emergentists (Mill, Lewes, Alexander, Lloyd Morgan, Broad).
- Emergent properties are **novel**: they appear only when matter is organised a certain way, and they're **not predictable or explainable** from the lower level. Contrast "resultant" properties like mass.
- Emergentism is a kind of **materialism about things** (nothing but matter) plus **anti-reductionism about properties**.
- Committed to supervenience (same base, same emergents) and to **downward causation**: the emergent property makes a difference to what the parts do.

### Realization

- From Putnam and computers. One program, many machines.
- A mental property is a **functional role**; a brain state **realizes** it by playing that role.
- **Multiple realizability**: pain could be C-fibres in us, something else in an octopus or a Martian. This was the main argument against the old identity theory.

### Reduction

- Old model (Nagel): derive the higher theory from the lower one plus **bridge laws** ("pain ↔ C-fibre firing"). Kim: bridge laws are just more unexplained correlations. An emergentist could accept them.
- Kim's model, **functional reduction**: (1) define the property by its causal role, (2) find what plays the role, (3) explain how. Works for genes (DNA), temperature. If a property can't be given a functional definition it can't be reduced.
- That's where consciousness sticks: qualia don't seem to be definable by role.

### The squeeze

(This is the argument Kim is famous for. The section developing it is one I only skimmed.)

- If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties aren't physical properties, then what's left for the mental to do? The brain state already did it. (**Causal exclusion**.)
- So non-reductive physicalism (the popular middle position: mental is distinct but dependent) is unstable. Either **reduce** (then the mental has its causal powers, because it just is the physical) or accept **epiphenomenalism**, or give up the causal closure of the physical, which is real dualism or real emergentism.

Put Robinson and Kim side by side: Robinson says reduction of thought makes meaning epiphenomenal. Kim says *non*-reduction makes it epiphenomenal unless you break closure. Between them the comfortable middle is gone.

## 5. Soul and Body

*Mine.*

- **Not Cartesian.** The patristic picture isn't a ghost driving a machine. Man is soul *and* body. The body is part of the person, is sanctified, and will be raised. Death is the *unnatural* separation of the two. So an Orthodox Christian shouldn't want just any dualism.
- **But not materialist either.** The soul survives death (Luke 23:43; Rev 6:9; the prayers for the departed; the saints). So it can't simply *be* the body or a state of it.
- Closest philosophical shape is probably **hylomorphism** (soul as form of the body, per Aristotle) with the Christian addition that this form subsists after death. Or what's now called **emergent dualism** (Hasker, whom Zimmerman lists among the dualists): the soul comes to be with the body but is a real individual thing with its own powers. I don't know that the Church has defined anything this fine-grained.
- **Nous.** The Fathers distinguish the *nous* (the "eye of the soul", what knows God) from discursive reasoning. Robinson's "grasp of intelligible form" is nearer to *nous* than a computational model could be. See [[Nous]].
- **Image of God.** If thought is the grasp of universals, and universals are grounded in the Logos, then that we can think at all is part of being made in the image. See [[Being Made in the Image of God]], [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].
- **Christology constrains this.** Apollinaris said the Word took the place of a human mind in Christ. Condemned: "what is not assumed is not healed". So a human *nous* is a real constituent of human nature, not an optional extra. And the Sixth Council's human *will* likewise. Any theory of mind where the mind is just a pattern of behaviour makes those definitions hard to state.
- **Zimmerman and the resurrection.** His problem (what thing am I, given my matter keeps changing?) is the old question about the resurrection body: which matter gets raised? The Fathers (e.g. St. Gregory of Nyssa, *On the Soul and the Resurrection*) say the soul recognises and regathers its own. So the *soul* carries the identity. That's in effect denying that I'm an *ens successivum*.
- **Kim's squeeze and free will.** If the physical is causally closed there's no room for the will to do anything, never mind for synergy. So the theological position needs closure to be false. That's a real commitment and should be owned. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]].
- **Argument from reason.** Robinson's point that a syntactic engine doesn't act *because of* meanings is the core of the C. S. Lewis / Plantinga argument that naturalism undermines trust in reasoning. It fits with the circularity point in [[Philosophy of Logic: What Grounds the Laws of Logic]] sec. 3.2.
