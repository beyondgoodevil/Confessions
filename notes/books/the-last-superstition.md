Very fun attack on the New Atheists. Feser's claim: the "superstition" of the title is **secularism**, the belief that modern science has disproved religion. Actually modern philosophy threw out Aristotle's metaphysics for non-scientific reasons, and almost all of the West's later problems (mind-body, induction, is-ought, personal identity) come from that move.

## Chapters

1. Bad Religion
2. Greeks Bearing Gifts
3. Getting Medieval

- Chapters 2–5 are "a crash course" in the history of Western philosophy, focused on what led to and away from the classical picture.

## 1. The Real Conflict: Metaphysics, Not Science

- Opens with **Antony Flew**, the famous atheist philosopher, becoming a philosophical theist (2004) after reading Aristotle seriously "for the first time"; the secular response was "uniformly derisive". (Flew gave Aristotle "first place" in his change, prompted by David Conway's *The Rediscovery of Wisdom*.)
- The New Atheists (Dawkins, Dennett, Harris, Hitchens) argue against a God no serious theist has held, and know almost nothing of the arguments they dismiss.
    - **Quentin Smith**, a serious atheist philosopher, lamented his fellow secularists' "appalling lack of knowledge" when they criticise religion.
- Their own "religion": secularism, with its dogmas (scientism, materialism) and moral fervour.
    - Secularism is the "**last superstition**": last as what remains when the others are gone, and last as "the mother of all superstitions".
- The real battle isn't science vs religion but **classical metaphysics vs mechanism**. The "war between science and religion" is "really a war between two rival philosophical worldviews", not a scientific or theological dispute.
- Natural-law morality doesn't mean God arbitrarily commands "just 'cause He feels like it", with hell as the only motive. No serious defender holds that.
- Locke (Protestant) and Vatican II (Catholic) both defended toleration and democracy on theological grounds; secularists like democracy less when school boards vote for teaching Intelligent Design.

## 2. Classical Metaphysics: Plato and Aristotle

**Plato**: forms are real, universal, unchanging; particulars participate in them. Against nominalism. See [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]].

- **Plato's arguments for a "third realm"** (with Frege's modern version):
    1. we know truths about triangles that were true before anyone drew one and would stay true if every triangle were erased
    2. mathematical truths are **discovered**, not invented
    3. "There is neither a material world nor any human mind" would have been **true** even if neither existed, so truth isn't material or mental
    4. if concepts and propositions existed only in your mind, no one else could ever grasp what you say
- **Against nominalism**: why do we call just these things "red"? The only plausible answer is "because they all have redness in common". And "resemblance" itself is a universal.

**Aristotle**
- His realism is "the most powerful and systematic realist metaphysics ever developed" (completed by his medieval followers).
- **Aristotle's middle way**: with Heraclitus, real things change; with Parmenides, what's real can't be change alone; with Plato, **form** is what's permanent. But Plato's separate Forms face the **Third Man** argument.
- **Moderate realism**: universals exist **in** things (always with other features); abstracted universals exist in the intellect, drawn from experience of real things. Not Platonism, not conceptualism. The key difference: Plato allows **uninstantiated** universals outside any mind; Aristotle doesn't.
- **Act and potency**: a thing's actual properties vs its capacities to change. Change is the actualising of a potency. Answers Parmenides (change is impossible) and Heraclitus (only change is real).
    - **Potency** isn't "anything at all": a rubber ball can potentially be melted, but not potentially follow someone around by itself.
- **Form and matter** (hylomorphism): a thing is matter organised by a form.
- **The four causes**:

| Cause | Question | For a statue |
|---|---|---|
| material | what is it made of? | bronze |
| formal | what is it? | the shape |
| efficient | what made it? | the sculptor |
| **final** | what is it for? | to honour a hero |

- Other examples: a rubber ball (material: rubber); a heart (formal: muscle tissue organised into atria and ventricles; final: pumping blood).
- **Final causes** are everywhere in nature, not just in design: an acorn is directed toward being an oak, fire toward heat. Even basic efficient causation needs them: a cause "points to" its typical effect. Feser's key thesis: "**Final causality underlies all potentiality and thus all materiality.**"
- A cause contains its effect **formally** (fire causing fire) or **eminently** (a power to produce it without being it).

## 3. God: Aquinas's Arguments and the Objections

- Quoting *A Fish Called Wanda* ("Aristotle was not Belgian... the central teaching of Buddhism is not 'Every man for himself'") on the New Atheists' grasp of philosophy.
- If you haven't understood and answered Aquinas (and Anselm, Scotus, Leibniz, Clarke), you haven't "made your case" against religion. Hitchens shows "a journalist who reads more than most of his colleagues by no means thereby understands more".
- Aquinas's arguments are not "scientific" but they're rational, a different species. Doubts about their premises usually come from rival **metaphysics**, not new scientific evidence.
- Hume's "it's conceivable the cause occurs without the effect" doesn't show causation isn't real: you'd still conclude a puddle was caused by the spigot.
- **Aquinas's Five Ways**, read correctly: not "the universe had a beginning" but **here and now**, things depend on a sustaining cause. Aquinas does **not** argue from "everything has a cause", and does **not** argue that the universe had a beginning (Dawkins gets both wrong).
- **Accidentally vs essentially ordered series** (*per accidens* / *per se*):
    - accidental: a father begets a son who begets a grandson; the father can die and the series goes on. Could in principle be infinite.
    - essential: the hand moves the stick that moves the stone; the stick has no power of its own; if the hand stops, everything stops. **Must** have a first member that is pure act. The First Way is about this kind.
    - Reply to the "**fallacy of composition**" charge: the argument isn't "each part is caused, so the whole is caused" but an essentially ordered series.
- The Five Ways:
    1. motion (change) → Unmoved Mover. See [[The Unmoved Mover]]. **Inertia** doesn't refute it: Newton's first law itself needs explaining.
    2. efficient causation → First Cause. Via **essence and existence**: the cause of existence must be pure existence itself, which is simple.
    3. contingency → necessary being
    4. degrees of perfection → maximum
    5. final causes (the "Fifth Way"): things directed to ends need an intellect directing them. Not Paley's design argument. Darwinism is a serious challenge to Paley and ID but "almost totally irrelevant" to Aquinas, and if anything "slightly helps" him, since it presupposes regular, end-directed causal powers.
- Pure act must be one, simple, immutable, eternal, omnipotent, intelligent and good: limitless knowledge, goodness etc. follow, since every limitation is a **privation**.
- Dawkins's "who designed the designer?" (his "central argument", with its own index entry) misunderstands the argument: pure act isn't a complex thing that needs a designer. The Five Ways aren't arguments from complexity:
    - Aquinas's divine simplicity isn't that kind of simplicity: the First Cause is simple
    - Aquinas isn't explaining the complexity of organs
    - his argument isn't probabilistic but a demonstration
- **Faith** in traditional theology isn't blind: reason establishes that God has revealed (e.g. by the Resurrection), and faith is believing what He reveals because He can't err. The book is about natural theology, not **revealed theology**; but faith, rightly understood, never contradicts reason.
- **The argument from evil** "begs the question" if it assumes the theist's claims (an afterlife, a greater good) are false; "I can't imagine what could outweigh suffering" is a double standard.

## 4. The Soul and Natural Law

- **The soul**: the form of the body. Three kinds:
    - **nutritive** (plants): just the plant's form, giving the powers of nutrition, growth, reproduction
    - **sensory** (animals): adds sense and self-movement
    - **rational** (humans): adds intellect. The intellect grasps universals, which can't be in a material organ (Ross's argument: thought is determinate; physical processes aren't). So the intellect is immaterial and survives death. Since it isn't material, the rational soul is **directly created by God**.
- **Abortion**: Harris's line that "every time you scratch your nose you've committed a Holocaust of potential human beings" confuses a **potential** human (skin cells, as a rubber ball is a "potential eraser") with an **actual** human at an early stage. The soul leaves only at **death**, not with brain damage or a vegetative state.
- **Natural law**: the good for a thing is fulfilling the ends set by its nature. Ethics follows from metaphysics: no is-ought gap if final causes are real. See [[Is Ought]].
    - The first principle (Aquinas): "**good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided**". Hume, having dropped final causes, says reason is "the slave of the passions" and there's nothing contrary to reason in preferring the destruction of the world to a scratch on one's finger.
    - "If what's good is what's natural, isn't everything good?" No: "natural" means according to a thing's **end**, not whatever happens. A man with an inborn tendency to alcoholism shows a defect, not that excessive drinking is good for him.
- Applied to sex: its natural ends are procreation and union, which grounds traditional sexual ethics. (The most controversial chapter.)
    - Its final cause is procreation in the full sense, "procreation in large numbers": generating and **rearing** children, which needs stability and ties fathers into long-term commitment. So on natural law it belongs in marriage.
    - Accusing natural lawyers of being "obsessed with sex" is "projection". Secular "research" into ethics is often "liberal apologetics with conclusions determined in advance".
- **Morality without God**: secular ethics can't ground obligation without final causes; Nietzsche saw it.

## 5. How the Moderns Lost It

- **Nominalism** (Ockham) led politically to "**atomistic individuals**" uniting only by contract. Feser sees **conservatism** (the family, not the individual or the state, as the basic unit) as the true "third way". See [[Conservatism: Burke, de Maistre, Scruton]].
    - Hitchens praises Ockham because nominalism undermines tracing causes back to a first cause: "fideism", which Hitchens oddly calls an advance for reason.
- Bacon, Descartes, Hobbes, Locke, Hume threw out formal and final causes, mostly for political and practical reasons (control of nature), not because science proved anything.
    - The **Mechanical Philosophy** was little more than **anti-Aristotelianism**. The moderns mocked "the dormitive virtue of opium" as a tautology, but it isn't empty: it says opium has a real power.
    - Locke's case: his system explains the evidence as well as scholasticism and is "simpler". Feser: only because it sweeps problems away. **Locke** also limited knowledge in religion to put all creeds on an equally low footing, for toleration.
- What's left is **mechanism**: matter is just quantity in motion. Colours, meanings, purposes get moved "into the mind".
    - **Secondary qualities**: redness as experienced isn't "in the apple" but in the mind.
- Then the mind is the leftover nobody can explain: hence the mind-body problem. Everything that didn't fit mechanism was swept under the rug of the mind, and then the mind couldn't be explained. A "materialist explanation of the mind" is like "a secularist explanation of God" or "a mechanistic explanation of final causes": the subject has been defined out. And:
    - **Hume's problems** (causation, induction) follow: without powers and ends, causes are loose and separate. Necessary connection "is all in us, not in the world". Goodman's "**grue**" puzzle too. See [[Hume on Causation and Induction]].
    - **is-ought**: no value in nature. Talk of **natural rights** needs a real natural order; for a Humean, "natural" can only mean "statistically common" or "conducive to fitness": no normative force.
    - **personal identity**: no substantial form. Without a form uniting the parts, there are only "loose and separate" parts, and calling them "a person" reflects our interests (hence **Parfit**'s denial of a persisting self). A free action is free because of its **form** (not that of a spasm).
- Modern philosophy is a series of failed attempts to fix problems it created. These "traditional problems of philosophy" go back only to the early moderns, "who, unlike the ancients and medievals, created problems rather than solved them".
- Dennett called Darwinism a "**universal acid**". Feser: it's the mechanistic picture as a whole that is the acid, eating away religion, morality, and science itself. The 20th century saw the consequences "in full bloom": totalitarian ideologies and the "**Great Disruption**" in morals (Fukuyama).

## 6. Mind, Meaning and the Failure of Materialism

- Reductive materialism is incoherent: eliminativism denies the beliefs it's arguing for (see [[The Argument from Reason]]).
    - Eliminative materialism is "absurd and incoherent": by its lights, "I feel anxious" is always false. But if there are no mental states, there's no "belief" in eliminativism either.
- **Intentionality is everywhere**: any theory or knowledge claim "points" beyond itself; "if the mind goes, science goes with it". Even a **match** is "directed toward" fire, not frost, even if never struck: final causality at the most basic level.
- **The mind as computer**: a calculator's outputs are "correct" only given **our** assignment of meanings ("2" means two); symbol manipulation alone has no meaning. If thinking were only algorithmic transitions, meanings would play no role in why we conclude what we do: the **argument from reason**.
    - The key argument (from Popper, Kripke, Ross, Putnam) is that physical processes are **indeterminate** in meaning, while thought is determinate. More fundamental than Searle's Chinese Room.
    - You can't turn Jupiter into a cheeseburger by calling it "Cheeseburger": meanings and natures aren't up to us.
- "Everything else has yielded to materialism, why not the mind?" shows "cluelessness": the mind is precisely where everything that didn't fit was put. That (not ignorance of neuroscience) is why Descartes and Locke were dualists. See [[Descartes: The Cogito and Mind-Body Dualism]].
- Dennett's and Dawkins's evolutionary explanations quietly assume teleology ("genes *for*", "function").
    - **Dennett** speaks of "Mother Nature's" purposes: either a peculiar new religion (conscious nature) or a concession that Aristotle was right (unconscious real goal-directedness). Either way, naturalism is abandoned.
    - **DNA** as "blueprint" or "program" points to the realisation of a form typical of the species: a **form or essence**, smuggled back in.
- **Nancy Cartwright**: science uncovers few universal "laws" outside artificial conditions; what it finds are **capacities** or **powers**, and talk of "interfering" conditions presupposes natural tendencies. The notion of a "law of nature" is a relic of Newton's theology. David Oderberg's *Real Essentialism* defends essence and existence today.
