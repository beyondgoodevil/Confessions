The short version of his big book *The Existence of God* (1979, 2004). Swinburne (Oxford, an Orthodox convert from Anglicanism) argues like a scientist: theism is a **hypothesis** that explains the evidence better than its rivals, and each piece of evidence raises its probability. No one argument proves God; together they make it more probable than not.

- The method: like scientists choosing between theories, use the criteria that show one theory is "more likely, on the basis of the data, to be true".

## 1. God

- God = a **person without a body** (a spirit), present everywhere, creator and sustainer, perfectly free, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, a source of moral obligation, eternal, necessary (in a sense), worthy of worship.
- A **person** = a being with powers, beliefs and purposes, who performs intentional actions.
- All the attributes follow from **infinite** power, knowledge and freedom:
    - **Omniscient**: all God's beliefs are true and He believes everything true.
    - **Omnipotent**: can do anything logically possible. Not "make a shape square and round": that's not weakness, the words don't describe anything.
    - **Perfectly free**: nothing outside Him determines what He does. Free agents act for reasons, regarding the action as good.
    - **Eternal** = **everlasting**, not timeless (Swinburne's preference). And eternity is **essential**: anything that began or could cease wouldn't be God.
- **Morality**: if moral truths like "it is wrong to torture children for fun" hold independently of God's will, they're like "nothing can be round and square": necessary truths. God, being perfectly free and knowing, will always do the good.
- Where reasons are equal, God performs a "**mental toss-up**", choosing in a way not determined by His nature.
- **Objection: God is incomprehensible**, so calling Him "powerful" and "knowing" is wrong. Swinburne: some words are used in stretched or **analogical** senses, as physicists use "wave" and "particle" for electrons. But if God were totally incomprehensible, there'd be no reason to worship Him.
- **Objection: the Christian God is three persons.** Swinburne argues for the God worshipped by Christians, Jews and Muslims, whom Christians call "God the Father"; Christianity adds that the Father eternally brings about the Son and the Spirit.

## 2. Two Kinds of Explanation

- The world consists of **substances** with powers and liabilities. What science discovers is the powers and liabilities of substances.

| Scientific (inanimate) | Personal |
|---|---|
| initial conditions + laws of nature | an agent + his intentions + his powers |
| the dynamite exploded because it was ignited | it was ignited because a terrorist intended it |

- Personal explanation isn't reducible to scientific (the mind can't be eliminated).
- A **complete** explanation traces purposes to the ultimate one (I go to the larder to get food, to eat regular meals...).
- God is a personal explanation of the whole world, including the laws.

**Criteria for a good explanation**: (1) it leads us to expect what we observe; (2) it's **simple**; (3) it fits background knowledge; (4) it has scope.

- Example: Newton's theory plus a hypothesised planet (Neptune) was the simplest account of Uranus's orbit.

## 3. Simplicity

- Of hypotheses that explain the data equally, the **simplest** is most likely true ("Ockham's razor"). Science works this way: infinitely many curves fit the observed positions of Mars, and we choose the simplest; it's why we prefer "F = ma" to an equally fitting but complicated formula.
- An **ultimate** explanation = one whose factors have no further explanation.
    - The materialist's ultimate explanation is the powers of innumerable particles: very many brute facts.
    - Theism postulates **one** substance with **infinite** power, knowledge and freedom.
- **God is simple**: "infinite power" is simpler than "power of exactly 4.7 units". So the God hypothesis has high prior probability.
- The infinite attributes go together: infinite power suggests no outside influences (infinite freedom), and that suggests infinite knowledge.
- God keeps each substance in existence and sustains its powers: the metal expands when heated because God sustains that power in it. He permits humans to form their purposes but doesn't make them choose.
- Analogy: in physics, fundamental particles are what they are only if they keep their powers; so God is God only with His essential properties.

## 4. The Universe and Its Order

- **Cosmological**: the universe is a complex thing; it isn't self-explaining. God is a simpler stopping point.
    - Newton's laws hold because they follow from General Relativity and Quantum Theory, which might follow from a Grand Unified Theory. But why that theory?
- **Order (design)**: the universe obeys **simple laws** everywhere: "temporal order". Not to be expected if there's no explanation; to be expected if God wants a world where creatures can understand and act.
    - The starting point isn't that we **perceive** order but that order **is there**.
    - Regularities of medium-sized objects are easily detected even by the unscientific: they make it possible for us to learn and act.
    - Aquinas's Fifth Way quoted: "everything in nature is directed to its goal by someone with understanding".
    - God has a reason to bring about **these** laws: they lead to the evolution of animals and humans.
    - **Darwin** explains our existence given the "machine" of natural selection, but not why that machine exists and operates.
- **Fine-tuning**: the constants allow life within very narrow ranges. God has reason to make embodied creatures; chance or a **multiverse** (inflation theory) are less simple, and a multiverse still needs explaining.

## 5. Humans

- **Consciousness**: the link between brain states and mental states is not explained by science and seems not explainable by it.
    - Physical events are public (anyone can check my desk is square); mental events are private.
    - Why does chocolate cause the taste we call chocolatey, not pineappley? No scientific explanation of the brain–mind correlations.
    - "It is highly irrational to say that something is not there just because you cannot explain how it came to be there."
    - Darwinism can explain why the soul–body connections are useful (desire for food when hungry), not why they exist.
- **Substance dualism** (*The Evolution of the Soul*): the essential part of each of us is an **immaterial soul**.
    - **Split-brain thought experiment**: transplant each hemisphere into a different body. Both would behave like me and claim to be me. But at most one is me, and no physical fact settles which. So my survival consists in the continuation of something else: my **soul**.
    - Animals with thought and feeling have souls too.
- God has reason to make beings with souls, who can choose and know. He may join a soul to a particular body by a "mental toss-up".
- See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]], [[The Argument from Reason]].

## 6. Evil

- A **theodicy** is needed, not just a defense, though "most people in deep distress need comfort, not argument".
- **Free will** is the core: a real choice between good and evil, including the power to harm others, is a great good.
    - If I suffer because of your bad choice, "that is not by any means pure loss for me": I'm **of use**, making your significant choice possible. Prisoners helped by being allowed to help the handicapped; the "poor little rich girl" pitied for never being of use.
    - Without vulnerability to real suffering, our choices would be like a pilot's in a simulator.
- **Natural evil** gives knowledge of the consequences of actions (we learn from natural processes what causes or prevents harm), and makes possible the best virtues and especially worthwhile choices: courage, compassion, patience.
- A good God may allow suffering for a limited time if it gives greater goods, and He can compensate in the life to come.
    - God is unlike a doctor, who'd have no right to harm a patient for others' sake: God is the author of our life and has rights a parent has over a child.
    - Thought experiment: would you choose to come into existence in a world where you suffer for others' benefit, given that otherwise they'd never exist? You may, though you're not obliged.
- The world is one where we're **responsible** for each other; that's a great good, but it requires the possibility of real harm. The reader will agree "to the extent to which he or she values responsibility, free choice, and being of use very much more than thrills of pleasure or absence of pain".
- See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].

## 7. Miracles and Religious Experience

- So far the evidence makes God's existence significantly probable; evil doesn't count against it much.
- **Comparative vs epistemic "seems"**: "it looks elliptical" (looks the way elliptical things look) vs "it seems to me that it is elliptical" (I'm inclined to believe it).
- **Principle of credulity**: if it seems to me that X is present, then probably X is present, unless there are special reasons for doubt. Applied to experience of God: the seeming counts as evidence.
    - Without it we'd be stuck: even corroboration relies on memory.
    - Defeaters: the subject is unreliable, the circumstances are unreliable, the object is probably not present, or the experience was not caused by it.
    - Someone who claims God told them to commit rape must be mistaken: we know on other grounds that rape is wrong.
- **Principle of testimony**: other things being equal, things are probably as others report them.
- So religious experience is evidence, unless we already know God doesn't exist. See [[Religious Experience as Evidence]].
- **Miracles** = a violation of natural law (e.g. a levitation we've checked for strings and magnets). If there's a God, He might intervene to reveal Himself.
    - Against Hume: if there's already some probability of God, testimony to miracles can be credible. "We may be mistaken" is too easily said: if the total evidence is strong, the event was brought about by God. See [[Hume on Causation and Induction]] sec. 5.
    - The **resurrection** is the central case (his later *The Resurrection of God Incarnate*).
- Tests for a **revelation**: the plausibility of its central doctrines on other grounds; miracles confirming it; comparison with other religions' claims. (The army "call-up" analogy: a father who doesn't veto his son's call-up for a grave cause, as God the Father doesn't spare His Son.)

## The Cumulative Case

- Each argument is **C-inductive** (raises the probability) and together they're **P-inductive** (make theism more probable than not).
- Bayes: evidence e confirms hypothesis h if e is more probable given h than without it.
- **So what?** (the epilogue): we have to act (vote, choose) without having considered everything. On the balance of probability, there is a God, and that should shape how we live.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- Swinburne's God is **not simple** in the classical sense: he denies divine timelessness and absolute simplicity. His "simplicity" is the simplicity of a hypothesis. Classical theists (Feser, Hart) find this a God among beings, a demiurge. See [[Divine Simplicity: Western and Orthodox Views]], [[The Experience of God]].
- He became Orthodox in 1995, but his philosophy is very Western-analytic.
