N. wrote about Socrates in every period of his life, and he never made up his mind. In a note from about 1875 he admitted, "Socrates, simply to confess it, stands so near to me, that I almost always fight a battle with him" (Dannhauser's translation). Werner Dannhauser's *Nietzsche's View of Socrates* (1974) follows that battle book by book. His thesis is that for N. "the Socratic life is somehow both a great temptation and something to be rejected," and that the attack is sharpest at the beginning and the end, while the middle period is the friendliest.

N. = Nietzsche. BT = *The Birth of Tragedy*, ASC = its 1886 "Attempt at Self-Criticism", HAH = *Human, All Too Human*, WS = *The Wanderer and His Shadow*, GS = *The Gay Science*, BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, GM = *Genealogy of Morality*, TI = *Twilight of the Idols*, Z = *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, EH = *Ecce Homo*. Quotations come from the translations listed above, except where I mark them as Dannhauser's.

### 1. Why Socrates Is a Problem at All

Before N., Dannhauser points out, people rejected **ancient philosophy** but never **Socrates** himself. Descartes said ancient moral writings were "palaces most superb and magnificent, which are yet built on sand and mud alone"; Hobbes called it "Vain Philosophy". Both still admired Socrates, because he wrote nothing and stood for the philosophic *life*, not a system. Renaissance humanists coined "Saint Socrates, pray for us", and Voltaire and Rousseau both admired him. Even Popper, who hated Plato, saved Socrates by saying Plato had corrupted him.

The young N. turns this upside down: Plato is "the most beautiful growth of antiquity" (BGE Preface), and it is **Socrates who corrupted Plato**. That is why BT is, in Dannhauser's words, "one of the rare attacks on Socrates in the history of philosophy". It is also more radical than Descartes or Hobbes. They thought philosophy could be done better. N.'s attack asks whether philosophy as the West has known it is possible at all: "Why Truth? Why Science? Why Philosophy?" On this view Plato, Descartes and Hobbes are all "children of Socrates", because all of them believe truth is there to be *discovered*, not created, and all of them trust reason too much.

Dannhauser also argues against the two readings of his day:
- **Lukács** (*The Destruction of Reason*) made N. a mere irrationalist who prefers Dionysus to Socrates. Dannhauser thinks this is a caricature. Still, Lukács is right that morality is at the core of N.'s thought and that the moral and the political cannot be separated in him.
- **Kaufmann** (*Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist*) ends his book with a chapter called "Nietzsche's Admiration for Socrates". In it N. is a gadfly whose motto is "Become who you are", and *Ecce Homo* is his *Apology*. Dannhauser grants the admiration but says Kaufmann makes N. "at once less ambiguous and less interesting than he really is". The *Apology* and EH are opposites in tone. Socrates is rooted in Athens, N. is a rootless traveller; Socrates protests his piety, N. flaunts his atheism; Socrates makes himself seem humbler than he is, N. makes himself seem more arrogant. Löwith called EH's tone "desperate buffoonery".

### 2. The Birth of Tragedy: A Newborn Daemon

The heart of BT was written first. Sections 8–15 are almost unchanged from N.'s lecture "Socrates and Greek Tragedy" of 1 February 1870, so the part about Socrates is the part he thought through earliest. The book is built symmetrically, and §13, the one on Socrates himself, sits exactly in the middle of its twenty-five sections.

The argument runs like a detective story (Dannhauser's comparison):
1. **Tragedy dies by suicide** (§11). Euripides "brought the spectator on stage", made the heroes ordinary, and wrote for two judges only. One was himself as a critic, the other a second spectator "who did not understand tragedy", whose name N. holds back.
2. **The accomplice is named** (§12): "In a certain sense Euripides, too, was merely a mask; the deity who spoke out of him was not Dionysos, nor Apollo, but an altogether newborn daemon called Socrates. This is the new opposition: the Dionysiac versus the Socratic." Euripides recanted in the *Bacchae*, but by then his tendency had already won.
3. **Aesthetic Socratism.** Its law is "'In order to be beautiful, everything must be reasonable' – a sentence formed in parallel to Socrates' dictum that 'Only he who knows is virtuous'". Later it becomes "Everything must be conscious in order to be good". So Euripides is the "first sober poet", who condemns his "drunken" predecessors.

Dannhauser notices that Socrates comes on stage as a **god** (a daemon), not a man, and that N. at once starts saying "Socratism". So N.'s subject is bigger than the historical Socrates. Socratism is (1) a hyper-rational tendency older than Socrates (Sophocles already shrank the chorus), (2) Socrates' own teachings, and (3) his influence on history. N. even allows that Socrates is *better* than Socratism. Just as Marx is better than Marxism, there is "a Socrates ill at ease in Socratism".

### 3. "Only by Instinct": The Monstrosity

§13 is N.'s portrait of Socrates himself. The Delphic oracle ranked Socrates wisest, Euripides second and Sophocles third, which ranks them by how much they *knew*. N. reverses the order, putting Aeschylus first and Socrates last. He does not reject wisdom as a standard, only the identification of wisdom with knowing. Socrates found that statesmen, poets and artists acted "only by instinct":

> "'Only by instinct': the phrase goes to the heart and centre of the Socratic tendency. With these words Socratism condemns existing art and existing ethics in equal measure ... Socrates believed that he was obliged to correct existence."

So Socrates walks with "a look of disrespect and superiority into a world where we would count ourselves supremely happy if we could even touch the hem of its cloak in awe". He is "the most questionable phenomenon in Antiquity".

**The daimonion.** N.'s clue is Socrates' inner voice, which only ever holds him back. "Whereas in the case of all productive people instinct is precisely the creative-affirmative force and consciousness makes critical and warning gestures, in the case of Socrates, by contrast, instinct becomes the critic and consciousness the creator – a true monstrosity *per defectum*." He is the "specific non-mystic", in whom logic is as overdeveloped as instinct is in a mystic.

Then the tone changes, and Dannhauser stresses this. Socrates' logical drive shows "a power of nature such as we encounter, to our awed surprise, only in the very greatest instinctual forces". Behind him turns "the enormous drive-wheel of logical Socratism". The section ends with Socrates going calmly to death, as Plato has him leave the *Symposium*, and becoming the new ideal of noble youth. Dannhauser's comment: "only the great are worthy opponents".

### 4. The Optimist, the Cyclops Eye and the Music-Making Socrates

§§14–15 move from the man to his effect:
- To "Socrates' one great Cyclopian eye", tragedy looked irrational and addressed to people of little understanding. Plato burned his tragedies to follow him. The dialogue form survives, but with art now the *ancilla* (handmaid) of philosophy, as philosophy had been of theology.
- **Dialectic is optimistic.** "Virtue is knowledge; sin is only committed out of ignorance; the virtuous man is a happy man." With every conclusion it celebrates a victory, so a dialectician can never be a tragic hero.
- **The theoretical man.** Socrates brought into the world "a profound delusion ... namely the imperturbable belief that thought, as it follows the thread of causality, reaches down into the deepest abysses of being, and that it is capable, not simply of understanding existence, but even of correcting it". Yet this delusion drives science to its limits, where "logic curls up around itself at these limits and finally bites its own tail" and turns into tragic insight.
- **The turning point.** N. calls Socrates "the vortex and turning-point of so-called world history". But he also says that without the Socratic drive to know, the energy of humanity would have gone into "general wars of extinction" and "a practical pessimism", even suicide. Dannhauser's conclusion: in BT Socrates is also a **preserver of life**.

The most surprising passage is the last days in prison. A dream kept telling Socrates, "Socrates, make music!" So he set Aesop to verse, and N. imagines him asking, "Perhaps there is a kingdom of wisdom from which the logician is banished? Perhaps art may even be a necessary correlative and supplement of science?" This is where the **music-making Socrates** comes from, the symbol of the culture N. hoped for. Socratism will be "transcended and incorporated", Dannhauser says, "not refuted"; the new artist will be a music-making Socrates, not another Aeschylus. Only then does N. call out, "The time of Socratic man is past. Put on wreaths of ivy, take up the thyrsus" (§20).

### 5. Tragic Versus Theoretical: Dannhauser's Assessment of BT

Dannhauser sets out the two "world views" N. opposes:

| | tragic view | theoretical (Socratic) view |
|---|---|---|
| the world | a chaos, a "contradiction in the heart of the world" | a cosmos, intelligible |
| how we know | by making and taking part | by detachment, reason |
| suffering | inevitable, to be affirmed and transfigured | the wound of existence can be healed |
| the high | derived from the low and frail | independent, with power of its own |
| existence | affirmed | judged and corrected |

He also contrasts BT with Aristotle's *Poetics*, which N. treats as aesthetic Socratism in its purest form. Aristotle wants a hero who is "not preeminently virtuous" and falls by an error of judgement, which quietly implies that reason brings happiness. So "Nietzsche's tragic hero suffers because he is an individual, Aristotle's because he is not individual enough". For Aristotle the goal is catharsis, an end to suffering; for N. it is a "metaphysical solace" that affirms suffering.

Dannhauser's objections to BT:
1. Socrates' place between Apollo and Dionysus is unclear; some of N.'s early notes even call Socrates and Plato "Apollinian".
2. N. never shows *why* reason cannot understand the irrational.
3. He ignores the difference between classical reason and later, thinner kinds of reason. Classical science tried to *understand* nature, while modern science tries to *master* it.
4. His history of the West is too sketchy to prove that all of modernity is already in Socrates.
5. The book is itself Socratic. It is a theoretical work against theory, an optimistic call to correct existence, and a philosophy book written against a philosopher. N. later wished his soul had "sung" instead of "spoken" (ASC 3). Dannhauser notes that "one concedes a good deal to Socrates merely by consenting to argue with him".

In 1886 N. himself sharpened the question in a famous passage (ASC 1):

> "Might not this very Socratism be a sign of decline, of exhaustion, of sickness, of the anarchic dissolution of the instincts? ... Is scientific method perhaps no more than fear of and flight from pessimism? A subtle defence against – truth? ... O, Socrates, Socrates, was that perhaps your secret? O, mysterious ironist, was this perhaps your – irony?"

In BT Socrates was a naive optimist. In 1886 he is a "mysterious ironist", and Dannhauser takes that change as the clue to everything N. says later.

### 6. The Alternative: Heraclitus and the Artist-Philosopher

N. does not reject philosophy as such in his early period. In the unfinished *Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks*, the pre-Socratics are "physicians" of a *healthy* culture, statesmen-philosophers who never turned against the moral instincts of the Greeks. Thales' "all is water" leaps beyond science to the intuition that all is one. Heraclitus is the hero: he affirms becoming as "the game of Zeus", has no use for moral imperatives, and lives in proud solitude ("To walk alone along the street belongs to the essence of the philosopher"). Dannhauser notes that this Heraclitus already looks like Zarathustra.

In this story Socrates is the turning point in three ways:
1. After him art and philosophy split apart.
2. He is both a new kind of scientist, "alien to the Greeks", and the destroyer of the old Democritean science. The new Socratic science sets the drive to know free.
3. He turns philosophy into schools and writing, so that from then on it sits at the edge of a culture instead of at its centre.

N. still lumped Socrates with the "pure types" from Thales on. Plato was the first "mixed character". But his notes say "Socrates throws the whole thing over".

### 7. The Middle Period: Socrates the Free Spirit

After his break with Wagner and his turn to science (HAH, *Dawn*, GS I–IV), N. is at his **friendliest** to Socrates. Art now seems to him effeminate and science manly, so the "inartistic Socrates" stops being much of a charge. Dannhauser gathers the passages:
- HAH I 126: Socrates' daimonion "perhaps" was an ear disease that he read morally. Here Socrates is, faintly, a forerunner of Christianity.
- HAH I 433 "Xanthippe": by making his home unbearable, she turned him into "the greatest street-dialectician of Athens". HAH I 437: hemlock awaits the free spirit, and Socrates had to send his wife away on his last day.
- *Mixed Opinions*: Socrates and Jesus died in the two greatest judicial murders in history, and both were veiled suicides.
- WS 72: testing the god to see whether he spoke the truth was "one of the finest compromises between piety and freedom of the spirit".
- *Dawn* 199: the Greeks valued virtue only as a means to power and glory, so they could not believe their ears when Socrates said the virtuous man is the happiest. This made old Greek nobility weak against him, and it means a *new* nobility will have to answer his critique.

The high point is WS 86, "Socrates":

> "If all goes well, the time will come when we will, in seeking to advance ourselves morally and rationally, prefer to take in our hands the memorabilia of Socrates, rather than the Bible ... Socrates has an advantage over the founder of Christianity in the cheerful form of his seriousness and his playful wisdom, which constitute the best spiritual condition for humans. He had, moreover, the greater understanding."

Here Socrates is the "mediator-sage" in whom every temperament takes part. He is no longer a narrow type, his irony is joy in life, and he is the liberator who sets people free instead of the destroyer of a culture.

### 8. The Dying Socrates (GS 340)

The middle period ends with the aphorism that sets up all the late attacks:

> "I admire the courage and wisdom of Socrates in everything he did, said—and did not say. This mocking and enamored monster and pied piper of Athens ... was not only the wisest chatterer of all time: he was equally great in silence. I wish he had remained taciturn also at the last moment of his life ... he said: 'O Crito, I owe Asclepius a rooster.' This ridiculous and terrible 'last word' means for those who have ears: 'O Crito, life is a disease.' ... Socrates, Socrates suffered life! And then he still revenged himself—with this veiled, gruesome, pious, and blasphemous saying. ... —Alas, my friends, we must overcome even the Greeks!"

Asclepius was the god of healing, so the cock would be a thank-offering for being cured, and the cure is death. Dannhauser draws three conclusions:
1. N. now prefers the **living** Socrates to the dying one. In BT it was the other way round.
2. The optimist of BT has become a **pessimist**, whose irony hid a hostility to life.
3. Even philosophy's greatest figure is tied to **revenge**.

Kaufmann's note in his translation points out where the aphorism stands. It comes right before GS 341, the first statement of eternal recurrence, and GS 342, the opening of Zarathustra. The answer to the dying Socrates is the thought of a life one would will to live again forever.

### 9. Beyond Good and Evil: The Secretive Ironist

In the late works Socrates is mostly mentioned in passing, but always at decisive moments:
- **Preface.** Plato's "pure spirit and the good in itself" was the most dangerous error so far. N. asks, "as a physician: 'What explains such a disease on the most beautiful plant of antiquity, on Plato? did the evil Socrates corrupt him? was Socrates the corrupter of youth after all? and did he deserve his hemlock?'" They are questions, Dannhauser notes, asked lightly, since dogmatism cannot be refuted dogmatically.
- **BGE 80**: "What did that god mean who counseled: 'Know thyself!' Was it perhaps: 'stop being of concern to yourself! become objective!' – And Socrates? – And the 'scientific human being'?"
- **BGE 190**: Socratism is really utilitarian, equating the good with the useful and pleasant. Plato poured his own nobility into his teacher, so the Platonic Socrates is, as in Homer's line on the Chimaera, "Plato in front, Plato in back," and a monster in the middle.
- **BGE 191**: the old quarrel of faith and knowledge is the quarrel of instinct and reason, and it "first appeared in the person of Socrates". He took the side of reason and laughed at his noble Athenians, who could not give reasons for what they did. But "silently and secretly he laughed about himself too", because he found the same thing in himself. "This was the actual falseness of that great secretive ironist." Plato, "more innocent ... and lacking the craftiness of a plebeian", really believed that reason and instinct aim at the same good. Descartes, "the father of rationalism (and consequently the grandfather of the Revolution)", is a purer Socratist than Socrates: "but reason is only a tool, and Descartes was superficial."
- **BGE 212**: every philosopher so far has been "the bad conscience of their time". Among exhausted Athenians who still used the old grand words, "perhaps irony was necessary for greatness of the soul: that Socratic, malicious certainty". Insofar as Socrates was *untimely*, N. admires him.
- **BGE 295**: the "genius of the heart", a "pied piper" and charmer of souls, sounds like Socrates until he turns out to be Dionysus, a god who *philosophizes*. Dannhauser contrasts this with the *Symposium*, where gods do not philosophize because they already have wisdom.

**GM III 7** adds a joke: "A married philosopher belongs in comedy, that is my proposition: and that exception Socrates, the malicious Socrates, it seems, married out of irony, just to demonstrate this proposition." Dannhauser picks up on the word **irony**. Irony means awareness, so an ironic Socrates may have known everything N. knows. N.'s answer, in TI, is to make Socrates' awareness *plebeian*.

### 10. Twilight of the Idols: The Problem of Socrates

"The Problem of Socrates" comes right after "Maxims and Arrows", a section that is really about N. himself. Dannhauser reads the pairing as a contest of two selves, ascent against decline: "To establish the superiority of one philosophy over another it is then necessary to establish the superiority of one philosophizing subject over another." The argument goes step by step:

1. **The consensus of the wise** (§1). "Concerning life, the wisest men of all ages have judged alike: it is no good ... Even Socrates said, as he died: 'To live—that means to be sick a long time: I owe Asclepius the Savior a rooster.'" Once people said that the agreement of the sages proves something true. N. replies, "At least something must be sick here."
2. **Value judgements are symptoms** (§2). "Judgments, judgments of value, concerning life, for it or against it, can, in the end, never be true: they have value only as symptoms ... the value of life cannot be estimated." Dannhauser objects that a decadent can still say truly that two and two are four, and that N.'s own affirmation is also a judgement on life.
3. **The body** (§3). "Socrates was plebs. We know, we can still see for ourselves, how ugly he was." Among the Greeks ugliness was "almost a refutation". Then the physiognomist who called him a *monstrum* full of every vice, to which Socrates "merely answered: 'You know me, sir!'". Dannhauser calls this "a caricature in dubious taste", but N. needs it: if there is no pure mind, the mind has to be read through the body.
4. **The soul** (§4). "Everything in him is exaggerated, buffo, a caricature; everything is at the same time concealed, ulterior, subterranean." N. asks what idiosyncrasy produced "that Socratic equation of reason, virtue, and happiness: that most bizarre of all equations".
5. **Dialectic as plebeian** (§§5–7). "With Socrates, Greek taste changes in favor of dialectics ... with dialectics the plebs come to the top." Among nobles, "Honest things, like honest men, do not carry their reasons in their hands like that". Yet "Socrates was the buffoon who got himself taken seriously". N. asks whether his irony was "plebeian *ressentiment*" and dialectic "a form of revenge".
6. **The fascination** (§§8–9). He "discovered a new kind of agon" and was "a great erotic". Above all he saw that his case was not unusual: "old Athens was coming to an end", "the instincts were in anarchy". He offered a cure: "The impulses want to play the tyrant; one must invent a counter-tyrant who is stronger." To the physiognomist he added: "This is true, but I mastered them all."
7. **The cure is part of the disease** (§§10–11). Reason was made a tyrant out of need: "there was but one choice: either to perish or—to be absurdly rational." But "Socrates was a misunderstanding; the whole improvement-morality, including the Christian, was a misunderstanding ... To have to fight the instincts—that is the formula of decadence: as long as life is ascending, happiness equals instinct."
8. **The last word** (§12). "Did he himself still comprehend this, this most brilliant of all self-outwitters? ... Socrates wanted to die: not Athens, but he himself chose the hemlock ... 'Socrates is no physician,' he said softly to himself; 'here death alone is the physician. Socrates himself has merely been sick a long time.'"

Dannhauser makes several points about this reading:
- **N.'s Socrates is continent, not temperate.** He holds his cravings down at great cost, where the traditional Socrates is untroubled by them.
- **Socrates is decadent but wise.** The section ends where it began, with the death, and it calls his courage to die *wisdom*. That suggests N. did not think the problem was solved.
- **The wise death is kept quiet.** Socrates speaks his last insight "softly to himself", as if life protects itself by keeping that wisdom from spreading.
- **The new equation replaces the old.** "Happiness equals instinct" takes the place of reason = virtue = happiness. Next to Aristotle's happiness as "activity of the soul in accordance with virtue", Dannhauser notes that the virtue missing from N. is *moderation*.

The next sections widen the attack from the philosopher to philosophy: "reason" as the falsifier of the senses (with Heraclitus excepted "with the highest respect"), "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable", which begins with Plato, and finally "Plato is boring", a "higher swindle" and a bridge "leading to the cross" (TI X 2). By now N. hardly separates Socrates from Plato. In the late notes they "merge into one great antagonist", and Platonism becomes "pre-Christian Christianity".

### 11. Zarathustra Against Socrates

Z never names Socrates, but Dannhauser reads Zarathustra as an anti-Socrates as much as an Antichrist:

| Socrates | Zarathustra |
|---|---|
| never leaves Athens except on campaign (*Crito* 52b) | "many lands" and peoples; his only home is the "children's land" |
| neither laughs nor weeps | laughs and weeps constantly |
| always in public (Xenophon, *Memorabilia* I.1.10) | mostly alone or with his animals, and we see him asleep |
| speaks to named young men | speaks to the sun, life, wisdom, a dwarf, his own soul |
| speaks | sings |
| a lover of wisdom, "best and wisest" of his time (*Phaedo*) | "weary of my wisdom", suffering from too much of it |
| a sun above him outside the cave (*Republic* 516b) | addresses the sun as an equal and ends "like a morning sun" |

The teachings clash point by point:
- **The equation.** In the Prologue, happiness, reason and virtue are "poverty and filth and wretched contentment".
- **The teacher of virtue.** "On the Chairs of Virtue" mocks a sage who counts "ten truths" and "ten reconciliations" a day and praises sleep, "lord of the virtues". This recalls Socrates' praise of dreamless sleep in the *Apology*. For Zarathustra the whole classical preference for rest over motion, being over becoming and contemplation over action is "sleepy".
- **The wise men.** "On the Famous Wise Men" calls them servants of the people. "Spirit is the life that itself cuts into life."
- **Moderation** is the one Socratic virtue Zarathustra never revalues; he simply dismisses it: "This, however, is mediocrity: even though it be called moderation" ("On the Virtue That Makes Smaller").

Dannhauser adds a doubt of his own. If truth is not discovered but created, then the will to power is only *Zarathustra's* truth ("the way does not exist"). Yet he also needs it to be simply true. The classics took nature as a limit; Zarathustra has none, and this is why he needs eternal recurrence to will even the past.

### 12. What They Share

The attack keeps turning up likenesses, and Dannhauser lists them. N. sounds like Socrates or Plato when he praises madness, says poets lie too much, puts warriors under the "saints of knowledge", distrusts pity, defends hierarchy, separates the good from the noble, and writes that "the greatest events—those are not our loudest but our stillest hours". His aphorisms deal with the same problem the dialogues deal with, how to write for the few; compare Plato's *Seventh Letter*, "there is no treatise of mine on these things". The will to power is like *eros* without a goal, and eternal recurrence is an attempt to see the world again as a cosmos to live in.

Above all, both make **morality the problem**. Zarathustra sounds most like Socrates in "On Old and New Tablets":

> "When I came to human beings I found them sitting on an old conceit: all of them believed they had long known what good and evil were for the human being ... This somnolence I disturbed when I taught: what good and evil are, that nobody knows— unless it be the creator!"

The last clause is where they part: for Socrates good and evil are found, for Zarathustra they are made, so philosophy as contemplation becomes philosophy as creation. Hence "the perpetual encounter with Socrates must perpetually turn into a quarrel with him".

### 13. Who Wins?

Dannhauser, a student of Leo Strauss, ends against N.:
- N. "paints with too broad a brush". His attack on dogmatism and systems "may hit Hegel, but it misses Socrates and Plato". His attacks on rationalism "may hit Descartes" but miss them too.
- He will not allow Socrates an awareness equal to his own, although "irony" and "secretive ironist" are N.'s own words for it. "No sustained analysis of even one Platonic dialogue buttresses any of Nietzsche's criticisms."
- His prized historical sense tempts him to think he understands the ancients better than they understood themselves.
- Seen as a personal contest, "Socrates emerges as the fuller, more profound, and more enduring figure. Socrates, who wore only one mask, compounded of irony and urbanity, emerges as more intriguing than Nietzsche, the man of many masks." He was just as shocking, "while giving moderation its due and dignifying the canons of public responsibility".

Dannhauser admits that N. saw most of these objections himself and stated them better. He ends with thanks: N.'s thoughts "lead one to a Socrates freed of all the crusts of tradition and shining as a representative of the grandeur that is philosophy".

### Conclusion

N.'s Socrates changes shape three times. He begins as the newborn daemon who killed tragedy, but also as a monstrous force of nature who kept life going and pointed beyond himself to a music-making Socrates. In the middle works he is the free spirit and cheerful sage, preferred to Jesus. At the end he is the ugly plebeian, the secretive ironist and the decadent whose cure was another disease, yet wise enough to know it and choose death. Behind every version is the same question: is reason a servant of life or its enemy? What kept N. close to Socrates is that both made morality the problem. What kept them at war is that Socrates looked for the good while N. wanted to create it. Dannhauser thinks the contest goes to Socrates, and he thinks it was N. who made Socrates worth contesting again.
