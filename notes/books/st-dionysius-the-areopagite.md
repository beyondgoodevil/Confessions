The most quoted writer the site doesn't have a page for. Behind the apophatic theology, the hierarchy of angels, "beyond being", and a lot of Maximus and Palamas.

## 1. Who

**The tradition**

- Dionysius, member of the Areopagus (Athens' high court), converted by St. Paul's sermon: "certain men clave unto him, and believed: among the which was Dionysius the Areopagite" (Acts 17:34).
- First bishop of Athens, one of the Seventy. Present at the Dormition of the Theotokos (which he describes). Martyred in Gaul c. 96 under Domitian.
- Feast **October 3**. Hieromartyr.

**The scholarship**

- The writings (the "corpus") weren't written in the 1st century. Earliest clear citation: Severus of Antioch, 518–28. They use Proclus (d. 485) closely. So c. 500, probably Syria. Hence "Pseudo-Dionysius".
- Doubts are old: Hypatius of Ephesus (532) questioned it; Valla and Erasmus in the Renaissance. Koch and Stiglmayr (1895) settled it.
- Guesses at the real author include Peter the Iberian (Bp. Alexander Golitzin).
- Calling it "forgery" is anachronistic. Writing in an ancient figure's name was an accepted form, and the author presents himself as handing on a tradition.

Does the dating matter for Orthodox use? The Church received the writings by their content, through Maximus, John of Damascus and the councils. Their authority doesn't hang on the first-century date. (That's my view; some Orthodox still hold to the traditional authorship.)

## 2. The Corpus

| Work | About |
|---|---|
| ***Divine Names*** | what the names of God in Scripture mean, and how they fall short |
| ***Mystical Theology*** | five short chapters: ascent beyond all names into the divine darkness |
| ***Celestial Hierarchy*** | nine ranks of angels in three triads |
| ***Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*** | the Church's sacraments and orders as a hierarchy |
| **Ten letters** | to monks, clergy, bishops. Letter 9 on symbolic theology |
| *Theological Outlines*, *Symbolic Theology* | lost (or never existed), known only from his references |

## 3. The Divine Names

- All the names come from Scripture ("the divine oracles").
- God gets them with **hyper-** ("beyond") and **pro-** ("before"): *hyperagathos* (beyond-good), *hyperousios* (beyond-being), *proon* (pre-being).
- Order of the book:
    - ch. 4: **Good** first, then light, **beauty**, love (*eros*). Good comes before Being because the Good gives being to what isn't yet. Plato's Good "beyond being", baptised. See [[The Good in Plato]].
    - ch. 5–7: Being, Life, Wisdom/Intellect.
    - later: Power, Peace, One, etc.
- Ch. 4 also has the long treatment of **evil** (sec. 7 below) and of **beauty**. See [[Beauty as a Way to God]].

## 4. Procession and Return

Neoplatonic triad (from Proclus): **abiding** (*mone*), **procession** (*proodos*), **return** (*epistrophe*). See [[Neoplatonism: Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus]].

- *Divine Names* is mostly about procession: God's goodness pouring out into creation.
- The other works are about return: how creatures go back up.
- **Divine eros** (*DN* 4.13): out of providential love God "goes outside himself" (*exo heautou ginetai*), is "charmed" (*thelgetai*) into dwelling in creation, and yet stays what He is. That's not Plotinus. The One doesn't love.
- Also not Plotinus: every being depends **directly** on God, not on a chain of intermediate principles.

That second point matters. The structure is Neoplatonic, but the God is personal, loving and Creator. Same pattern as the logoi in Maximus. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]].

## 5. Hierarchy

The word "hierarchy" is his coinage. It means a sacred order through which light is passed on, not a chain of command.

| Celestial (3 × 3) | |
|---|---|
| Highest | seraphim, cherubim, thrones |
| Middle | dominions, powers, authorities |
| Lowest | principalities, archangels, angels |

| Ecclesiastical | Initiators | Initiated |
|---|---|---|
| perfecting | bishops (hierarchs) | monks |
| illuminating | priests | holy people (laity) |
| purifying | deacons | catechumens, penitents, possessed |

- Each rank receives from the one above and passes on to the one below.
- The three functions: **purify, illumine, perfect**. Same as the three stages of [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
- The **Liturgy** is the hierarchy in motion: the bishop goes out from the altar to the people and returns.
- "Theurgy" (*theourgia*), "God-work": for him, God's own saving work in the sacraments, the "consummation" of theology. Not pagan ritual magic, though he takes the word from Iamblichus and Proclus.

## 6. Saying and Unsaying

| | Kataphatic | Apophatic |
|---|---|---|
| Does | affirms names of God | denies them |
| Moves | downward, from highest names to lowest symbols | upward, from lowest denials to highest |
| Example | God is good, wise, light | God is not good as we know good |

- Then **deny the denials too**. God is beyond both affirmation and negation. Otherwise "not-X" becomes the new idol.
- **Symbolic theology**: Scripture calls God a rock, a worm, drunk, asleep. These "dissimilar similarities" are actually *safer* than noble images, because no one mistakes God for a worm.
- Theology here is **hymning** and prayer, not cataloguing.

## 7. The Divine Darkness

*Mystical Theology* 1.

- "Mystical" (*mystikos*) means "hidden", not "a private feeling".
- Moses goes up Sinai, leaves the people and the priests behind, and enters the cloud: "the truly mysterious darkness of unknowing" (*agnosia*), "brilliant darkness", "hidden silence".
- Not darkness as mere absence of light: a darkness from *too much* light. Compare 1 Tim 6:16, "dwelling in the light which no man can approach". See [[1 Timothy 6:16: God Dwells in Unapproachable Light, aka His Uncreated Glory]].
- Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* had the same picture a century earlier.
- Palamas later reads this darkness as the uncreated light itself. See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]].

## 8. Evil

- Evil is **privation**, absence of a due good, with no being of its own (*DN* 4.18–35).
- Taken from Proclus's *On the Existence of Evils* (that evil is "parasitic" on good, a by-product of aims that fail). Corrects Plotinus, who had located evil in matter.
- Passed to Aquinas and the whole later tradition. See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]], [[Death and Corruptibility as Privations of Life and the Ability to Sin]].

## 9. Influence

**East**

- St. Maximus the Confessor wrote scholia on him and built the *Mystagogy* on his liturgical theology.
- St. John of Damascus quoted him in defence of icons (*Letter to Titus*).
- Palamas and the hesychasts: unknowability of the essence, union with God in light.

**West**

- Translated by Hilduin (c. 838) and Eriugena (862).
- Aquinas cites him constantly; the *Summa*'s plan (from God, back to God) follows abiding–procession–return.
- Mystics: Eckhart, *The Cloud of Unknowing*, Nicholas of Cusa, John of the Cross.
- Gothic architecture: Abbot Suger's light-filled St-Denis (Dionysius was confused with St. Denis of Paris).
- **Luther**: Dionysius "platonizes more than he Christianizes". Protestant suspicion has continued.
- Modern: Marion and Derrida on negative theology. See [[Heidegger and the God Beyond Being]].

**The Orthodox debate**

- Critical, mildly: Fr. Georges Florovsky and Fr. John Meyendorff, worried the hierarchy scheme was more Neoplatonic than biblical (grace by rank).
- Defending: Vladimir Lossky (his apophaticism is the foundation of Orthodox theology); Bp. Alexander Golitzin (the corpus is a thoroughly Christian, liturgical and monastic theology).

## 10. Questions

- Is the hierarchy compatible with every Christian having direct access to God (Heb 4:16)? Golitzin's answer: hierarchy is *how* the gift reaches everyone, not a barrier.
- How close is his One to Plotinus's? Sec. 4 suggests the differences (eros, direct dependence, creation) are decisive. Worth testing against [[Neoplatonic Idea of Divine Simplicity]].
