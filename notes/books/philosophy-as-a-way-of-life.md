Hadot (1922–2010, Collège de France, specialist on Plotinus and Marcus Aurelius). Collected essays with one main idea: for the ancients, philosophy was not a set of doctrines but a **choice of a way of life**, practised by **spiritual exercises** that transform the person. The doctrines exist to serve the life.

## Structure

- **I. Method**: 1. Forms of life and forms of discourse; 2. Philosophy, exegesis, and creative mistakes
- **II. Spiritual Exercises**: 3. Spiritual exercises; 4. Ancient spiritual exercises and "Christian philosophy"
- **III. Figures**: 5. Socrates; 6. Marcus Aurelius; 7. Reflections on the idea of the "cultivation of the self" (against Foucault)
- **IV. Themes**: 8. Only the present is our happiness; 9. The view from above; 10. The sage and the world; 11. Philosophy as a way of life

## 1. Why "Spiritual" Exercises

- Not "ethical" (too narrow), not "intellectual" (too cerebral), not "thought" exercises. They involve the **whole person**: thought, imagination, feeling, will.
- Their aim is to change one's **way of seeing and being**: to move from inauthentic, anxious, passion-driven life to a life of reason, freedom and peace.
- The schools differ in doctrine but share the goal: the Stoic, the Epicurean, the Platonist all try to become a **sage**.

## 2. The Exercises

From Philo's lists and the Stoics:

| Exercise | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Attention** (*prosochē*) | constant vigilance over oneself, presence of mind. "The fundamental Stoic attitude" |
| **Living in the present** | the past is gone, the future not ours; only the present is in our power |
| **Meditation** (*meletē*) | rehearsing principles until they're ready to hand ("have them at hand like a doctor his instruments") |
| **Premeditation of evils** (*praemeditatio malorum*) | imagining poverty, exile, death in advance so they don't overwhelm us |
| **Memorisation** | short sayings (*kephalaia*) to recall in difficulty |
| **Self-examination** | each evening: what did I do wrong? (Pythagoreans, Seneca). See [[Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans]] |
| **Reading, listening, research** | commentary on texts as a spiritual practice |
| **Self-mastery, indifference to indifferent things** | only virtue is good |
| **Learning to die** | Plato, *Phaedo* 67e: philosophy is "practice for dying": separating the soul from the passions of the body |
| **The view from above** | imagining the earth from the heights, seeing human affairs as tiny. Marcus: "Asia and Europe are corners of the universe" |
| **Dialogue** | Socratic questioning as an exercise performed on the other person and oneself |

## 3. Socrates

- Socrates writes nothing, claims to know nothing, and lives his philosophy. The *dialogue* isn't to transmit doctrine but to make the interlocutor **examine himself**: "the unexamined life is not worth living".
- Socratic ignorance is an invitation to a conversion of the whole self, not a theory.
- He's the model of the philosopher as a person "out of place" (*atopos*), at odds with ordinary values.

## 4. Marcus Aurelius

- The *Meditations* (literally *To Himself*) aren't a treatise but **exercises**: Marcus writing the Stoic principles to himself, again and again, to keep them alive.
- Structure follows Epictetus's three disciplines:
    - **desire**: accept what happens as willed by the cosmic Logos
    - **action**: act justly for the common good
    - **assent**: judge impressions correctly; don't add "this is bad"
- The repetitions are the point: they're meditations, not arguments.

## 5. Christianity as "the True Philosophy"

The most important chapter for this site.

- Early Christian writers (Justin, Clement, Origen, the Cappadocians, Evagrius) called Christianity "**philosophy**", and later **monasticism** was called "the philosophy" or "the philosophy according to Christ".
- They took over the exercises directly:
    - **attention** → *prosochē*, **watchfulness** (*nepsis*): the title of the Philokalia is "the love of the beautiful" and its key word is *nepsis*. See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]]
    - **self-examination** → the monastic examination of thoughts; Dorotheus of Gaza
    - **remembrance of death** → *mnēmē thanatou*, a step on Climacus's ladder. See [[St. John Climacus: The Ladder of Divine Ascent]]
    - **apatheia** (Stoic) → **dispassion**, freedom from the passions (Evagrius)
    - **spiritual direction** and confession of thoughts
- Hadot's claim: Christian spirituality is to a large extent **ancient philosophy continued**, with Christ as the Logos.
- **What changes**: the exercises now aim at **union with God** by grace, not self-sufficiency; obedience and humility replace the sage's autonomy; the model is Christ, not the sage.

## 6. How Philosophy Became Theory

- In the Middle Ages, philosophy became the **handmaid of theology** in the universities: a set of concepts used by theology, while the way of life went to the monasteries.
- Modern philosophy inherited that: philosophy as **discourse** about theory, done by professionals, not a way of living. Hadot wants philosophy to recover its existential dimension (and finds some of it in Montaigne, Descartes's *Meditations*, Spinoza, Kant, Nietzsche, Wittgenstein).
- Against **Foucault**, who used Hadot for an "aesthetics of existence": the ancients weren't cultivating a beautiful **self** but trying to move **beyond** the self into universal reason and the cosmos.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- Fits the Orthodox self-understanding very well: theology isn't an academic subject but the fruit of a life of prayer and purification. Evagrius: "if you are a theologian you will pray truly, and if you pray truly you are a theologian." See [[Vladimir Lossky: The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church]] ch. 1.
- But the Orthodox would stress the break too: the Fathers used the exercises, but the source is **grace** and the sacraments; the goal is **theosis**, not tranquillity. See [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]].
