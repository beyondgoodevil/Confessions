Hadot (1922–2010, Collège de France, specialist on Plotinus and Marcus Aurelius). Collected essays with one main idea: for the ancients, philosophy was not a set of doctrines but a **choice of a way of life**, practised by **spiritual exercises** that transform the person. The doctrines exist to serve the life.

- Hadot's method (from his teachers Paul Henry and Pierre Courcelle): first a scrupulous, textually critical reading, then an exact translation; only then interpretation. His conclusions depend on his translations.
    - **Courcelle**'s *Recherches sur les Confessions* (1950) "almost caused a scandal" by its reading of Augustine's conversion (the garden scene as partly literary).
    - Two inseparable projects: explain Latin thought by its Greek background, and recover lost Greek thought in Latin writers.
- Hadot became "detached" from Catholicism (he'd been a priest) but it formed him (closing interview).

- **I. Method**: 1. Forms of life and forms of discourse; 2. Philosophy, exegesis, and creative mistakes

- **III. Figures**: 5. Socrates; 6. Marcus Aurelius; 7. Reflections on the idea of the "cultivation of the self" (against Foucault)
- **IV. Themes**: 8. Only the present is our happiness; 9. The view from above; 10. The sage and the world; 11. Philosophy as a way of life

## 1. Why "Spiritual" Exercises

- Not "ethical" (too narrow), not "intellectual" (too cerebral), not "thought" exercises. They involve the **whole person**: thought, imagination, feeling, will.
- Their aim is to change one's **way of seeing and being**: to move from inauthentic, anxious, passion-driven life to a life of reason, freedom and peace.
- The schools differ in doctrine but share the goal: the Stoic, the Epicurean, the Platonist all try to become a **sage**.
    - From the 3rd c. BC to the 3rd c. AD, six traditions stayed remarkably stable: Platonism, Aristotelianism, Stoicism, Epicureanism, Scepticism, Cynicism. Their core **dogmas and rules of life** stayed unchanged.
- They raise the individual to "the life of the objective Spirit": seeing from the perspective of the **Whole** ("Become eternal by transcending yourself").
- Philosophy as **therapy of the passions** (Friedmann: "Try to get rid of your own passions"). Epictetus: "**The philosopher's school is a clinic.**"
- **Askēsis** in the ancient sense is "exercise", not modern "asceticism" (abstinence from food, sleep, sex).
- "The idea of the 'purely spiritual' is untenable."
- Hadot's two senses of "theoretical": opposed to "practical" (discourse vs lived philosophy), and *théorétique* (contemplative), which is itself a way of life.
- Plotinus (*Enn.* VI.7.36) distinguishes rational theology that **teaches about** the Good from spiritual exercises that **lead to** it. Teaching can allude to mystical experience but can't lead to it; everything "technical" can be taught.

## 2. The Exercises

From Philo's lists and the Stoics:

| Exercise | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Attention** (*prosochē*) | constant vigilance over oneself, presence of mind. "The fundamental Stoic attitude" |
| **Living in the present** | the past is gone, the future not ours; only the present is in our power |
| **Meditation** (*meletē*) | rehearsing principles until they're ready to hand ("have them at hand like a doctor his instruments") |
| **Premeditation of evils** (*praemeditatio malorum*) | imagining poverty, exile, death in advance so they don't overwhelm us |
| **Memorisation** | short sayings (*kephalaia*) to recall in difficulty |
| **Self-examination** | each evening: what did I do wrong? (Pythagoreans, Seneca). See [[Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans]] |
| **Reading, listening, research** | commentary on texts as a spiritual practice |
| **Self-mastery, indifference to indifferent things** | only virtue is good |
| **Learning to die** | Plato, *Phaedo* 67e: philosophy is "practice for dying": separating the soul from the passions of the body |
| **The view from above** | imagining the earth from the heights, seeing human affairs as tiny. Marcus: "Asia and Europe are corners of the universe" |
| **Dialogue** | Socratic questioning as an exercise performed on the other person and oneself |

- The Stoic keeps the fundamental rule **at hand** (*procheiron*): what depends on us and what doesn't. Keep life's events "before our eyes" in its light.
- *Meletē* = **meditation**: "an effort to assimilate an idea and make it come alive in the soul". Epicurus: "Meditate on these things night and day... and you shall live like a god among men."
- Exercises in the schools: discussing "**theses**" (Is death an evil? Should the wise man marry?) dialectically or rhetorically.
- The Platonic **dialogue** is a spiritual exercise: examination of conscience, "Know thyself". **Plotinus** "was present at the same time to himself and to others" (Porphyry).
- Learning to die: *Phaedo*; Seneca, Marcus.
- **Only the present is our happiness**: Epicurean: "the life of a foolish man is fearful and unpleasant; it is swept away into the future". Stoic: two ways of defining the present: as a limit (which doesn't exist, since time is divisible) or relative to **consciousness**: "delimit the present". Goethe's *Faust*: the instant as "the living symbol of the unexplorable".
- **The view from above**: the soul rising to observe the cosmos; the Montgolfier balloon (1783) as modern image; Baudelaire's "Elevation". Marcus: "the rational soul travels through the whole universe and the void around it".
- **The sage and the world**: Lucretius and Seneca see the world "as though for the first time". **Paul Klee**: the artist feels himself "a piece of nature".

## 3. Socrates

- Xenophon: when Hippias demanded Socrates' definition of justice, Socrates said: "**Instead of speaking of it, I make it understood by my acts.**"
- Socrates writes nothing, claims to know nothing, and lives his philosophy. The *dialogue* isn't to transmit doctrine but to make the interlocutor **examine himself**: "the unexamined life is not worth living".
- Socratic ignorance is an invitation to a conversion of the whole self, not a theory.
- He's the model of the philosopher as a person "out of place" (*atopos*: strange, unclassifiable), at odds with ordinary values.
- Plato effaces himself behind Socrates and avoids "I".
- People fell in love not with Socrates' beauty (he had none) but with his **love**, which in the *Symposium* is desire for the beauty we lack. Eros is the son of **Penia** (Poverty) and **Poros** ("way out", resource).
- Goethe: "If only I could be Alcibiades for one day and one night, and then die!"
- Socrates' last words ("Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius") read by Nietzsche as "O Crito, life is a disease."

## 4. Marcus Aurelius

- The *Meditations* (literally *To Himself*) aren't a treatise but **exercises**: Marcus writing the Stoic principles to himself, again and again, to keep them alive.
- The "opium" theory (he took theriac, which contained poppy juice, so his *Meditations*' cosmic visions were drug-induced): Hadot rejects it. The visions are exercises of the imagination, like Seneca's "place before your mind's eye the abyss of time".
- Structure follows Epictetus's three disciplines. Epictetus was the first Stoic to distinguish the **three activities of the soul** (desire, action, assent); Arrian grouped his *Manual* by them, and Marcus's substance comes from Epictetus:
    - **desire**: accept what happens as willed by the cosmic Logos
    - **action**: act justly for the common good
    - **assent**: judge impressions correctly; don't add "this is bad"
- The repetitions are the point: they're meditations, not arguments.
- "**Physical definitions**": describe things as they are, stripping conventional value: purple robes are sheep's wool dipped in shellfish blood; sexual union is "a little epilepsy" (Hippocrates and Democritus).
- We should "love" to see events happen, because universal nature "loves" to produce them.
- **Joy** vs pleasure: the Stoics distinguished *hēdonē* (pleasure) from *eupatheia* (joy). Seneca: joy is in "the conscience turned towards the good".

## 5. Christianity as "the True Philosophy"

The most important chapter for this site.

- Early Christian writers (Justin, Clement, Origen, the Cappadocians, Evagrius) called Christianity "**philosophy**", and later **monasticism** was called "the philosophy" or "the philosophy according to Christ".
- They took over the exercises directly:
    - **attention** → *prosochē*, **watchfulness** (*nepsis*): the title of the Philokalia is "the love of the beautiful" and its key word is *nepsis*. See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]]
    - **self-examination** → the monastic examination of thoughts; Dorotheus of Gaza
    - **remembrance of death** → *mnēmē thanatou*, a step on Climacus's ladder. See [[The Ladder of Divine Ascent]]
    - **apatheia** (Stoic) → **dispassion**, freedom from the passions (Evagrius)
    - **spiritual direction** and confession of thoughts
    - the monks kept the principles "at hand", but from the Gospel and the "sayings of the ancients" (the first monks)
- **Evagrius**'s scheme: *praktikē* (ethics) → *physikē* ("the Kingdom of Heaven": contemplation of the natures and *logoi* of things) → *theologikē* ("the Kingdom of God": knowledge of the Trinity). Two other key virtues: **penitence** and **obedience**.
- Hadot's work on Christianity: Ambrose borrowing from Plotinus ("a Plotinian ascesis put in the service of an Origenian mysticism of Jesus"); Ambrose identifies the Good with Christ.
    - "**Contamination**": paganism and Christianity each adopting ideas and practices of the adversary.
    - On **Augustine**: the images in *Confessions* II describing his inner state are theological; the **pear theft** is an account of original sin.
- Hadot's claim: Christian spirituality is to a large extent **ancient philosophy continued**, with Christ as the Logos.
- **What changes**: the exercises now aim at **union with God** by grace, not self-sufficiency; obedience and humility replace the sage's autonomy; the model is Christ, not the sage.

## 6. How Philosophy Became Theory

- In the Middle Ages, philosophy became the **handmaid of theology** in the universities: a set of concepts used by theology, while the way of life went to the monasteries.
    - Dom Jean Leclercq: in the monastic Middle Ages *philosophia* meant "not a theory or a way of knowing, but a lived wisdom". The medieval universities separated theology (faith) from philosophy (reason).
- **Philosophy as exegesis**: from the 1st c. BC (Andronicus edits Aristotle) to the Renaissance (Zabarella), the search for truth was a search for the meaning of authoritative texts.
    - Ancient works are often "**zetetic**" ("seeking"): written to answer a question for a particular audience, so they needn't be consistent on all points. That explains apparent inconsistencies in Plato and Aristotle.
    - "**Creative mistakes**": misreadings that produced new ideas (Porphyry's "being" distinction to solve a problem in Plato). Porphyry distinguished **being as infinitive** (pure, indeterminate activity: the first One) and **being as participle** (the first determined substance participating in it): a source of Boethius's *esse* / *id quod est*. See [[Aristotle East and West]] sec. 4.
- Modern philosophy inherited that: philosophy as **discourse** about theory, done by professionals, not a way of living. Hadot wants philosophy to recover its existential dimension (and finds some of it in Montaigne, Descartes's *Meditations*, Spinoza, Kant, Nietzsche, Wittgenstein).
- Husserl presents himself as heir to "Know thyself" from Socrates through Augustine to Descartes; Heidegger's authenticity as "lucid anticipation of death" recalls the exercise of death (though Heidegger distinguishes it from *meditatio mortis*).
- Against **Foucault**, who used Hadot for an "aesthetics of existence": the ancients weren't cultivating a beautiful **self** but trying to move **beyond** the self into universal reason and the cosmos.
