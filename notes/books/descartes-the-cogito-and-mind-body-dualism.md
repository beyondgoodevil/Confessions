Descartes (1596–1650) is called the father of modern philosophy because he started again from the **thinking self**: find something certain, then rebuild knowledge on it. The *Meditations* are six "days" of meditation (on the model of spiritual exercises; see [[Pierre Hadot: Philosophy as a Way of Life]]).

## Meditation I: Doubt

- Goal: "to demolish everything and start again right from the foundations".
- Not doubting each belief but the **foundations**:
    1. **Senses** sometimes deceive (a tower looks round from afar). But surely not that I'm sitting by the fire?
    2. **Dreaming**: there are no sure signs to tell waking from dreaming. But even in dreams, simple things (shape, number, 2 + 3 = 5) seem true.
    3. **The evil demon** (*malin génie*): a powerful deceiver could make me wrong even about maths. So everything can be doubted.

## Meditation II: The Cogito

- Even if a demon deceives me, **I** must exist to be deceived. "**I am, I exist**, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind." (The famous form "*cogito, ergo sum*" is from the *Discourse on Method*.)
- What am I? A **thinking thing** (*res cogitans*): something that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, refuses, imagines and senses.
- **The wax**: melt a piece of wax and every sensed quality changes (smell, colour, shape, hardness), yet it's the same wax. So I know it not by senses or imagination but by the **intellect** alone. And the mind is better known than the body.

## Meditation III: God (the Trademark Argument)

- **Rule**: whatever I perceive **clearly and distinctly** is true. But a deceiving God could undermine even this; so I need to know God exists and isn't a deceiver.
- Ideas have **objective reality** (what they represent). The cause of an idea must have at least as much **formal reality** (actual being) as the idea has objective reality.
- I have the idea of an **infinite, perfect** being. I'm finite and imperfect; I couldn't have produced it. So only an infinite being could be its cause. God exists.
- God put this idea in me "like the mark of the craftsman stamped on his work": hence "trademark".

## Meditation IV: Error

- If God is no deceiver, why do I err? Because my **will** (infinite in scope) goes beyond my **understanding** (finite). I affirm what I don't clearly perceive.
- Fix: suspend judgement unless clear and distinct.

## Meditation V: Matter and the Ontological Argument

- **Essence of matter**: **extension** (length, breadth, depth), knowable mathematically.
- **Ontological argument**: existence belongs to God's essence as having three angles = two right angles belongs to a triangle's. So God exists. See [[The Ontological Argument]].

## Meditation VI: Mind and Body

**The real distinction**
1. I can clearly and distinctly conceive mind without body, and body without mind.
2. Whatever I can clearly and distinctly conceive apart, God can make exist apart.
3. So mind and body are **really distinct** substances.

Also the **divisibility** argument: the body is divisible, the mind isn't (I can't find half a mind). So they differ.

- Mind (*res cogitans*): thinking, unextended. Body (*res extensa*): extended, non-thinking.
- The external world exists: God gave me a strong inclination to believe bodies cause my sensations; if they didn't, God would be a deceiver.
- Yet mind and body are **closely united**: "I am not merely present in my body as a sailor in a ship"; pain is felt, not just observed.

## Objections

| Objection | Who | Point |
|---|---|---|
| **Cartesian circle** | Arnauld | clear and distinct perception proves God; God guarantees clear and distinct perception. Circular |
| **Interaction** | Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (1643) | how can an unextended mind move an extended body? Causation by contact needs extension. Descartes's answer (the pineal gland) didn't help. See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]] |
| **Conceivability ≠ possibility** | Arnauld | I can conceive a right triangle without knowing Pythagoras's theorem holds; that doesn't make them separable |
| **"I think" assumes an "I"** | Lichtenberg, Nietzsche | at most "there is thinking" |
| **Animals** | many | Descartes said animals are machines without souls. Absurd consequences |

## Death of the Soul: The Story Afterwards

Barrett's narrative in three parts:
- **Gathering of a new age**: 17th c. science treats nature as mechanism; the soul is the one thing left outside it (Descartes).
- **The pivot**: Locke, Hume and Kant: the soul is reduced to a bundle, or to a function of experience; Kant makes it unknowable.
- **Dispersion**: 19th–20th c.: psychology without a soul, behaviourism, the mind as computer. The soul disappears.

The upshot: once nature was taken to be **mere extension**, the mind had nowhere to fit, and in the end it was eliminated.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- Descartes's dualism is **not** the patristic or Aristotelian view. For Aristotle and the Fathers the soul is the **form of the body**, and a human is **one** thing, soul and body together. Descartes makes two substances and then can't connect them. Feser's account of how the moderns created this problem is in [[Edward Feser: The Last Superstition]] sec. 5.
- The method (start from the self, doubt everything) became the default of modern philosophy: the self as a private inner theatre. Orthodox critics (and Heidegger) see it as the root of modern individualism. Compare [[John Zizioulas: Being as Communion]]: the person exists in communion, not as a self-enclosed thinker.
- But the cogito is still a good reply to total scepticism, and the argument that the mind isn't extended is close to Augustine's ("if I am deceived, I am": *City of God* XI.26).
