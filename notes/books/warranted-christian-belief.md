The third volume of Plantinga's warrant trilogy. He doesn't argue that Christianity is **true**. He argues that the main objection, that it's irrational or unjustified even if true (the **de jure** objection), fails. If Christianity is true, it's probably warranted.

## 1. De Facto and De Jure

- **De facto**: is Christianity **true**?
- **De jure**: even if it is, is believing it **rational, justified, warranted**? Most objections today are of this kind ("there's no evidence", "it's wishful thinking").
- Plantinga's question: is there a de jure objection that **doesn't depend** on Christianity being false?

## 2. Can We Even Talk About God?

- **Kant**: our concepts apply only to phenomena, so we can't refer to God (the noumenal). Plantinga: then Kant can't say that either; and it doesn't follow from anything Kant proves.
- **Hick, Kaufman**: "the Real" is beyond all our concepts. Plantinga: if no concept applies, not even "the Real" does; self-refuting.

## 3. Classical Foundationalism Fails

- **Classical foundationalism**: a belief is justified only if it's self-evident, incorrigible (about one's own mental states), or evident to the senses, or argued for from such.
- But that principle itself is none of these. So it's **self-refuting**. Also, by its standard we couldn't believe in other minds, the past, or the external world.
- So belief in God doesn't need to be argued from such foundations. It can be **properly basic**: rational without being based on other beliefs, like belief in other minds. (Plantinga's earlier *God and Other Minds* made this parallel.)

## 4. Warrant as Proper Function

A belief has **warrant** (what makes true belief knowledge) if it's produced by cognitive faculties that are:
1. **functioning properly** (not damaged)
2. in an **appropriate environment** (the one they were designed for)
3. according to a **design plan aimed at truth** (not at survival or comfort)
4. a design plan that's **successful**: a high probability of truth

## 5. Freud and Marx

- **Freud**: belief in God is wish-fulfilment. **Marx**: it's the result of a perverted social order.
- Both are really saying belief in God isn't produced by **properly functioning faculties aimed at truth**: a de jure objection about warrant.
- But they **assume** atheism: only if there's no God is belief in God a malfunction. If there is a God, He may well have made us to know Him, and then belief comes from proper function.
- **So**: the de jure question isn't independent of the de facto one. If Christianity is true, it probably *is* warranted. The real question is just whether it's true. "There aren't any de jure objections independent of de facto ones."

## 6. The Aquinas/Calvin Model

- **Sensus divinitatis** (Calvin, after Rom 1:19-20; Aquinas: a general, confused knowledge of God is naturally implanted): a faculty producing belief in God in certain circumstances: the starry sky, danger, guilt, gratitude, beauty.
- Beliefs formed this way are **basic** (not inferred), and have warrant if the faculty works properly.
- **Sin** damages the faculty (ch. 7): the noetic effects of sin. That's why not everyone believes. Rom 1:21: "their foolish heart was darkened."

## 7. The Extended Model

For full **Christian** belief (Trinity, Incarnation, Atonement), not just theism:
1. **Scripture**: God's revelation.
2. **The internal instigation of the Holy Spirit** (Aquinas) / the **internal testimony of the Spirit** (Calvin): the Spirit moves us to believe what Scripture teaches.
3. **Faith**: the result, a sure knowledge, "revealed to our minds and sealed upon our hearts" (Calvin).
- Faith in this sense is a **kind of knowledge**, warranted by the Spirit's work, not a leap against evidence.

## 8. Defeaters?

| Alleged defeater | Plantinga |
|---|---|
| **Historical biblical criticism** (ch. 12) | "Historical Biblical Criticism" methodologically excludes revelation (Troeltsch). Its results depend on its assumptions; a believer doesn't have to share them |
| **Postmodernism** | its denial of truth isn't a threat; it's equally a problem for everyone |
| **Pluralism** | the believer who knows other religions and still believes isn't arrogant: anyone who believes anything disagrees with someone. A believer may think his own faculties are working well and others' not |
| **Evil** (ch. 14) | evil is a serious objection to non-believers, but for a believer with the experience of God, it doesn't defeat. A good God might have reasons we can't see (the "noseeum" point) |

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The most famous move: no de jure objection independent of de facto. So the atheist must argue Christianity is *false*; calling it irrational isn't enough.
- Close to the Orthodox view: knowledge of God isn't a conclusion of argument but given through a faculty (the *nous*) that sin darkens and grace heals. Plantinga's *sensus divinitatis* is basically the nous. See [[Nous]], [[Religious Experience as Evidence]].
- Weakness: the model is circular-looking from outside (it's warranted if true). Plantinga admits it: the model shows *possibility*, not that one should believe.

## Chapter by Chapter

**1. Kant**
- Christians assume we can refer to God ("the all-powerful, all-knowing creator") and predicate properties of Him. Kaufman and others deny this, drawing on Kant. (Plantinga also files the logical positivists' **verifiability criterion** and Wittgensteinians like D. Z. Phillips here: "positivism by other means".)
- Two readings of Kant: the **two-world** picture (things in themselves, *Dinge an sich*, are a separate realm) and the **one-world** picture (the same things considered as they appear and as they are). On the one-world reading, if we can't apply concepts to the *Dinge*, nothing is wise or good at all: nothing special about God.
- The **antinomies** (e.g. the world had a beginning / didn't) are supposed to show reason overreaches. Plantinga goes through the argument for a beginning: it assumes an infinite series can't be "completed", but events could take a second, then half, then a quarter... So there's no conclusive argument either way.
- **Self-refutation**: if we can't refer to or think about things *an sich*, how could we grasp the premise saying so?

**2. Kaufman and Hick**
- **Kaufman**: the "real referent" of "God" is an unknown X; Christians worship only the "available referent", an imaginative construct. Plantinga: then why call it "the real referent"? And saying Christians worship a construct is "outrageous".
- "Cultural and linguistic frames shape all our thinking": if that casts doubt on Christianity, it casts doubt on itself too.
- **Hick**: "we cannot speak of the Real *an sich* in literal terms"; it's "the noumenal ground of the encountered gods". Plantinga: if no substantial property applies, then the Real isn't wise, so it has non-wisdom; isn't loving, so it has non-love... And "being the ground of the gods" is itself a substantial property. Hick's distinction between "substantial" and "formal" properties doesn't hold.
- Calvin doesn't teach that we know nothing of God's essence.
- The irony: Hick's view tells the believer "my only comfort in life and in death is that I know the sad truth: believing Christianity has beneficial effects, but its teachings are false" (contrast the Heidelberg Catechism Q. 1: "my only comfort... is that I belong to my faithful Saviour Jesus Christ"). That's "intellectual imperialism".

**3. Justification and the Classical Picture**
- **Evidentialism**: belief in God is rational only if supported by good evidence (a better name: "evidentialism with respect to belief in God").
- **Locke** is the source: we must regulate opinion by **reason**, proportioning assent to evidence. Then what room for "the great things of the Gospel" (Jonathan Edwards)? Locke allows faith only where reason shows the proposition comes from God.
- **Clifford**: "it is wrong, always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." **Deontologism** (we have intellectual duties) + evidentialism.
- **Mackie**, *The Miracle of Theism*: theism isn't "rationally defensible" since improbable on the evidence.
- **Classical foundationalism** (CP) is **self-referentially incoherent**: it isn't properly basic by its own standard, and can't be argued for from such beliefs.
- On **deontological** justification, a sincere believer who has done her best is justified, whatever her belief (Paul: "if a man considers a thing impure, to him it is impure", Rom 14:14; even the Pine Rest patient proud of inventing "rotational reproduction" can be blameless). So justification in this sense can't be what the objector means.

**4. Rationality**
- Five senses of "rational": (1) having reason (Aristotelian), (2) proper function, (3) conforming to the deliverances of reason, (4) means-end rationality (*Zweckrationalität*), (5) deontological.
- **Internal rationality**: forming the right beliefs in response to experience (sensory imagery and "**doxastic experience**", the felt pull of a belief: "impulsional evidence").
- **Alston**: Christian mystical practice (CMP) is rational to engage in, like sense perception (SP), since it's a socially established practice not shown unreliable. Plantinga: CMP and SP are "uncredentialed": no non-circular argument for their reliability. Asking whether it would be rational to adopt SP from an "original position" with only reason, memory and introspection is beside the point.
- Still no de jure question found.

**5. Warrant and the Freud-and-Marx Complaint**
- **Freud**: theistic belief arises from **wish-fulfilment**; it's an "illusion". Of religion, art and philosophy, only religion "is to be taken seriously as an enemy" of science.
- **Hume** too: religion arises not from "the pure love of truth" but from hopes and fears.
- **Marx**: religion is "the opium of the people", produced by a disordered society.
- **Warrant** (from *Warrant and Proper Function*): true belief isn't knowledge if true "just by accident". A belief has warrant if produced by faculties functioning properly, in the right **cognitive environment** (both the large-scale "maxi-environment" and the local "mini-environment"), by a design plan aimed at truth, with high probability of success.
- The F&M complaint really says religious belief fails the **proper-function** condition (Freud) or the **environment** condition (Marx).

**6. Warranted Belief in God (the A/C model)**
- A "**model**": a way it could be that belief in God is warranted. Plantinga claims it's **epistemically possible** and that there are no cogent objections to it being true (that don't presuppose God doesn't exist).
- Rom 1:19-20: "what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them." Calvin: the *sensus divinitatis*, from the "mother's womb". Seventy years of failed Soviet efforts to uproot Christianity "tend to confirm" its strength.
- Theistic beliefs are **occasioned** by circumstances (the starry heavens, danger, guilt, gratitude), not **inferred** from them: like remembering what you had for breakfast. "Perception" in an analogically extended sense.
- The naturalist who thinks the order of things is due to "the ensemble of natural laws" in a way believes *de re* of God that He is that ensemble: knowledge "admitting of many errors".
- **The key result**: if theism is **true**, it very likely **has** warrant (God would want us to know Him); if false, it very likely doesn't. So there's no de jure objection independent of the de facto one.
- **Against Freud**: the alleged evidence doesn't fit. Christianity includes that we are sinners deserving wrath, "broken, wretched": not obviously what wish-fulfilment would invent.

**7. Sin and Its Cognitive Consequences**
- Plantinga doesn't know an argument for Christian belief likely to convince someone who doesn't accept its conclusion; and if Christianity is true, the best way to hold it isn't as a conclusion of argument anyway.
- **Aquinas**: in corrupted nature man "falls short even of what he can do by his nature"; grace is needed even to live up to our original nature.
- Sin is first a **madness of the will**: Milton's Satan, "Evil, be thou my good", is perfectly intelligible.
- **Sloth** (acedia): not laziness but "spiritual deadness, blindness, torpor, a failure to be aware of God's presence".
- **Calvin**: "As soon as ever we depart from Christ, there is nothing... respecting which we are not necessarily deceived": at least, we miss the most important truth about everything.
- **Hume's skepticism** about reason ends in a defeater for itself; Reid: testing a man's honesty by asking him is ridiculous, and the same with reason.
- **The EAAN** (evolutionary argument against naturalism) appears here: P(R/N&E) is low or inscrutable. Possibilities for how belief relates to behaviour: **epiphenomenalism** (belief causes nothing), **semantic epiphenomenalism** (belief causes behaviour but its **content** doesn't: Cummins says this is the received view), and the commonsense view. Even on the commonsense view, adaptive behaviour is "at best a third-rate reason" to think our beliefs mostly true. See [[The Argument from Reason]] sec. 7.

**8. The Extended A/C Model: Revealed to Our Minds**
- Three elements: **Scripture**, the **internal instigation of the Holy Spirit** (IIHS), and **faith**.
- Faith = Calvin's "firm and certain knowledge of God's benevolence towards us, founded upon the truth of the freely given promise in Christ, both **revealed to our minds** and **sealed upon our hearts** through the Holy Spirit."
- The Spirit's work is a **special, supernatural** cognitive process, not part of our original equipment.
- The content of faith is "the great things of the gospel": what's in the intersection of the great creeds. These are **self-authenticating**; the rest of Scripture not necessarily so.
- "**Fundamentalist**" as used today is mostly a term of abuse ("If you called someone a sumbitch, would you feel obliged first to define the term?").
- **Why not an argument?** Plantinga runs the probabilistic case: P(theism on K) × P(Jesus rose / theism) × P(He taught X)... The **probabilities multiply** and shrink, so historical arguments alone give a low probability for the full Christian story (the "**dwindling probabilities**" argument). Faith's warrant doesn't depend on such arguments.
- "Unless a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3): **cognitive renewal**.

**9. The Testimonial Model: Sealed upon Our Hearts**
- **Aquinas** repeats "the Holy Spirit makes us lovers of God" four times in five pages. **Luther**: believing *in* God vs believing *that* He exists.
- **Jonathan Edwards**, *Religious Affections*: the regenerate have a **new sense**, "a new kind of perception", of the beauty and excellency of divine things, unavailable to "natural men".
- Which comes first, loving the gospel or believing it? Perhaps the Spirit first heals the "madness of our wills", so we love God above self, and then believe.
- **Freud's sublimation** (religion is redirected sexual energy) reversed: love for God is fundamental, and sexual **eros** is a sign or pointer to it. C. S. Lewis's "inconsolable secret": we don't want merely to see beauty but to be united with it.
- Edwards: "The infinite happiness of the Father consists in the enjoyment of His Son": not agape but something like eros, "transposed into another key".

**10. Objections**
- The model isn't an **argument from religious experience**: no one argues "I feel God's presence, therefore God exists"; the belief is basic.
- **Gale**: religious experience isn't cognitive since a "veridical perception of God" is impossible. Plantinga: experience of God can come and go (asleep, distracted, angry at God) like any perception.
- **The Great Pumpkin objection** ("Son of Great Pumpkin"): if belief in God can be properly basic, why not belief in the Great Pumpkin? **Michael Martin**: voodoo believers could say the same, so reformed epistemology is "radically relativistic". Plantinga: Reformed epistemologists don't have to accept that any belief is properly basic for anyone; and the objection isn't independent of the truth of Christian belief.

**11. Defeaters and Defeat**
- A **defeater**: a belief that makes it irrational to keep another. **Rebutting** (evidence it's false) vs **undercutting** (evidence the source is unreliable). The fake-barn case.
- A defeater can arise irrationally and still defeat (a cognitive malfunction making something seem obvious).
- **Projection theories** (Freud, Marx, Durkheim) at most offer undercutting defeaters, and even if belief in God can be "explained" without God, theism may explain much else.

**12. Two (or More) Kinds of Scripture Scholarship**
- **Traditional biblical commentary**: Scripture is a unified communication from God; what it teaches is true, though it's not always easy to tell what it teaches.
- **Historical biblical criticism** (HBC): brackets what is known by faith, proceeds "scientifically".
- **Troeltschian** HBC assumes **methodological naturalism** (no miracles: the "principle of analogy"); **Duhemian** HBC uses only premises everyone accepts.
- Examples: Strauss (1835): sacred history is "only fable" for the enlightened; Morton Smith: Jesus a magician and homosexual; Lüdemann: the resurrection is "an empty formula that must be rejected by anyone holding a scientific worldview".
- The "**preemptive 'we'**": "we" moderns can't believe in miracles, as if those who disagree don't exist.
- The conclusion: Troeltschian HBC starts from assumptions the Christian rejects; Duhemian HBC ignores part of what the Christian takes as evidence. Either way it isn't a defeater: "I needn't pay attention to it." See [[Sola Scriptura: The Main Arguments and Replies]].

**13. Postmodernism and Pluralism**
- Some postmodern claims (God is dead, no objective morality, no truth) conflict with Christianity, but postmoderns "don't ordinarily give arguments for anything at all".
- **Rorty**: "the idea that truth was made rather than found" took hold two centuries ago. Plantinga: we make **sentences**; we don't make it true that there were dinosaurs.
- **Pluralism**: the "**Accident of birth**" argument: if I'd been born elsewhere I'd believe something else, so my belief isn't warranted. Too strong: it would apply to the pluralist's own belief too.
- **Exclusivism** charged with arbitrariness and arrogance. Options: the "abstemious pluralist" who withholds belief. But withholding is also a position, and someone has to be wrong.
- **Newman**: something beyond intellect ("acuteness, sagacity, subtlety") is needed to grasp religious truth. Believers needn't think their beliefs are on an epistemic par with those who disagree.

**14. Suffering and Evil**
- **Mackie's logical argument** (God and evil inconsistent) is now generally thought to fail (after Plantinga's free will defense).
- The **"noseeum" inference** ("I can't see what reason God could have, so probably He has none"): Wykstra, Alston, van Inwagen: given the epistemic distance between us and God, why would we see His reasons?
- **Rowe**: counterbalanced by arguments of equal strength for the opposite.
- **Draper**: theism is "evidentially challenged". Plantinga: a theist can admit that and still rationally believe; if Christian belief has warrant, it isn't defeated.
- **Non-argumentative defeaters**: evil can cause anger at God, like Job. But a believer may also see suffering as sharing in Christ's ("fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him", Rom 8:17), even an occasion of gratitude. Reference to Stump's paper on Job. See [[Wandering in Darkness]].
- Final point: any de jure objection to Christian belief either becomes or presupposes a de facto objection.
