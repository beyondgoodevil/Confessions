The third volume of Plantinga's warrant trilogy. He doesn't argue that Christianity is **true**. He argues that the main objection, that it's irrational or unjustified even if true (the **de jure** objection), fails. If Christianity is true, it's probably warranted.

## 1. De Facto and De Jure

- **De facto**: is Christianity **true**?
- **De jure**: even if it is, is believing it **rational, justified, warranted**? Most objections today are of this kind ("there's no evidence", "it's wishful thinking").
- Plantinga's question: is there a de jure objection that **doesn't depend** on Christianity being false?

## 2. Can We Even Talk About God?

- **Kant**: our concepts apply only to phenomena, so we can't refer to God (the noumenal). Plantinga: then Kant can't say that either; and it doesn't follow from anything Kant proves.
- **Hick, Kaufman**: "the Real" is beyond all our concepts. Plantinga: if no concept applies, not even "the Real" does; self-refuting.

## 3. Classical Foundationalism Fails

- **Classical foundationalism**: a belief is justified only if it's self-evident, incorrigible (about one's own mental states), or evident to the senses, or argued for from such.
- But that principle itself is none of these. So it's **self-refuting**. Also, by its standard we couldn't believe in other minds, the past, or the external world.
- So belief in God doesn't need to be argued from such foundations. It can be **properly basic**: rational without being based on other beliefs, like belief in other minds. (Plantinga's earlier *God and Other Minds* made this parallel.)

## 4. Warrant as Proper Function

A belief has **warrant** (what makes true belief knowledge) if it's produced by cognitive faculties that are:
1. **functioning properly** (not damaged)
2. in an **appropriate environment** (the one they were designed for)
3. according to a **design plan aimed at truth** (not at survival or comfort)
4. a design plan that's **successful**: a high probability of truth

## 5. Freud and Marx

- **Freud**: belief in God is wish-fulfilment. **Marx**: it's the result of a perverted social order.
- Both are really saying belief in God isn't produced by **properly functioning faculties aimed at truth**: a de jure objection about warrant.
- But they **assume** atheism: only if there's no God is belief in God a malfunction. If there is a God, He may well have made us to know Him, and then belief comes from proper function.
- **So**: the de jure question isn't independent of the de facto one. If Christianity is true, it probably *is* warranted. The real question is just whether it's true. "There aren't any de jure objections independent of de facto ones."

## 6. The Aquinas/Calvin Model

- **Sensus divinitatis** (Calvin, after Rom 1:19-20; Aquinas: a general, confused knowledge of God is naturally implanted): a faculty producing belief in God in certain circumstances: the starry sky, danger, guilt, gratitude, beauty.
- Beliefs formed this way are **basic** (not inferred), and have warrant if the faculty works properly.
- **Sin** damages the faculty (ch. 7): the noetic effects of sin. That's why not everyone believes. Rom 1:21: "their foolish heart was darkened."

## 7. The Extended Model

For full **Christian** belief (Trinity, Incarnation, Atonement), not just theism:
1. **Scripture**: God's revelation.
2. **The internal instigation of the Holy Spirit** (Aquinas) / the **internal testimony of the Spirit** (Calvin): the Spirit moves us to believe what Scripture teaches.
3. **Faith**: the result, a sure knowledge, "revealed to our minds and sealed upon our hearts" (Calvin).
- Faith in this sense is a **kind of knowledge**, warranted by the Spirit's work, not a leap against evidence.

## 8. Defeaters?

| Alleged defeater | Plantinga |
|---|---|
| **Historical biblical criticism** (ch. 12) | "Historical Biblical Criticism" methodologically excludes revelation (Troeltsch). Its results depend on its assumptions; a believer doesn't have to share them |
| **Postmodernism** | its denial of truth isn't a threat; it's equally a problem for everyone |
| **Pluralism** | the believer who knows other religions and still believes isn't arrogant: anyone who believes anything disagrees with someone. A believer may think his own faculties are working well and others' not |
| **Evil** (ch. 14) | evil is a serious objection to non-believers, but for a believer with the experience of God, it doesn't defeat. A good God might have reasons we can't see (the "noseeum" point) |

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The most famous move: no de jure objection independent of de facto. So the atheist must argue Christianity is *false*; calling it irrational isn't enough.
- Close to the Orthodox view: knowledge of God isn't a conclusion of argument but given through a faculty (the *nous*) that sin darkens and grace heals. Plantinga's *sensus divinitatis* is basically the nous. See [[Nous]], [[Religious Experience as Evidence]].
- Weakness: the model is circular-looking from outside (it's warranted if true). Plantinga admits it: the model shows *possibility*, not that one should believe.
