A collection of essays, not a single argument, but one idea runs through all of them: **there is no true being without communion**. Nothing exists as an isolated individual, not even God. And the place where this is realised is the **Eucharist**, which makes the Church.

## 1. Personhood and Being

**The Greek problem**
- Greek philosophy thought of being as a closed, necessary cosmos. The One, or the world, is what's ultimate; particular things come and go. There's no room for the **person** as unique and free.
- The Greek *prosōpon* was a mask: a role on top of what you really are.
- Roman *persona*: a legal or social role.

**The patristic revolution**
- The Cappadocians identified ***hypostasis*** (concrete being) with ***prosōpon*** (person). So the person isn't a mask added to being. It **is** being in its concrete form.
- **The Father as cause**: God's being comes from a **person**, the Father, not from an impersonal substance. The Father freely begets the Son and brings forth the Spirit. So "God exists" because of a free, personal act of love. See [[Monarchical Trinitarianism]].
- So: **"being" is "communion"**. God is not first one substance and then three persons in relation. The communion of persons *is* the one God.
- Freedom and love are therefore ontological, not just moral.

**Two kinds of human existence**

| Hypostasis of biological existence | Hypostasis of ecclesial existence |
|---|---|
| born from *eros* and natural necessity | born from baptism, "from above" (John 3:3) |
| individualism: I exist as separate from others | person: I exist in relation |
| ends in death: nature wins over the person | eschatological: open to the Kingdom |
| the "tragic" human condition | a new way of being, given in Christ |

- Biological existence is caught between two failures: it wants personal communion but produces **individuals**, and it wants to survive but ends in **death**.
- Baptism and the Eucharist give a new hypostasis: you exist not because you were born, but because you are loved by God and in communion. That's salvation as **ontological**, not just moral.

## 2. Truth and Communion

Six patristic approaches to truth (the six sections):
1. **Logos** (Justin, Clement, Origen): truth as the rational structure of the world. Risk: Greek cosmology.
2. **Eucharistic** (Ignatius, Irenaeus): truth as life, given in communion.
3. **Trinitarian** (Athanasius, Cappadocians): truth is relational, because God is.
4. **Apophatic** (Dionysius): truth beyond concepts.
5. **Christological** (Maximus): truth as the person of Christ, uniting created and uncreated.
6. **Eikon**: truth as image, a reflection of the eschatological reality.

The point: truth is **"I am the truth"** (John 14:6), a person, met in communion, not a set of propositions.

## 3. Christ, the Spirit and the Church

- Christ is not an individual first and then a community. He is a **corporate personality**: "the one and the many".
- What makes Christ "the many" is the **Spirit**. The Spirit constitutes the Church, not just inspires it.
- So Christology has to be **conditioned by Pneumatology**. Zizioulas criticises the West (and Lossky) for separating the two economies. See [[The Holy Spirit in Orthodox Theology]].
- The Spirit also brings the **eschaton** into history: the Church is the Kingdom anticipated.

## 4. Eucharist and the Church

- **"The Eucharist makes the Church."** It's not one sacrament among others but the event in which the Church *is* the Church.
- **Catholicity** isn't geographical or numerical. Each local Eucharistic assembly, around one bishop, is the whole Church: the whole Christ is present in each.
- The **bishop** stands in the place of Christ at the Eucharist (Ignatius: "where the bishop is, there let the people be"). One city, one bishop, one Eucharist.
- **Apostolic succession**: not just a chain of hands from the past (historical) but also the community's being gathered around Christ as the apostles will be in the Kingdom (eschatological).
- **Laity** are an order too: everyone is ordained in baptism-chrismation. No one exists in the Church outside a relation.

## 5. Criticisms

- **Reading the Cappadocians** (Turcescu, Larchet, Behr): they didn't have a modern concept of "person", and they usually *don't* say the Father's person is the cause of the divine essence. Zizioulas is accused of reading personalist philosophy (Heidegger, Buber, Levinas) back into the Fathers.
- **Person over nature** goes too far: the Fathers make the essence and persons equally primary. Danger of making the essence secondary.
- **Eucharistic ecclesiology** (from Afanasiev) can make the Church look like a sum of local Churches, weakening primacy or universal structure. Zizioulas himself tried to balance this with a "primacy" at each level.
- **Lossky vs Zizioulas**: Lossky separates Christ (nature) and Spirit (persons); Zizioulas unites them. See [[The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church]].

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The main idea is powerful: God's oneness comes from the Father's love, not from an impersonal substance, which ties into the [[The Problem of the One and the Many]] and the anti-Augustinian critique.
- The "biological vs ecclesial hypostasis" is a clear way to state the Orthodox view of the Fall and salvation: death is the outcome of existing as an individual. See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]].
- Read with caution on the patristic claims; read with Lossky and Schmemann for the rest.

## More from the Book

**Person and mask (ch. 1)**
- In Greek theatre the *prosōpon* (mask) wasn't unrelated to the person, but the relationship was **tragic**: the actor tries to be free of his given nature, and the mask shows the longing but can't make it real. For the Greeks, "to be a person means that he has something added to his being."
- The Latin *una substantia, tres personae* wasn't accepted in the East at first because *persona* had no **ontological** content and sounded **Sabellian** (God in three roles).
- After Aristotle, *hypostasis* gradually displaced *hypokeimenon* (which had a material sense) and came to mean "real, concrete being" as opposed to the merely apparent. The Cappadocians then identified it with *prosōpon*: the "revolution".
- **"Substance never exists in a naked state"**: never without a hypostasis, a "mode of existence" (*tropos hyparxeōs*). The one divine substance is God's being **only because** it has three modes of existence, which it owes not to the substance but to one person, the Father. "Outside the Trinity there is no God."

**Freedom and necessity**
- The ultimate challenge to a person's freedom is the **necessity of existence**: I didn't choose to exist. Only God is free in the positive sense, since nothing is "given" to Him.
- A created person "subsists" as necessity, not freedom: the "first passion" is ontological necessity.
- **"The person does not die only because it is loved and loves."** Outside love a person becomes "a thing" without identity or name, without a face. Life and love are identified in the person.
- "Only-begotten" in John means not just a unique way of generation but "Him who is **beloved** in a unique manner".
- **Baptism** is adoption: the identification of a person's hypostasis with the hypostasis of the Son. That's the "ecclesial hypostasis", realised in history in the Church.
- Why "the love of money is the root of all evil" (1 Tim 6:10): it locates the security of one's being in **substance** (and *ousia* came early to mean "property, possessions") instead of in the person. Eros and the body aren't to be abandoned but "hypostasised" in the ecclesial way.

**Truth (ch. 2)**
- In the OT, truth (*emeth*) is God's **faithfulness**: His word is sure, His oath unbreakable (Ps 132:11). Truth happens in history, through promises.
- Against **Justin and Origen**: they made Christ the truth by participation in a pre-existing Wisdom/Logos: "Christ participates in wisdom", not wisdom depending on Christ. Zizioulas: that inverts "I am the truth".
- **Athanasius** made being relational: "Father" is a relational term, so substance has relation built in. He distinguished God's **will** (creation) from His **nature** (the Son's generation).
- **Maximus**: "God does not know things according to their own nature, but He recognises them as the realisations of His own will." God's knowledge is His love.
- **Apophaticism** isn't agnosticism: its aim was to move truth out of Greek ontology into **love and communion**. The Greek philosophers' "beyond" was the *nous* purified; the Fathers' "beyond" (*hyper*-) goes beyond the *nous* itself.
- Truth "**springs up from our midst**": the community itself becomes the truth (the "pillar of truth", 1 Tim 3:15). That's why the ancient councils' definitions end in **anathemas**: drawing the boundary of the communion more than defining.

**Christ, the Spirit, the Church (ch. 3)**
- **Khomiakov** (influenced by Möhler) made the Church a "charismatic society". **Florovsky** answered that ecclesiology is "**a chapter of Christology**", but didn't solve how Christology and Pneumatology fit.
- There's only **one** economy with a history: the Christ event. Even Pentecost belongs to it.
- The Spirit is "**beyond history**": when He acts in history it's to bring in the **eschaton**. He raises Christ, makes Christ the eschatological "last Adam", and makes Christ "not one but **many**".

**Catholicity (ch. 4)**
- Early Christians spoke of "catholic **Churches**" in the plural. In *Martyrdom of Polycarp*, if *katholikē* meant "universal", the phrase "the catholic Church throughout the world" would be a tautology ("the universal Church in the universe").
- For Ignatius there's no "universal Church" behind the local one: the whole Christ and the whole Church are in the **local episcopal community**.
- Catholicity is first **Christological**, not moral. And it's a call not to conquer the world but to a **kenotic** struggle against "anti-catholic demonic powers".
- The **name of the community** is mentioned in the prayer at a bishop's ordination: no bishop without a flock. Even **titular bishops** (bishops without a real diocese) are ordained to the name of some diocese, even one that no longer exists.
- The **layman** isn't a "non-ordained" person: baptism and chrismation are an ordination into the order of laity.

**Apostolic succession (ch. 5)**
- Two approaches:
    - **historical**: the apostles are a link to a past event; succession is a line through time; apostolicity comes "from the side of the past"
    - **eschatological**: the apostles are the college that will judge Israel (Matt 19:28); "James and the presbyters" in Acts 15 represent the eschatological court
- Early sources speak of apostolic "**successions**" (plural), just as of "catholic Churches". Each episcopal community contains the **whole** succession.
- Cyprian: "*apostolus, id est episcopus*". Ignatius: the bishop wasn't necessarily the teacher; famous teachers were presbyters (Clement, Origen). So succession wasn't originally a succession of **teaching**.
- The danger in losing the eschatological side: looking for a "*vicarius Christi*" above the episcopal college (an implied critique of the papacy).

**Ministry (ch. 6)**
- There are no "non-ordained" persons in the Church. Ordination aims at making man "not an individual but a **person**", an "ek-static" being, related.
- No "priesthood" as a general substance (*sacerdotium*) passed from ordainer to ordained. Ministry is defined by **communion** (*koinōnia*), neither by "ontologism" (an indelible character in the individual) nor by "functionalism".
