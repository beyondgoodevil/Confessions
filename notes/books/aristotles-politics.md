The *Politics* is the second half of a single inquiry with the *Nicomachean Ethics*: the Ethics asks what the good life is; the Politics asks what kind of community makes it possible. "The end of the city is the good life." Eight books, probably lecture notes, not fully unified. See [[Virtue Ethics: Aristotle and the Fathers]].

Names in brackets below (Bradley, Keyt, Irwin…) are the authors of essays in the Keyt and Miller companion.

## Book I: The City and the Household

**The city exists by nature**
- Man and woman form a household; households form a village; villages form a **city** (*polis*), which is **self-sufficient**.
    - Aristotle analyses the compound (the polis) into its uncompounded elements, then gives a **genetic** account: household → village → polis (Kullmann).
    - The village is "an offshoot or colony (*apoikia*) of the household (*oikia*)"; its members are "men of the same milk" (Bradley).
    - Homer's Cyclopes lived "scattered" (*sporades*): the earliest humans lived that way (from Plato's *Laws*).
    - A lower stage exists for its own sake **and** as a step to the higher (Bradley).
    - *Eudemian Ethics*: "in the household primarily lie the origins and sources of friendship, constitution and the just."
    - Humans, unlike other animals, associate not only for procreation but for "the other activities of life" (Adkins).
- The city "comes into being for the sake of life, but exists for the sake of the **good life**".
- **"Man is by nature a political animal."** More political than bees, because he has **speech** (*logos*), which can express the just and unjust. Whoever can live without a city "is either a beast or a god".
    - More intelligent animals with memory treat offspring more "politically". The "political" is the fundamental human characteristic (Kullmann).
    - Humans "dualise" between political and dispersed living (*History of Animals*), but the solitary is an exception (Cooper).
- The city is **prior** to the individual by nature, as the whole is prior to the part: a hand cut off is a hand only in name.
    - The **political impulse is genetically ingrained**: "a foot or a hand detached from the body can only be called foot or hand homonymously"; so the isolated man (Kullmann).
- **Three basic theorems** (Keyt): the polis exists by nature; man is by nature political; the polis is **prior** in nature to the individual.
    - Keyt analyses the arguments formally and finds them weak: "nature is an end" ("what each thing is when its coming-into-being is completed, this we call its nature: of a man, a horse, a house[hold]").
    - The **organic thesis** (polis prior to individual): a thing that loses its defining capacity becomes a different thing (homonymy principle). Ackrill: "is a newly-made rudder not yet a rudder because not installed in a boat?"
    - Lawgivers are "craftsmen of laws and constitutions"; "he who first framed the political community was a cause of the greatest goods": tension with the polis being natural.
    - The age of laws doesn't prove them good: primitive men were probably "rather unintelligent, being earth-born or survivors of a catastrophe".

**Natural slavery** (the most notorious section)
- Some people are "slaves by nature": able to follow reason but not to deliberate; better off ruled.
    - Natural slaves "do not have, but can apprehend, reason". Smith examines whether the theory is coherent.
- But Aristotle admits many actual slaves are slaves only by **convention** (war captives), which is unjust. Critics: he never shows who the natural slaves are.
- Also: male rule over female, father over children, each a different kind of rule.
    - Man's and woman's *erga* (functions) differ (Adkins).
    - Meno's list of virtues (for men, women, slaves) vs definitions fit only free adult males.
    - The *ergon* is defined by society: Aristotle claims women and slaves have defective souls so their roles are "natural", but "his language betrays him".

**Wealth-getting**
- **Household management** uses property for living well: there's a natural limit.
- **Money-making** for its own sake (*chrematistikē*) has no limit: unnatural. Usury is most unnatural: money breeding money.
- **Exchange value** (Meikle): *EN* V.5: exchange is "reciprocity on the basis of **proportion**, not equality": not two doctors but a doctor and a farmer, unequal.
    - **Money** was introduced because "all things exchanged must be comparable"; **need** (*chreia*) "holds together" the association.
    - **Marx**: "the brilliancy of Aristotle's genius is shown by this alone, that he discovered, in the expression of the value of commodities, a relation of equality."
    - Against Schumpeter: Aristotle isn't doing market economics, but analysing commensurability as such.
- Influential on the Fathers and medieval bans on usury.

## Book II: Critique of Plato's Republic and Others

- Plato wanted the guardians to share wives, children and property, to make the city **one**. Aristotle:
    - *Koinōnia politikē* = "having in common": Aristotle asks how much a city must share (Stalley).
    - too much unity destroys the city: a city is a plurality, not one person
        - A man is more of a unity than a household, a household more than a city: Socrates makes the city "too much of a unity". "It is not in the nature of a city to be one in the way some say, and what is said to be the greatest good for cities actually destroys them."
    - "what is common to the greatest number gets the least care": everyone's child is no one's
        - If everyone calls the same child "mine", "mine" means "belonging to me with many others": friendship becomes "**watery**".
    - **private property, common use**: own privately, share generously. Generosity needs property.
        - The evils Plato wants to remove "come about not because people do not hold property in common, but because they are **vicious**" (Irwin).
        - Irwin's objection: that's no more persuasive than "we need wars because we value bravery" or "we need beggars because we value charity". A reply: there's a distinctive value in **private** generosity.
- Reviews Sparta, Crete, Carthage: all flawed, mostly by aiming at war rather than peace.

## Book III: Citizen and Constitution

- A **citizen**: one who shares in **ruling** (judging and deliberating). The good citizen and the good man aren't always the same: it depends on the regime.
    - Citizenship = "**ruling and being ruled**": the citizen shares in deliberative, executive and judicial office. Citizens were educated for obedience **and** command, the second through the first (Bradley).
    - All Greeks agreed the *agathos* (good man) should rule, deliberate and defend the city (Adkins).
- **Six constitutions**: rule by one, few or many, for the **common good** (correct) or for the rulers' own interest (deviant):

| Rulers | Correct | Deviant |
|---|---|---|
| one | kingship | tyranny |
| few | aristocracy | oligarchy (really: rule of the rich) |
| many | **polity** (*politeia*) | democracy (really: rule of the poor) |

- The three good constitutions in descending order, the three bad in descending badness: "**the corruption of the best (kingship) is the worst (tyranny)**" (Bradley).
- Deviant constitutions are posterior **evaluatively** (they're bad). Plato's *Laws*: the Stranger refuses the name "constitution" to arrangements not for the common good.
- **Justice** is **proportional** equality: equals to equals, unequals to unequals. Democrats think free birth makes all equal; oligarchs think wealth makes people unequal. Both partly right. The real measure is contribution to the **good life**: virtue.
    - **Distributive justice**: honours and rights distributed in proportion to **worth** (*axia*): "equality of ratios", not absolute equality. Hence an "oligarchic right" and a "democratic right", each partly right (Bradley).
    - The formula: worth of Callias / worth of Coriscus = value of parcel a / value of parcel b (Keyt).
    - "All agree the just must be according to worth of some sort, but not the same sort: democrats say freedom, oligarchs wealth or birth, aristocrats virtue" (*EN* V.3).
    - The **flute argument**: the best flutes should go to the best flute-players, not the richest or best-born; likewise office to those with **political virtue**. (In Ethiopia, offices went by height!)
    - "Those who contribute most to such a community have a larger share in the polis."
- **Natural justice** (Miller): "unwritten law" has no single fixed meaning in Greek authors (Ostwald).
    - Natural = what happens "always or for the most part"; so natural justice allows **exceptions** in exceptional circumstances. Some justice is natural even though it varies: "everywhere only one constitution is best according to nature".
    - Syllogism: everything natural is good; everything good (in human conduct) is just; so everything natural is just.
    - The *Rhetoric* uses natural-law arguments persuasively, for audiences without philosophical training.
- **Rule of law** vs rule of the best man: law is "reason without desire", better generally; but law can't foresee every case.
    - Absolute kingship (*pambasileia*) is justified only if one man surpasses all the rest put together in virtue (Keyt).

## Books IV–VI: Actual Regimes

- Books IV–VI are called "empirical": about actual constitutions, not "the constitution of our prayers" (Rowe).
- Two practical aims for political science: (1) a **virtuous** city, or the nearest approximation; (2) **stability** and order. In IV–VI injustice is avoided because it leads to faction (*stasis*): a means, not an end.
- **Polity**, a mixture of oligarchy and democracy, is the best **practicable** regime.
- **The middle class**: the city is best governed when the middle is large: the rich are arrogant, the poor are envious; the middle are the most reasonable and stable. "The mean is best."
- Different kinds of democracy and oligarchy; offices; courts.
    - Each has four sub-types (in one place five), ranked from moderate to extreme: Aristotle wants to **rank**, not just classify (Mulgan).
    - Oligarchs aim at their own enrichment.
    - What defines oligarchy and democracy: **economic class** (rich vs poor) rather than number. But by making freedom democracy's principle, Aristotle admits number matters too: majority rule by the poor mass.

## Book V: Revolution

- The general cause of faction: **inequality** perceived unjustly (the many want equality; the few want superiority).
    - Three groups of causes of faction: the state of mind, the objects sought (gain and honour), and the occasions. **Gain** is both an objective and an occasion (anger at others' unfair gain) (Polansky).
    - Against Plato's "nuptial number" in *Republic* VIII as the cause of decline.
- How each regime falls and how to preserve it: moderation, avoid insolence, keep the middle strong, education in the spirit of the regime.
    - The democratic idea of freedom ("living as one wishes") is unworthy: life guided by the constitution is "not slavery but safety" (V.9) (Irwin).
    - "To live as one likes" is the idea of liberty Aristotle links first with **barbarism** and then with the degraded mob-rule that ruined the free Greek cities (Bradley).
- How tyrants preserve themselves: destroy friendship and trust, keep people poor and busy, spy. A grim, very modern-sounding list.

## Books VII–VIII: The Best Regime

- The happiest life for a city, as for a person, is the life of **virtue**, with enough external goods.
    - An "**inclusive ends**" view of happiness: the good life includes a range of excellent activities (Depew).
- Is the active (political) life or the contemplative life best? Contemplation is activity too: the highest.
    - The lover of contemplation can distinguish genuine from distorted politics, and avoids the apolitical intellectual's mistake of thinking all rule is "a kind of mastery" (VII.3). Contemplation, useless and therefore inherently good, lets one tell what's good in itself from what's merely instrumental (Depew).
- The best city: modest size (all citizens can know each other), territory, citizens with leisure.
    - The ideal size: who could be general of a huge multitude, or herald, "unless he had the voice of Stentor"? (Bradley)
    - Not all "parts" of the state are equal: farmers and craftsmen are necessary conditions, not properly "parts".
- **Leisure** (*scholē*): the point of work is leisure, as of war is peace. Leisure is for the highest activities, not idleness. (The source of Pieper's *Leisure, the Basis of Culture*.)
- **Education** should be public and common, forming character. **Music** is central, not for utility but for noble leisure and the formation of the passions.
    - The fool "whose sense no more can feel but his own wringing" needs education and law to move from life "according to passion" to citizenship (Bradley).

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The *Politics* is the opposite of modern liberalism's starting point: the individual isn't prior to the community; the community exists for **virtue**, not just to protect rights. MacIntyre and the conservatives draw on this. See [[Conservatism: Burke, de Maistre, Scruton]].
- **Aquinas** took over most of it (*De Regno*, the *Summa*'s treatise on law), Christianised: the city's end is virtue, but the human final end is God, which the Church serves.
- **Natural slavery** shows the limit of Aristotle's natural reasoning; Gregory of Nyssa's sermon against slavery (*Homily 4 on Ecclesiastes*) is the Christian answer: man is in God's image; who can buy that?
- The "political animal" fits the Orthodox anthropology of communion. See [[Being as Communion]].
