"Presocratic" is a bit misleading: some were Socrates' contemporaries. What unites them is the question of **nature** (*physis*): what everything is made of and why it changes, asked without appealing to myths about the gods. Everything we have of them is **fragments** quoted by later writers (Aristotle, Simplicius, Hippolytus, Sextus, Diogenes Laertius).

## 1. The Milesians: the Arche

| Thinker | The *arche* (principle) | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Thales** (c. 585 BC) | water | "all things are full of gods"; the magnet has a soul |
| **Anaximander** | the *apeiron* (boundless) | opposites separate out of an unlimited stuff and "pay penalty to one another for their injustice according to the ordering of time". The first surviving sentence of Western philosophy |
| **Anaximenes** | air | condensation and rarefaction produce everything else: the first mechanism |

The question they start is **the one and the many**: what single principle underlies the many things? See [[The Problem of the One and the Many]].

## 2. Pythagoras

Number and harmony as the principle. The soul's immortality and transmigration. See [[Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans]].

## 3. Heraclitus (c. 500 BC): Becoming

- **Flux**: "You cannot step twice into the same river, for other waters are ever flowing on." Everything changes.
- **Fire**: the world "is an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures".
- **Unity of opposites**: "the road up and the road down are one and the same"; "war is the father of all". Tension holds things together, like a bow or lyre.
- **The Logos**: one rational principle governs all change. "Listening not to me but to the Logos, it is wise to agree that all things are one." Most people don't grasp it, though it's common to all.
- Called "the Obscure". Wrote in riddles on purpose.

**The Logos and John 1.** The Stoics took over Heraclitus's Logos as the divine reason in the world. St. Justin Martyr: Heraclitus and Socrates lived "with the Logos" and were in a sense Christians before Christ. John's "In the beginning was the Logos" speaks into this world, but the Logos is now a **person** who becomes flesh.

## 4. Parmenides (c. 475 BC): Being

The poem *On Nature*: a goddess shows the youth two ways.

- **The way of truth**: "**it is**, and it is impossible for it not to be." What-is is; what-is-not is not and cannot even be thought.
- **The way of opinion** (*doxa*): mortals' belief in change, plurality, coming-to-be. Wandering, "two-headed".

**The argument**
1. You can only think and speak of what is.
2. Coming-to-be would mean coming from what-is-not. But nothing comes from nothing.
3. So what-is is **ungenerated, imperishable, one, continuous, unchanging**, like a perfect sphere.
4. Change and plurality are illusions of the senses.

**Melissus**: what-is is also infinite.

**Zeno's paradoxes** defend Parmenides by showing motion and plurality lead to absurdity:
- **Achilles and the tortoise**: Achilles must first reach where the tortoise was, by which time it's moved on... forever.
- **The dichotomy**: to cross a distance you must first cross half, and half of that...
- **The arrow**: at each instant the arrow occupies a space equal to itself, so at each instant it's at rest.

Aristotle's answer: the distance is only **potentially** infinitely divisible, not actually divided. Modern answer: convergent infinite series.

**Why it matters.** Parmenides sets the problem for the whole tradition: how can there be change and many things, if being is one? Plato answers with the Forms (being) and the sensible world (becoming); Aristotle with **act and potency**: change is from potential being, not from nothing. See [[Aristotle's Physics: Nature, Change and the Four Causes]], [[Edward Feser: The Last Superstition]].

## 5. The Pluralists: Replies to Parmenides

Accept that real being doesn't come to be or perish, but make it **many**.

| Thinker | Elements | Moving cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Empedocles** | four roots: earth, water, air, fire | **Love** (combines) and **Strife** (separates), in cycles |
| **Anaxagoras** | infinitely many stuffs; "in everything there is a portion of everything" | **Nous** (Mind), which set the mixture rotating. Socrates was excited by this, then disappointed that Anaxagoras didn't use Mind to explain *why* things are best (*Phaedo* 97–99) |
| **Leucippus, Democritus** (atomists) | **atoms** (uncuttable) and **void** | none: atoms collide by necessity. "By convention sweet, by convention bitter... in reality atoms and void" |

The atomists are the ancestors of materialism. See [[Epicurus and Lucretius]].

## 6. Rational Theology: Xenophanes

- Against Homer and Hesiod: they "attributed to the gods everything that is a shame among men: theft, adultery, deceit".
- "If cattle and horses had hands... horses would draw the forms of gods like horses." Gods made in human image.
- Instead: "**One god**, greatest among gods and men, not at all like mortals in body or thought." He moves all things by thought without moving. A step toward monotheism and toward Aristotle's unmoved mover.

## 7. The Sophists

- **Protagoras**: "Man is the measure of all things." Relativism. Agnostic about the gods.
- **Gorgias**: nothing exists; if it did, it couldn't be known; if known, it couldn't be communicated. A parody of Parmenides.
- **Nature vs convention** (*physis* vs *nomos*): is justice natural or a human invention? Antiphon: convention. Socrates and Plato answer this.

## 8. Notes

*Mine.*

- The two big themes, being (Parmenides) and becoming (Heraclitus), run through everything after. In Christian terms: God as "I AM" (*ho ōn*, Exod 3:14) is being, creation is becoming, and the Logos joins them. See [[The Name of God]].
- Parmenides' "nothing comes from nothing" is the principle Christianity has to answer with **creation ex nihilo**: not from nothing as material, but by God's will. See [[Copan and Craig: Creation out of Nothing]].
- Anaxagoras's Nous and Xenophanes' one god show the Greeks reaching toward a single divine mind before Plato.
