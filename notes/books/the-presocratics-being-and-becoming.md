"Presocratic" is a bit misleading: some were Socrates' contemporaries. What unites them is the question of **nature** (*physis*): what everything is made of and why it changes, asked without appealing to myths about the gods. Everything we have of them is **fragments** quoted by later writers (Aristotle, Simplicius, Hippolytus, Sextus, Diogenes Laertius).

## 1. The Milesians: the Arche

| Thinker | The *arche* (principle) | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Thales** (c. 585 BC) | water | "all things are full of gods"; the magnet has a soul |
| **Anaximander** | the *apeiron* (boundless) | opposites separate out of an unlimited stuff and "pay penalty to one another for their injustice according to the ordering of time". The first surviving sentence of Western philosophy |
| **Anaximenes** | air | condensation and rarefaction produce everything else: the first mechanism |

The question they start is **the one and the many**: what single principle underlies the many things? See [[The Problem of the One and the Many]].

## 2. Pythagoras

Number and harmony as the principle. The soul's immortality and transmigration. See [[Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans]].

## 3. Heraclitus (c. 500 BC): Becoming

- **Flux**: "You cannot step twice into the same river, for other waters are ever flowing on." Everything changes.
- **Fire**: the world "is an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures".
- **Unity of opposites**: "the road up and the road down are one and the same"; "war is the father of all". Tension holds things together, like a bow or lyre.
- **The Logos**: one rational principle governs all change. "Listening not to me but to the Logos, it is wise to agree that all things are one." Most people don't grasp it, though it's common to all.
- Called "the Obscure". Wrote in riddles on purpose.

**The Logos and John 1.** The Stoics took over Heraclitus's Logos as the divine reason in the world. St. Justin Martyr: Heraclitus and Socrates lived "with the Logos" and were in a sense Christians before Christ. John's "In the beginning was the Logos" speaks into this world, but the Logos is now a **person** who becomes flesh.

## 4. Parmenides (c. 475 BC): Being

The poem *On Nature*: a goddess shows the youth two ways.

- **The way of truth**: "**it is**, and it is impossible for it not to be." What-is is; what-is-not is not and cannot even be thought.
- **The way of opinion** (*doxa*): mortals' belief in change, plurality, coming-to-be. Wandering, "two-headed".

**The argument**
1. You can only think and speak of what is.
2. Coming-to-be would mean coming from what-is-not. But nothing comes from nothing.
3. So what-is is **ungenerated, imperishable, one, continuous, unchanging**, like a perfect sphere.
4. Change and plurality are illusions of the senses.

**Melissus**: what-is is also infinite.

**Zeno's paradoxes** defend Parmenides by showing motion and plurality lead to absurdity:
- **Achilles and the tortoise**: Achilles must first reach where the tortoise was, by which time it's moved on... forever.
- **The dichotomy**: to cross a distance you must first cross half, and half of that...
- **The arrow**: at each instant the arrow occupies a space equal to itself, so at each instant it's at rest.

Aristotle's answer: the distance is only **potentially** infinitely divisible, not actually divided. Modern answer: convergent infinite series.

**Why it matters.** Parmenides sets the problem for the whole tradition: how can there be change and many things, if being is one? Plato answers with the Forms (being) and the sensible world (becoming); Aristotle with **act and potency**: change is from potential being, not from nothing. See [[Aristotle's Physics: Nature, Change and the Four Causes]], [[The Last Superstition]].

## 5. The Pluralists: Replies to Parmenides

Accept that real being doesn't come to be or perish, but make it **many**.

| Thinker | Elements | Moving cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Empedocles** | four roots: earth, water, air, fire | **Love** (combines) and **Strife** (separates), in cycles |
| **Anaxagoras** | infinitely many stuffs; "in everything there is a portion of everything" | **Nous** (Mind), which set the mixture rotating. Socrates was excited by this, then disappointed that Anaxagoras didn't use Mind to explain *why* things are best (*Phaedo* 97–99) |
| **Leucippus, Democritus** (atomists) | **atoms** (uncuttable) and **void** | none: atoms collide by necessity. "By convention sweet, by convention bitter... in reality atoms and void" |

The atomists are the ancestors of materialism. See [[Epicurus and Lucretius]].

## 6. Rational Theology: Xenophanes

- Against Homer and Hesiod: they "attributed to the gods everything that is a shame among men: theft, adultery, deceit".
- "If cattle and horses had hands... horses would draw the forms of gods like horses." Gods made in human image.
- Instead: "**One god**, greatest among gods and men, not at all like mortals in body or thought." He moves all things by thought without moving. A step toward monotheism and toward Aristotle's unmoved mover.

## 7. The Sophists

- **Protagoras**: "Man is the measure of all things." Relativism. Agnostic about the gods.
- **Gorgias**: nothing exists; if it did, it couldn't be known; if known, it couldn't be communicated. A parody of Parmenides.
- **Nature vs convention** (*physis* vs *nomos*): is justice natural or a human invention? Antiphon: convention. Socrates and Plato answer this.

## 8. Notes

*Mine.*

- The two big themes, being (Parmenides) and becoming (Heraclitus), run through everything after. In Christian terms: God as "I AM" (*ho ōn*, Exod 3:14) is being, creation is becoming, and the Logos joins them. See [[The Name of God]].
- Parmenides' "nothing comes from nothing" is the principle Christianity has to answer with **creation ex nihilo**: not from nothing as material, but by God's will. See [[Creation out of Nothing]].
- Anaxagoras's Nous and Xenophanes' one god show the Greeks reaching toward a single divine mind before Plato.

## The Cambridge Companion, Chapter by Chapter

**Lives and writings**
- **Thales** (b. c. 624 BC, Miletus): "first inquirer into nature" (Aristotle), one of the Seven Sages.
- **Anaximander**: one of the first Greeks to write a book in **prose**.
- **Anaxagoras**: his book opened "All things were together"; 16 passages survive, nearly all in Simplicius's commentary on the *Physics*.
- **Diogenes of Apollonia**: mocked by Aristophanes in *Clouds* for making divine **air** the only principle.
- **Gorgias** (b. c. 480, Leontini): reputed to live past 100. **Leucippus**: "the first to posit atoms as principles". **Philolaus**: contemporary of Socrates (taught Cebes and Simmias of the *Phaedo*).
- Fragments are cited by **Diels–Kranz** (DK) numbers: "A" = testimonies, "B" = actual quoted words.

**1. The scope of early Greek philosophy (Long)**
- "Presocratic" is a modern label (Diels, following Zeller's great history), misleading: it lumps together contemporaries of Socrates and implies they were just a prelude.
- What they share: "inquiry into nature", accounts of "all things". Heraclitus describes himself as "distinguishing each thing according to its nature" (*physis*).
- Their accounts had "little basis in measurement" or controlled checks: not physics in our sense.

**2. Sources (Mansfeld)**
- Almost everything comes through later writers: Aristotle, **Theophrastus**'s *Physical Opinions* (organised by topic), the "doxographers" (ps.-Plutarch, Aetius), Simplicius, Sextus, Diogenes Laertius.
- Ancient writers sorted philosophers into **successions** (teacher → pupil): an "Ionian" line, an "Italian" line (Pythagoreans), an "**Eleatic**" line from Xenophanes. These are partly constructions.
- Danger: projecting modern philosophical fashions onto fragments.

**3. The beginnings of cosmology (Algra)**
- Before philosophy: Hesiod's gods (Night, Earth, Chaos) behave anthropomorphically; sun, earth and rivers were invoked in oaths.
- Early cosmogonies use a **biological model**: the cosmos is "born", like living things.
- Aristotle read the Milesians through his own "material cause": that out of which things come **and** which persists. He objected that "wood does not make a bed, nor bronze a statue": something else causes the change.
- **Anaximenes**: as breath-soul (*pneuma*) holds us together, so air surrounds and steers the cosmos.
- Not "scientific" in a Baconian (systematic observation) or Popperian sense.

**4. The Pythagorean tradition (Huffman)**
- Later sources make him divine: Iamblichus, "the divine Pythagoras"; Porphyry: "about no one else have greater and more extraordinary things been believed".
- **Heraclitus**: Pythagoras "the chief of swindlers", whose "polymathy" was "an evil trickery". See [[Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans]].
- The **tetraktys** is "the harmony in which the Sirens sing".
- **Philolaus**'s fragments, once doubted, are now the key primary texts: limiters and unlimiteds "fitted together" by **harmonia** and **number**. "The first thing fitted together, the one in the centre of the sphere, is called the **hearth**" (the central fire).

**5. Heraclitus (Hussey)**
- "Much learning does not teach the mind."
- *Logos* in ordinary Greek: "what is said", word, story, account, with rich extra meanings.
- The key: **unity-in-opposites**, unfolding in time: not "the world is everything that is the case" but "everything that is **coming to be** the case".
- A **conservation principle**: a constant amount of "fire-equivalent" through all changes. The fire is alive, intelligent, purposive: "**Thunderbolt steers all things**."
- "The same thing is living and dead, waking and sleeping, young and old; for these changed are those, and those changed are these."
- "A man is called childish (*nēpios*, 'wordless') by a god, as a child by a man."
- "To God all things are fine and good and just; but human beings suppose some things unjust, others just" (B102).

**6. Parmenides and Melissus (Sedley)**
- "Is" for a Greek ear is a nonspecific use of the basic sense, not specially "exists".
- "The choice about these things lies in this: **it is, or it is not**."
- The goddess's first argument: "you could not know what is not, nor speak of it."
- What is is "all alike", no gaps; held by "mighty Necessity in the bonds of a limit".
- **The Way of Seeming**: mortals' world built from two forms, **light** (bright, rare, fiery) and **night** (dark, dense, cold).
- **Melissus**: if there's no void, it's **full**; if full, it can't move. Oddly, he also makes the One **incorporeal**.

**7. Zeno (McKirahan)**
- Plato says Zeno argued against plurality; actually several arguments target **motion**.
- If many, each must be both so small as to have no size and "so large as to be unlimited".
- **Dichotomy**, two readings: you can't **complete** a motion (half, then half the rest...) or you can't even **begin** one (first half the first half...).
- Defining the sum of an infinite series as the limit of partial sums doesn't by itself answer Zeno: "stipulating a definition doesn't make anything possible."
- **The arrow**: "what is moving is always at a now", i.e. at an instant. Aristotle: motion doesn't occur at an instant but over an interval.

**8. Empedocles and Anaxagoras: Responses to Parmenides (Graham)**
- **Empedocles**: as Love asserts itself, detached limbs form and join by chance into monsters, "man-faced ox-kind and ox-headed man-kind": an early survival-of-the-fit idea. Also a religious doctrine of the soul's wanderings, probably Pythagorean.
- **Anaxagoras**: "Together were all things, boundless in multitude and in smallness."
- Graham argues Parmenides wasn't a strict monist: he gave a "manual" for a responsible natural philosophy, which the pluralists followed. Their changeless ultimate stuffs move toward distinguishing agent/patient, mind/matter, soul/body.

**9. The atomists (Taylor)**
- Atoms can't split, by the **Principle of Sufficient Reason**: no reason to split here rather than there.
- The void is infinite ("the infinite").
- Leucippus: "**Nothing happens at random, but everything from reason (logos) and by necessity**": PSR, not purpose.
- Democritus's epistemology: "by convention sweet... in reality atoms and void"; at times seems to deny all knowledge ("in truth we know nothing"). Ethics (good as "cheerfulness") wasn't derived from the physics.

**10. Rational theology (Broadie)**
- Anaxagoras's **Mind** is "mixed with nothing, alone by itself": not because divinity requires separateness but because only separate can it **divide** things.
- Critical theology began as a search for "piously appropriate discourse" about the divine.
- Xenophanes' god is "not at all like mortals in body or thought", yet not necessarily incorporeal.
- Heraclitus rebuked Homer for wishing conflict away: "there would be no attunement without contrariety".
- Parmenides never calls his Being divine. Empedocles' **Hate** is "cursed", "evil": unlike Heraclitus's creative War, "father of all".

**11. Early interest in knowledge (Lesher)**
- **Poetic pessimism**: the archaic poets first reflected on the limits of human knowledge; the philosophers became optimistic.
- Heraclitus: "One thing, the only wise thing, is willing and unwilling to be called by the name of Zeus."
- Parmenides distinguishes "true trust" (the way of truth) from "likelihood" (the way of seeming): two kinds of knowledge.
- The "**what it is**" of a thing becomes fundamental to classical theories of knowledge.

**12. Soul, sensation, and thought (Laks)**
- *Psychē* in the early thinkers mostly means **life**.
- Heraclitus: "for souls it is pleasure or death to become moist"; the best soul is dry.
- Early theories of the senses are about **travel** and **contact** (effluences, pores): sight and hearing were the hardest to explain.
- Anaxagoras: all sensation is accompanied by **pain**.

**13. Culpability, responsibility, cause (Vegetti)**
- *Aitia* ("cause") originally meant **blame, responsibility** (legal sense); *prophasis* = pretext, occasion. Herodotus: "Because it was destined that things would go badly for him, this was the occasion."
- The Hippocratic doctors (*On the Sacred Disease*: epilepsy isn't sacred but has natural causes) developed the medical notion of cause.
- Draco's law distinguished voluntary from involuntary homicide.

**14. Rhetoric and relativism: Protagoras and Gorgias (Woodruff)**
- The first teachers of rhetoric: **Corax and Tisias** in Sicily. **Gorgias** "took Athens by storm" in 427 BC; surviving speeches: *Encomium of Helen*, *Defence of Palamedes*.
- Protagoras's "man the measure" probably claims **equality in truth**. Plato's "secret teaching" interpretation is Plato's own.
- Problem: how can Protagoras **teach** if relativism means he knows no more than his pupils?
- Gorgias teaches rhetoric "in total abstraction from any subject matter": separating teaching from knowledge.

**15. Protagoras and Antiphon: debates on justice (Decleva Caizzi)**
- Justice as "rendering to each what is due" (Simonides, in *Republic* I).
- Justice as a **compact** for mutual protection: "a second-best advantage" (cf. Glaucon in *Republic* II: anyone who could do injustice with impunity and didn't would be thought mad).
- Aristoxenus claimed "virtually all of the *Republic*" was in Protagoras's *Antilogika*.
- **Antiphon**: the demands of **nature** (*physis*) vs the weak sanction of **convention** (*nomos*): what counts is utility. Thucydides: "War is a stern teacher."

**16. The poetics of early Greek philosophy (Most)**
- "From the beginning all have learned according to Homer" (Xenophanes); "Hesiod is the teacher of most men" (Heraclitus): yet these two criticise Homer and Hesiod most harshly.
- Five goals the epic poets claimed, which the philosophers inherited: above all **truthfulness**.
- Why did Parmenides and Empedocles write in **verse**? To claim a divine source of truth beyond human delusion.
- Heraclitus's pun: *biós* = "bow", *bíos* = "life": "the name of the bow is life, its work is death".
- Aristotle (*Poetics*): Empedocles is a natural philosopher, not a poet, despite the verse.
