The basic idea: we reason. We draw conclusions *because* they follow from premises. If the world is at bottom only matter in motion, it's hard to see how that can happen. So reasoning points beyond materialism. If materialism is reached by an argument, it undercuts itself.

Reppert's way of putting it: "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1). On theism reason is on the **ground floor** of reality. On materialism, in the beginning the word was *not*: reason is a late by-product.

## 1. Two Kinds of Worldview

| Mentalistic | Materialistic |
|---|---|
| mind is fundamental; matter is created by or flows from mind | matter is fundamental; mind is a late product |
| top-down | bottom-up |
| Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, Christian theism, Hindu pantheism, Spinoza, idealism | Democritus, modern naturalism |

## 2. What Any Materialism Needs

Reppert's "minimal materialism". Not a straw man: most naturalists would sign up to all three.

1. **A mechanistic base level.** "Basic physics" is free of:
    - **purpose** (particles don't aim at anything)
    - **meaning** / intentionality (they aren't *about* anything)
    - **normativity** (rocks in an avalanche don't go where they ought to)
    - **subjectivity** (no point of view)

    Not necessarily deterministic: there can be chance.
2. **Causal closure.** Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. Nothing non-physical makes a difference.
3. **Supervenience.** Everything else (minds, morals, meanings) is fixed by the physical. Same physical facts, same everything.

The question: can **rational inference** fit inside these three?

## 3. How Strong Should the Argument Be?

- Not a **decisive** argument (one every rational person must accept). Hardly any philosophical argument is.
- Reppert uses a **Bayesian** model: does the fact of reason make theism *more likely* than it was before? If so, it counts as evidence, even if other things count the other way.
- Avoid "tunnel vision": weigh it with everything else, including evil.
- His comparison: the problem of reason is as serious for naturalism as the problem of evil is for theism, maybe more. Theists have worked hard on evil; naturalists have hardly started on reason.

## 4. History: Lewis and Anscombe

**Earlier versions**: Kant, Balfour, Pratt (1922), Haldane. J. B. S. Haldane: "if my mental processes are determined wholly by the motions of atoms in my brain, I have no reason to suppose that my beliefs are true."

**C. S. Lewis, *Miracles* (1947), ch. 3**: "The Self-Contradiction of the Naturalist".
- Two senses of "because": **cause and effect** ("he cried out *because* it hurt") and **ground and consequent** ("it must be wet *because* it's raining").
- If naturalism is true, every belief has a full explanation in non-rational causes. Then no belief is held because of its *grounds*.
- So no belief, including naturalism, would be rationally held.

**Elizabeth Anscombe** (Socratic Club, Oxford, 1948) objected:
- "valid" applies to arguments, not to causes; Lewis mixed up "irrational" and "non-rational" causes.
- the same belief can have a causal explanation *and* be held for good reasons; two kinds of explanation don't compete.
- Lewis hadn't made clear what an "act of knowing solely determined by what is known" would be.

**Lewis revised the chapter** (1960 edition, retitled "The Cardinal Difficulty of Naturalism"). The legend says Lewis was "defeated" and gave up apologetics. Anscombe herself later said the revised version was much better and that the question was still open: even granting there are grounds, what do they have to do with the actual *occurrence* of a belief? "We haven't got an answer."

**Reppert's repair**: drop "full explanation"; use the three marks in sec. 2. Even non-reductive materialism allows only **physical causation**. So the question is whether the mental explanations needed for inference are allowed by causal closure. If not, we have to choose between inference and materialism. And since materialism is offered as the conclusion of an inference, we have to give it up.

## 5. The Transcendental Structure

What must be true for **science** to be possible? Reppert lists conditions, which are also conditions of arguing *for* materialism:

1. there are states of mind with **propositional content** (beliefs about things)
2. those contents are **true or false**
3. the **laws of logic** are true and we can know them
4. the logical relations between beliefs play a **causal** role in forming new beliefs
5. the **same person** grasps premise and conclusion
6. our reasoning faculties are generally **reliable**

Each is a separate argument. If materialism can't make room for one of them, it undercuts its own support. Same move as in [[TAG]] and [[It Is Impossible to Have a Coherent Worldview Without Objective Truths]].

## 6. The Sub-Arguments

**From intentionality**
- Thoughts are *about* things. Physical states aren't intrinsically about anything (a pattern of ink or neurons means something only to an interpreter).
- On materialism, aboutness has to be derived or "naturalised" (causal or teleological theories). Reppert: these give at most indeterminate content (Quine's "gavagai", Kripke's Wittgenstein on rules). But reasoning needs **determinate** content: I mean *this*, not something nearly like it.
- So if content is indeterminate, there is no fact about what I'm inferring.

**From mental causation**
- For me to *infer*, my believing the premises must cause my believing the conclusion, *because of* their logical relation.
- But by causal closure, the brain state that realises the conclusion is fully caused by prior physical states, whatever their contents. The content is causally idle (epiphenomenal).
- **Hasker's counterfactual test**: if the premises had had different content but the same physical realisation, would I still have reached the same conclusion? On materialism, yes. Then the content made no difference.
- Kim's **exclusion problem** says the same thing from the other side: in a closed physical world mental properties seem to be excluded from causing anything.

**From the psychological relevance of logical laws**
- The laws of logic are true in all possible worlds, and timeless. In inference they must actually *guide* thought.
- How can a necessary, abstract truth make a difference to the movements of particles? On materialism, the brain does what physics says; logic is at best an outside description of it. See [[Philosophy of Logic: What Grounds the Laws of Logic]].

**From the unity of consciousness**
- An inference needs **one** subject who holds premises and conclusion together. A bundle of neural events, each in its own place, doesn't add up to someone who *sees* that the conclusion follows. See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]].

## 7. Evolution: Lewis and Plantinga

- **Lewis**: if our reasoning arose by natural selection, it was selected for useful **responses**, not for insight into truth. Evolution could make us behave well without making us infer well.
- **Patricia Churchland**: "the principal chore of nervous systems is to get the body parts where they should be in order that the organism may survive... Truth, whatever that is, definitely takes the hindmost."
- **Plantinga's Evolutionary Argument Against Naturalism (EAAN)**:
    1. Let R = our cognitive faculties are reliable; N = naturalism; E = we evolved by unguided natural selection.
    2. P(R | N & E) is low or inscrutable: selection cares about behaviour, and false beliefs can produce adaptive behaviour just as well.
    3. Anyone who believes N & E and sees (2) has a **defeater** for R.
    4. A defeater for R is a defeater for every belief, including N & E.
    5. So N & E is self-defeating: it can't be rationally believed.
- Darwin himself had "the horrid doubt": "whether the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy."
- This is **not** an argument against evolution, only against evolution *plus* naturalism. On theism, God could guide or use the process to produce reliable minds.

## 8. Objections

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| **Anscombe**: causal and rational explanations don't compete | they do, given causal closure: only physical causes are left, and the content of a thought adds nothing |
| **Computers reason, and they're physical** | computers manipulate symbols according to rules *we* gave them, and whose meaning *we* supply (Searle's Chinese Room). Derived intentionality, not original |
| **Evolution selects for truth: true beliefs help survival** | only behaviour is selected; true and false belief-desire pairs can give the same behaviour. And abstract reasoning (set theory) had no survival value |
| **"God of the gaps"** | the argument isn't from what science hasn't explained *yet*, but from what the materialist framework *can't* explain in principle (Larmer: not all gap-arguments are bad) |
| **Inadequacy objection**: "God did it" explains nothing; better to say "I don't know" | theism *does* explain: if mind is basic, reason doesn't have to be generated out of non-reason. It's on the ground floor |
| **Dualism has its own interaction problem** | that's a problem about *how*, not *whether*; the materialist problem is whether reason exists at all |
| **Supervenience solves it** | supervenience just states the problem: the rational supervening on the non-rational, without saying how normativity arises |

## 9. Notes

*Mine.*

- Same family as [[Critique of Empiricism]] and the TAG material: arguments about the preconditions of thought, not about the existence of an external object.
- In Orthodox terms reason (*logos*) in man is a participation in the divine Logos: John 1:9, "the true Light, which lighteth every man". The Fathers call the *logoi* of creatures the Word's thoughts in things. That's why the world is intelligible and the mind can know it. See [[Nous]].
- The weakest premise is probably the claim that naturalised intentionality must be indeterminate. The strongest is the mental causation one: it's the same as Kim's exclusion problem, which naturalists themselves admit is hard.
