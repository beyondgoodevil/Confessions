"Conservatism" as a self-conscious philosophy starts as a **reaction to the French Revolution**: the attempt to remake society from scratch on the basis of abstract reason and the rights of man. The three thinkers here represent three versions: Burke's prudential and Whig; de Maistre's theological and reactionary; Scruton's cultural and modern.

## 1. Edmund Burke (1729–1797)

*Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), written as a letter, before the Terror, which he more or less predicted.

- Burke was answering **Richard Price**'s sermon to the Revolution Society at the Old Jewry (Nov 1789), which celebrated 1688 as establishing the right "to choose our own governors, cashier them for misconduct, and frame a government for ourselves". Unitarians had become the leading radical Dissenters, linking political radicalism with religious heterodoxy (Turner's introduction to the Yale edition).
- The predictions (O'Brien, Yale edition): the radicals would destroy the moderates, and a military dictator would arise.
- Almost 90% of the *Reflections* is argument and analysis, not just passion (O'Brien).
- He argued the **Enlightenment** caused the Revolution; later historians refined this (Turner).
    - Burke never used "the Enlightenment"; he spoke ironically of "this enlightened age" (McMahon, Yale edition: Burke and the "literary cabal").
    - The philosophes "were possessed with a spirit of proselytism in the most fanatical degree, and from thence... with the spirit of persecution". The Bavarian **Illuminati** conspiracy.
    - McMahon: "There is a good deal of light in Burke's critique of the Enlightenment."

**Against abstract rights**
- The revolutionaries reason from **"the rights of man"**, an abstract theory, and deduce a new constitution. But society is too complex for reason to design. "The nature of man is intricate; the objects of society are of the greatest possible complexity."
    - Burke opposed "**visionary politicians**" who reasoned from first principles rather than history and circumstances (Turner).
    - On deliberation: "call in the aid of the farmer and the physician, rather than the professor of metaphysics".
    - The French remade France on three "geometrical, arithmetical and financial" bases (territory, population, contribution), "like their ornamental gardeners, forming everything into an exact level".
    - Wolfe (Yale edition, Burke and social science): human beings have a "**second nature**" (culture); they aren't like the unvarying objects of natural science. Burke anticipates the critique of rational choice theory and of E. O. Wilson's "consilience".
- Real rights are the **inherited rights of Englishmen**, worked out over centuries (Magna Carta, 1688), not metaphysical ones.
    - On the king: the law "calls this high magistrate not our servant... but 'our sovereign Lord the king'".
- **Prophet against the tyranny of theory** (O'Brien): the line from Rousseau's "guide" of the General Will to **Robespierre**, and on to Marx and 20th-century totalitarianism.
    - Weber: beware the "**ethic of ultimate ends**", efforts to create the Kingdom of God on earth now (Wolfe).

**Prejudice and prescription**
- **"Prejudice"** (pre-judgement) isn't irrational: it's the **stored wisdom of generations**, "the general bank and capital of nations and of ages". Better to trust it than each person's "private stock of reason", which is small.
- **Prescription**: long possession gives a title. Institutions that have lasted have proved themselves.
    - But he didn't want "to confine power, authority, and distinction to blood and names and titles": "virtue and wisdom" qualify for government, "but virtue is never tried but by some difficulty and some struggle" (Turner).
    - Rakove (Yale edition, why American constitutionalism worked): Americans treat a constitution as fundamental law adopted at one moment, the opposite of Burke's evolving constitution. Madison would have rejected Burke's defence of unequal representation; the founders still built in protections for minorities (the Senate, the electoral college).

**Society as a partnership across generations**
> "Society is indeed a contract... a partnership in all science; a partnership in all art; a partnership in every virtue... a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born."

- Scruton: when law rests only on contract, obedience is just the other side of free choice; the French Revolution moved quickly "to the moral vacuum that arises when a society is based entirely on claims against it rather than duties towards it".

**The little platoons**
- "To be attached to the subdivision, to love the **little platoon** we belong to in society, is the first principle... of public affections." Love of family, parish, locality comes before love of humanity.
    - Scruton: for Burke, **civil society**, not the state, is the true source of order and authority; the little platoons are where traditions form.

**Reform, not revolution**
- "A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation." Burke supported the American colonists, Catholic emancipation in Ireland, and the impeachment of Warren Hastings for abuses in India. Conservatism isn't opposing all change but changing **gradually**, keeping continuity.
    - Burke, an Irishman with a Catholic mother, defended 1688 while supporting Catholic relief; he lost his Bristol seat (1780) partly for it (O'Brien).
    - The French monarchy, "though by no means a free and therefore by no means a good constitution", was "a despotism rather in appearance than in reality", tempered by religion, laws, manners.

**Religion**
- "Man is by his constitution a **religious animal**." The established Church consecrates the state; atheism undermines it.
    - On toleration: the English "tolerate, not because they despise opinions, but because they respect justice".
- The famous lament for Marie Antoinette: "the age of chivalry is gone... that of sophisters, economists, and calculators has succeeded."
    - Paris "smitten with the cold, dry, petrific mace of a false and unfeeling philosophy".

## 2. Joseph de Maistre (1753–1821)

A Savoyard Catholic, exiled by the Revolution, ambassador in St Petersburg. Much darker than Burke.

**Providence**: *Considerations on France* (1797)
- The Revolution isn't something men did; men were its **instruments**. It's a divine **punishment** on France for the Enlightenment's impiety, and its horrors will purify.
- "The Revolution leads men more than men lead it."

**Against written constitutions**
- "A constitution is not something men make." Real constitutions grow, are mostly **unwritten**, and have a divine origin. What is written is weakest. A constitution made for "man" in general is made for no one: "I have seen Frenchmen, Italians, Russians... but as for **man**, I declare I have never met him."

**Sacrifice and the executioner** (*St Petersburg Dialogues*)
- The world is bound together by **sacrifice** and **suffering**: the innocent suffer for the guilty (reversibility), as Christ did.
- The **executioner** is the cornerstone of social order: "take this incomprehensible agent from the world, and in an instant order gives way to chaos." Shocking on purpose.

**Authority**
- Society needs **sovereignty**, and sovereignty needs **infallibility** in its sphere. In religion, the **pope** (*Du Pape*, 1819): a key text of ultramontanism behind Vatican I. See [[Vatican I's View of Papal Infallibility]].
    - Ultramontanism remained a force in French conservatism into the 20th-century Catholic revival (Scruton).

## 3. Roger Scruton (1944–2020)

*Conservatism: An Invitation to the Great Tradition* (history) and *How to Be a Conservative* (a personal statement).

**The history**: from Aristotle and Hooker through Hume, Burke, Hegel, Coleridge, Disraeli to the post-war conservatives (Oakeshott, Hayek, Kirk). Conservatism is first a defence of **liberty** under law, then (19th c.) a defence of **culture** against industrialism, then (20th c.) of the free market against socialism.
- **Before conservatism**: political thinking starts from features of the human condition that limit it: family loyalty creates rivalry between families and tribes (Montagues and Capulets).
- **Liberty under law**:
    - **James Harrington**: "an empire of laws, not of men"; written constitution, bicameral government.
    - **Locke**: natural rights exist even in a state of nature; legitimate government rests on **contract**.
    - Jefferson's "checks and balances".
    - **Hume**: consent responds to a belief in legitimacy rather than founding it.
    - **Adam Smith**: the "**impartial spectator**" we consult in imagination to judge ourselves.
    - The **American** Constitution was conservative in intent: to secure what the colonists had once enjoyed before the Crown taxed without authority.
- **Germany and France**:
    - **Kant**: morality from "autonomy of the will". **Hegel** saw this as "abstract right": rights and duties defined by reason alone, without history.
    - Hegel: true freedom includes recognising **obligations not freely chosen** ("obligations of piety"). **Civil society** is the sphere of free association and institutions that arise by "the cunning of reason" (an invisible hand). The distinction between **state** and civil society is essential.
    - **Tocqueville** warned of "**democratic despotism**": majority sentiment overriding minorities.
- **Culture against industrialism**:
    - **Coleridge**: against Bentham's "felicific calculus"; drew on German idealism. The state is "the representative acting-power of the nation", for both freedom and order.
    - **Ruskin, Arnold**: the machine age degraded work and leisure into means, and so art.
    - **T. S. Eliot**: *The Criterion*; the battle is fought in the realm of high culture.
    - Romantic nationalism in Germany; Thoreau's heroic independence at Walden, which repudiates Jefferson's rural settlements.
- **Against socialism**:
    - Conservatism began as a "**yes, but...**" to classical liberalism's "yes" to popular sovereignty.
    - **Oakeshott**: against "**rationalism**" in politics (seeing it in means–end terms with a clearly formulated goal) and against "ideology".
    - American conservatives against the New Deal and against "**moral equivalence**" between American democracy and Soviet communism.
    - When Scruton was young, conservatism was defined by the worldwide confrontation between **freedom and totalitarianism**.
- **Now**:
    - Conservatives at a huge disadvantage in the intellectual world; "culture wars" became central.
    - **Maitland**'s constitutional history undermines Marxist economic determinism.
    - **Ayn Rand**'s "objectivism"; neo-liberalism (Chicago school, public choice theory).

**Main ideas**
- **"Conservatism starts from a sentiment that all mature people can readily share: the sentiment that good things are easily destroyed, but not easily created."**
- ***Oikophilia***: love of **home**: the settled place, its landscape, customs and people. Against ***oikophobia***, the elite's repudiation of its own inheritance. Grounds environmentalism, rightly understood (*Green Philosophy*).
- The **first-person plural**: a "we" of membership comes before politics; the nation is the form of membership that makes democracy possible.
    - "The language of politics is spoken in the **first-person plural**, and the duty of the politician is to maintain that first-person plural in being." A pre-political loyalty makes neighbours who voted differently treat each other as fellow citizens: the government is "ours", not "mine" or "yours".
    - Isaiah Berlin's negative/positive liberty misses the idea of a **free community**: real, socially formed constraints, tacitly accepted as part of citizenship.
    - Our identity is defined "not by our religion, our tribe or our race but by our **country**, the sovereign territory in which we have built the free form of life we share".
    - Immigration and **Islam**: Huntington's "American Creed" from Anglo-Protestant culture; **Pierre Manent**: we can't expect Muslim citizens to embrace the French revolutionary doctrine of human rights; offer instead a shared object of loyalty (the nation).
- **The sacred**: Scruton's late work: beauty, the sacred and the person as what modern culture can't explain and can't do without. *The Face of God*, *Beauty*.
- **Markets**: the free market as a store of social knowledge (Hayek) but needing to be limited by institutions, family, the sacred.
    - Adam Smith: prices from free exchange as information for trade. **Hayek**: interfering with the market destroys the information needed for rational economic decisions.
- What conservatives should be "for": the nation, the rule of law, liberty, the family, beauty, the sacred, and the "little platoons".

## 4. Comparing Them

| | Burke | de Maistre | Scruton |
|---|---|---|---|
| basis | prudence, experience, prescription | providence, sacrifice, authority | inheritance, home, the sacred |
| religion | establishment, social usefulness and truth | everything; theocratic | cultural and personal; Anglican |
| attitude to change | gradual reform | providential judgement | protective, gradual |
| key image | the little platoons | the executioner | the home (*oikos*) |

## 5. Notes

*Mine.*

- Conservatism's **anthropology** is the classical one: man is a political and religious animal, formed by community and tradition, not a free-floating chooser. See [[Aristotle's Politics]].
- Burke's "prejudice" and "prescription" are close to how the Orthodox understand **Tradition**: the accumulated life of the community, trusted over individual reasoning. But for the Church, Tradition is guaranteed by the Holy Spirit, not just by having lasted. See [[Sola Scriptura: The Main Arguments and Replies]], [[The Holy Spirit in Orthodox Theology]].
- Augustine's caution is a corrective to all of them: no earthly city is the Kingdom; the conservative can idolise the nation as much as the revolutionary idolises the future. See [[Augustine's City of God]].
- De Maistre is the one most opposed to the Orthodox in ecclesiology (papal infallibility), yet he spent fifteen years in Orthodox Russia.
