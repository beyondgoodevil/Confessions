Jonathan King's book isn't about art or nature. It is a **doctrine of God**: beauty belongs to God himself, and it is seen above all in how **fitting** God's plan is, centred on the **Son as incarnate Redeemer**. His motto is that everything God does is "perfectly fitting—and hence beautiful." Here's how the argument goes:

### 1. What Beauty Is: Fittingness

King starts from the question Socrates put to Hippias, "could you tell me what the beautiful is?" His definition, "stemming out of the classical tradition":

> "Beauty is an intrinsic quality of things which, when perceived, pleases the mind by displaying a certain kind of fittingness." (Introduction)

- **Fittingness** is his umbrella word for all the objective marks of beauty: "proportion, unity, variety, symmetry, harmony, intricacy, delicacy, simplicity, or suggestiveness." Ugliness is "ill-fittingness," a deformity of form. He borrows Wolterstorff's idea that grace, vitality and delicacy are kinds of **fittingness-intensity**.
- **Realism.** Beauty exists whether or not anyone sees it, though perceiving it always brings delight. He takes Augustine's side: things "give pleasure because they are beautiful," not the other way round. Gilson: the pleasure "does not constitute beauty itself, but it betrays its presence." So beauty is **objectively real, subjectively experienced**.
- **Beyond aesthetics narrowly.** Beauty can be conspicuous (a painting, a melody) or subtle: the "propriety" and order of a well-made story or a well-ordered plan. This broad sense is what lets him speak of the beauty of **salvation history**.

### 2. Beauty Among the Transcendentals

Following the medieval tradition, truth, goodness and beauty are "transcendentals": properties convertible with being itself, distinct in concept but identical in reality.

| | Relation of being to | We approach it by |
|---|---|---|
| **Truth** | the mind (being as knowable) | knowledge |
| **Goodness** | the will (being as desirable) | desire |
| **Beauty** | mind and will together (being as "delightable") | delight |

- **The classical formula:** in the true the intellect rests, in the good the will rests, in the beautiful **both rest together**. Beauty is not wanted as a means to something else; its delight is the end.
- **So God is beautiful.** Since the transcendentals are perfections that exist most perfectly in God, beauty is a **divine attribute**. King adds that God's truth, goodness and beauty correspond to his **knowledge, will and beatitude** (his own delight).

### 3. Four Witnesses

1. **Anselm.** Fittingness is central to *Cur Deus Homo*: "it was fitting that the devil, who through the tasting of a tree defeated the human being ... should be defeated by a human being through the suffering on a tree," and many such correspondences "demonstrate the indescribable beauty that belongs to our redemption" (1.3).
2. **Aquinas.** Three criteria of beauty: **proportion** (consonance, harmony), **integrity** (wholeness: lacking nothing a thing's form requires) and **clarity** (the splendour each form gives off). The creature's beauty "is nothing else than the likeness of the divine beauty participated in things." Following Armand Maurer: God's nature is pure unity, but "He is three in Persons. In His personal life there is harmony and proportion and consequently beauty."
3. **Bavinck.** God is "the apex of unchanging beauty," but because the language sounds Neoplatonic he prefers to speak of God's **majesty and glory**. For him beauty and glory are the same thing.
4. **Barth.** Beauty isn't one of the attributes that are the divine essence. It is an explanatory concept for **glory**: God "radiates joy, so that He is all He is with and not without beauty. Otherwise His glory might well be joyless." The secrets of God's beauty are the **Trinity** and the **Incarnation**.

- **Scripture's own link** is beauty with **glory**, majesty and kingship: "to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD" (Ps 27:4), "splendor and majesty are before him; strength and beauty are in his sanctuary" (Ps 96:6), Isaiah's and Ezekiel's visions of the enthroned Lord.

### 4. The Thesis: The Son Is the Lens

> "The Son's fittingness as incarnate Redeemer, and in the divine economy in general, is the critical lens for seeing God's beauty." (Introduction)

Because God is simple (his attributes are not parts), **everything God does is beautiful**: it is God-glorifying by definition. King finds the plan's beauty in its **symmetry**:

1. Creation is through the Son, **and** re-creation is through the Son.
2. The Son is the image through whom humanity was first imaged, **and** as the last Adam he re-images the redeemed.
3. The only-begotten Son's relation to the Father is the pattern for the **adopted sonship** he wins for us.

All divine action is fittingly "from the Father, through the Son, and in the Holy Spirit."

### 5. The Drama in Four Acts

1. **Creation: Beauty's Debut (featured: Irenaeus).** Scripture's overall shape is a "sublime comedy": it moves from a good beginning through catastrophe to a happier end. Genesis 1–2 is a **"proto-eschaton"** whose themes point forward to the end. The image of God has three aspects: **official** (royal priest), **constitutional** (body and soul) and **ethical-relational**. Christ **recapitulates** Adam's and Israel's history (Irenaeus).
2. **The Incarnation: Beauty Condescending (featured: Balthasar).** For Balthasar, "Beauty is the word that shall be our first." Christ is the **central form** of revelation, whose light "breaks forth from the form's interior," and only the "eyes of faith" see it. King reads Philippians 2 (the form of a slave) and the **Transfiguration** this way.
   - **Isaiah 53:2:** "he had no form or majesty that we should look at him, and no beauty that we should desire him." (The Septuagint has ***kallos***.) The Servant's ugliness is not a contradiction of God's beauty but its **fittingness** for the Messiah's work of self-giving love. Christ's "true intrinsic beauty is hidden from people because they look at him entirely from a human standpoint." With Balthasar, this is "the glory of God's poor love."
3. **The Cross: Beauty Redeeming.** Hebrews uses the very word: "For it was fitting (*prepō*) that he, for whom and by whom all things exist, in bringing many sons to glory, should make the founder of their salvation perfect through suffering" (Heb 2:10; cf. 7:26). The cross fits both God's creative purpose (Psalm 8's glory of man restored) and Christ's priesthood. King argues Christ's **kingly glory** is epitomized in his death.
4. **Re-creation: Beauty's Denouement (featured: Jonathan Edwards).** For Edwards, "God is God ... chiefly by his divine beauty," and beauty is **"being's consent to being."** One alone cannot be excellent, since there could be no consent, so God's beauty is the Trinity: the mutual love of Father and Son, whose bond is the Spirit, "his infinite beauty." The Church is being "adorned" as the Bride, and the end of creation will be "incomparably ... more glorious than its protological beginning." (King also argues, following Anselm, that even the justice done to the damned is "fitting".)

### 6. Assessment

- **What's strong.** Grounding beauty in the **Trinity** and the **Incarnation** rather than in taste is right, and the notion of **fittingness** is genuinely useful. It explains why Isaiah 53 and the cross can be called beautiful without making beauty mean prettiness. It also joins the existing note's point about the beauty of the cross (see [[Beauty as a Way to God]]) to a precise theory.
- **Where the Orthodox would differ.**
  1. **Glory as energy.** King, with Barth and Bavinck, identifies beauty with God's *glory*. Orthodoxy agrees, but understands the glory seen on Tabor as God's **uncreated energy**, really shared with the saints. Beauty, then, isn't only something we contemplate in the plan of salvation; it is a light we are given to **become** (see [[Saint Gregory of Nyssa: Essence Energies]] and [[2 Peter 1:3-4: Theosis]]).
  2. **The Fathers on beauty.** King's medieval witnesses are Anselm and Aquinas. Dionysius (God as Beauty because he "calls" all things) and the *Philokalia* are mostly offstage, and they would make the account less forensic and more ascetic.
  3. **Retributive "fittingness."** The idea that hell is in some way beautiful (Anselm, Edwards) sits uneasily with the Eastern emphasis on healing over satisfaction.
  4. **Icons.** A dogmatics of beauty centred on the Incarnation leads naturally to the icon, which King doesn't discuss: if the Son's human form reveals the Father's glory, that form can be depicted (see [[Theology of the Icon]]).

### Conclusion

King defines beauty as an objective quality that pleases the mind by displaying **fittingness**, and argues, with Anselm, Aquinas, Bavinck and Barth, that beauty is a perfection of God, bound up with his glory and his own delight. His central claim is that the **Son's fittingness as incarnate Redeemer** is the lens for seeing that beauty. The symmetry of creation and new creation through the Son, the Servant with "no beauty that we should desire him," the cross that "was fitting" for the founder of our salvation, and the Bride adorned at the end all show a plan whose beauty is not prettiness but perfect appropriateness to God's self-giving love. The Orthodox would add that this glory is not only displayed but shared.
