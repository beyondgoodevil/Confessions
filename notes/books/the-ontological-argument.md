**Ontological arguments** try to prove God exists from general principles plus what God *is*, with no premise about the world. Maydole's chapter reconstructs six historical versions in symbolic logic and adds two of his own. Most of it is formal derivations I didn't work through. I read the prose around them. Background needed: [[Modal Logic: Necessity and Possibility]].

## 1. How Maydole Tests an Argument

Four tests, applied to each version:

1. **Valid?**
2. **Premises true?**
3. **Question-begging?**
4. **Can it be parodied?**

On parodies. A parody = same form, absurd conclusion. It refutes the original in one of two ways:

- same form + true premises + false conclusion → the form is **invalid**
- both valid, and the parody's premises are *at least as justifiable* → the original must have an unjustified premise

So to beat a parody you show it's invalid, or that one of its premises is worse than yours.

## 2. Anselm

*Proslogion* 2 (c. 1078). God = "that than which nothing greater can be conceived".

1. Even the fool (Ps 14:1) understands the phrase. So this being exists **in the understanding**.
2. Suppose it exists *only* in the understanding.
3. Then it can be conceived to exist in reality too, which is greater.
4. So something greater than "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" can be conceived. Contradiction.
5. So it exists in reality.

Maydole's notes:

- "Greater" = objectively better, more worthy. Assumes a **great chain of being**: a horse is better than a tree, a man than a horse. Anselm argues for a top.
- Strictly the *concept* is in the understanding, not the being. He tidies that.
- Needs quantifying over **possible** things that may not exist.
- With those repairs his reconstruction comes out valid.

**Gaunilo's island.** A monk, same century: by the same reasoning, the greatest conceivable island exists.

- Maydole's reply: whichever way you formalise it, either a premise is false (for any island a greater is conceivable: there's no maximum number of palm trees) or the parody is invalid.
- Anselm's own reply was the same idea: the argument only works for what can't be thought not to exist.

## 3. Descartes and Leibniz

*Meditation V*:

1. God = a supremely perfect being.
2. Existence (really: **necessary** existence) is a perfection.
3. So God has it, as surely as a triangle has angles summing to two right angles.

- Maydole: Descartes can't mean bare existence is in the concept, or he begs the question. What's in the concept is "exists necessarily *if at all*".
- **Leibniz's addition**: the argument only shows God exists *if God is possible*. A "greatest number" sounds fine and is contradictory. So first prove **all perfections are compatible**. His proof: perfections are simple and positive, so they can't conflict.

**Kant's objection**: "existence is not a real predicate".

- A hundred real thalers contain no more *in concept* than a hundred possible ones. Saying a thing exists adds nothing to what it is.
- Maydole: Kant is "half right". Existence can't be part of a thing's concept (that would beg the question). But it's still a property things have: real thalers improve my finances and possible ones don't.
- And the modal versions don't use *existence* as the perfection. They use **necessary existence**, which clearly does distinguish kinds of thing.

## 4. The Modal Versions

Twentieth century. Malcolm and Hartshorne noticed a second argument in *Proslogion* 3.

Core form (Hartshorne):

1. Possibly, a perfect being exists. ◇G
2. Necessarily, if a perfect being exists, it exists necessarily. □(G → □G) ("Anselm's principle")
3. So a perfect being exists.

In S5, 1 and 2 give □G.

**Plantinga's "victorious" version** (1974):

- **Maximal excellence** = omniscient, omnipotent, morally perfect.
- **Maximal greatness** = maximally excellent in *every* possible world.
- Premise: maximal greatness is possibly exemplified.
- A property that's had in every world or none, if possibly had, is had. So it's exemplified in every world, including this one.

All three are valid. Everything hangs on premise 1. What they say for it:

- Hartshorne: use the other proofs to show it's conceivable.
- Malcolm: the concept has a place in people's lives, so it's not self-contradictory.
- Plantinga: it's rational to accept, though he doesn't claim it's a proof. (Maydole, drily: "hardly the highest standard for what counts as rational!")

**The reverse parody.** This is the real problem:

1. Possibly, a perfect being does **not** exist. ◇¬G
2. Same Anselm's principle.
3. So a perfect being does not exist.

Equally valid. So whoever starts decides the outcome. "Possibly God exists" is not the modest premise it sounds like: for a necessary being, possible = actual. And "I can conceive it" doesn't establish possibility either way.

So the ontological argument at least proves this: **God's existence is either necessary or impossible**. There's no "happens to exist" or "happens not to". That's worth having.

## 5. Gödel

- Two handwritten pages, 1970, shown to Dana Scott. Published after his death.
- An axiom system about **positive properties** (roughly: pure perfections, involving no privation).
    - a property or its negation is positive, not both
    - what a positive property entails is positive
    - being **God-like** (having every positive property) is positive
    - necessary existence is positive
- Theorem: a God-like being necessarily exists.
- The possibility premise is *derived* here, not assumed: positive properties can't entail a contradiction, so being God-like is possible. This is Leibniz's compatibility proof done rigorously.
- Known problem (Sobel): the axioms as given imply **modal collapse**, everything true is necessarily true. Later versions (Anderson and others) repair it.

## 6. Maydole's Own: The Modal Perfection Argument

- A **perfection** = a property it's necessarily better to have than not.
- **Supreme** = nothing could be greater, and it's greater than everything else.

1. If a property is a perfection, its negation isn't.
2. Perfections entail only perfections.
3. Being supreme is a perfection.

From these: possibly a supreme being exists. Then by modal logic: one does, and only one.

- Advantage: like Gödel, the possibility premise is proved from other premises, so the reverse parody has nothing to grab.
- He argues "almost supreme" parodies fail because being almost supreme isn't a perfection.
- Costs he admits: it uses the Barcan formula (controversial), and premise 3 carries a lot.

His conclusion is restrained: some ontological arguments are sound, non-question-begging and parody-proof. "Good logic does convince sometimes. Other times, something else is needed."

## 7. Where It Stands

| Version | Valid? | Weak point |
|---|---|---|
| Anselm | yes, as reconstructed | "greater", existence-in-reality as great-making |
| Descartes–Leibniz | yes | is existence a perfection? are perfections compatible? |
| Malcolm / Hartshorne / Plantinga | yes (S5) | the possibility premise; reverse parody |
| Gödel | yes | what "positive" means; modal collapse |
| Maydole | yes | Barcan formula; premise 3 |

General worries:

- **S5.** Needed throughout. See [[Modal Logic: Necessity and Possibility]] sec. 5.
- **Conceivable ≠ possible.**
- **Begging the question?** In a valid argument the conclusion is always "in" the premises. The question is whether you could reasonably accept the premises without already believing the conclusion. For the possibility premise that's doubtful.
- Aquinas rejected Anselm's argument: God's existence is self-evident *in itself* but not *to us*, since we don't know His essence. Feser doesn't include an ontological argument among his five either.

## 8. The East and the Ontological Argument

*Mine.*

- It's a Western argument. Anselm, 1070s, Latin. I don't know of any Eastern Father who argues this way, and there's a reason.
- **We don't have a concept of God's essence.** The argument starts from *what God is* and reads existence off it. Orthodox theology says what God is, is unknowable: we know *that* He is and know Him in His energies, not the definition of His essence. If so there's nothing to run the argument on. This is close to Aquinas's objection, and the East would press it harder. See [[Essence-Energies Distinction]].
- "That than which nothing greater can be conceived" is, to be fair, a *negative* description. It doesn't claim to grasp God. Anselm even says in ch. 15 that God is greater than can be conceived. So it's nearer to apophatic theology than it looks, and it's written as a prayer ("faith seeking understanding"), not as a proof for atheists.
- **"I AM."** Exodus 3:14, ὁ ὤν, on every icon of Christ. God as the one whose very name is being. The ontological argument is a philosopher's way of saying God can't be thought of as possibly not existing. As an argument it may fail; as an expression of what "God" means it's right.
- **What it gets right for apologetics**: God isn't one more thing that might or might not be there, like a planet. Either necessary or impossible. That changes what counts as evidence. "There's no evidence for God, like there's none for unicorns" misunderstands the kind of claim. Same lesson as the cosmological arguments: see [[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]] sec. 5.
- **Perfect-being theology** as a *method* (work out God's attributes by asking what's greatest) is riskier. It can produce a God of the philosophers who's too great to become incarnate or to suffer. The Cross isn't what anyone would have deduced. See [[God Being Pure Act Makes Incarnation Impossible]].
- Link to TAG: both say God's existence isn't a contingent matter to be settled by sampling the world. But TAG argues from what reasoning presupposes, not from a definition. See [[TAG]], [[Philosophy of Logic: What Grounds the Laws of Logic]] sec. 6.
