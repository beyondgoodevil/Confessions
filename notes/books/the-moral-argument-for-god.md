The question isn't "can atheists be good?" (obviously they can). It's whether, if there is no God, there is anything *for* them to be good *by*: whether there are moral **facts** at all, and whether we could know them.

## 1. Nietzsche vs George Eliot

- **George Eliot** (Mary Ann Evans) gave up belief in God but held to duty as "peremptory and absolute". A "religion of humanity": live for others even "beneath the on-coming shadow of an endless night".
- **Nietzsche** sneered at the "English flatheads": they're rid of the Christian God and now cling all the more to Christian morality. But "by breaking one main concept out of it, the faith in God, one breaks the whole."
- Their intuitions are just echoes of the dead God, whose churches are now his "tombs and sepulchres". "There are altogether no moral facts."
- **The moral argument** takes Nietzsche's conditional and Eliot's conviction:
    1. If there is no God, there are no moral facts. (Nietzsche)
    2. There are moral facts. (Eliot)
    3. So there is a God.

See [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]] for the Nietzsche side.

## 2. Versions Linville Sets Aside

| Version | Problem |
|---|---|
| **Law needs a lawgiver** | trades on an ambiguity of "law" |
| **Carrot and stick**: no heaven or hell, no reason to be moral | makes morality self-interest |
| **Only believers can know right and wrong** | false: Rom 2:14-15, the law "written in their hearts" |
| **Atheists can't be virtuous** | false, and not the point |
| **Societies without God decay** | sociology, not metaethics |

**Kant** is better: morality commands the **highest good** (virtue crowned with happiness). If there's no God to bring that about, the moral law demands the impossible. So we must postulate God (not as the *author* of the law but as its "director"). And if "one vast tomb" swallows good and wicked alike, it's hard to see why morality ultimately matters.

Linville offers two separate arguments.

## 3. Argument 1: From Evolutionary Naturalism

**The naturalist story**
- Ruse and Wilson: "ethics as we understand it is an illusion fobbed off on us by our genes in order to get us to cooperate."
- Natural selection installed "epigenetic rules", tendencies to feel obligation, guilt, fairness, because those feelings led to reproductive success. Not because they track any real obligations.
- Ruse: morality only works if we *believe* it's objective; Darwinism shows it isn't. "The illusion of objectivity."
- **Hume** already: look for the vice in a wilful murder and you'll find only your own feeling. See [[Is Ought]], [[Hume on Causation and Induction]].
- **Darwin**, *Descent of Man*: if men were reared like hive-bees, unmarried females would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers to kill their fertile daughters, and no one would think of interfering.

**The argument**
1. On naturalism, our moral beliefs are best explained by natural selection.
2. Natural selection selects for **fitness**, not **truth**. Our moral beliefs would be the same whether or not they were true.
3. So, on naturalism, we have no reason to think they're true: an **undercutting defeater** for moral beliefs as a whole.
4. On theism, God can have arranged (through evolution or otherwise) for our moral faculties to track moral truth.
5. So if we're warranted in our moral beliefs, theism explains this better than naturalism.

It's an epistemological argument, like the argument from reason. See [[The Argument from Reason]] sec. 7. Sharon Street makes the same point from the secular side: a "Darwinian dilemma" for any moral realism.

**Reply: Sturgeon's moral explanations.** Nicholas Sturgeon: moral facts *do* explain things (Hitler did what he did *because* he was depraved), so moral facts can be "tracked" by our beliefs, like other natural facts.

**Linville's answer**: take the hive-bee world. There, the beliefs that infanticide is a sacred duty are fitness-conferring. Either
- they're true there: then justice doesn't supervene on the same natural facts (or supervenes on fitness itself, which is absurd: moral = whatever helps reproduction); or
- they're false: then fitness and moral truth come apart, and our beliefs here are true only by luck.

Either way naturalism can't guarantee that moral beliefs which help survival are true.

## 4. Argument 2: From Personal Dignity

**Step 1: our moral beliefs assume dignity.**
- What we take as obvious (rape is wrong, torturing children for fun is wrong) seems to rest on the **worth of persons**: persons ought never to be treated that way. Kant's "never merely as a means".
- Test the main theories against this:

| Theory | Why it fails to explain the wrongness of, say, rape |
|---|---|
| **Utilitarianism** | wrong only because it lowers total welfare; could be right if it raised it. Persons are just containers for utility (Rawls: doesn't take seriously the distinction between persons) |
| **Virtue ethics** (Hursthouse) | "raping would be uncharitable" is true but grotesquely understated. The wrong is to the *victim*, not a flaw in the rapist's character. Midgley: refraining from cruelty only to keep one's own character clean is narcissistic |
| **Environmental / biocentric ethics** | puts persons on a level with other living things; loses what's special about persons |

- Linville: a virtue ethic grounded in **respect for persons** could work, but then dignity is doing the work.

**Step 2: naturalism can't ground dignity.**
- On naturalism persons are products of unguided processes, latecomers, not different in kind from other animals. Being human is "speciesism" (Singer).
- Attempts to locate dignity in natural capacities (rationality, self-awareness) make it come in **degrees** and exclude infants and the severely impaired. Then they don't have full dignity.

**Step 3: theism can.**
- If the ultimate reality is a **Person**, personhood is at the top of reality, not a by-product. Persons made by and for a personal God, in His image, have worth not because of capacities but because of what they *are* and who they're related to.
- So persons as such have dignity, whatever their abilities.

## 5. Common Objections

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| **Euthyphro**: is it good because God commands it, or does He command it because it's good? | neither: God's commands come from His nature, which *is* the good. See [[The Euthyphro Dilemma and the Orthodox Answer]] |
| **Moral realism without God** (Shafer-Landau, Wielenberg, Enoch): moral facts are just brute necessary truths | possible in principle, but then there's no explanation of why our faculties grasp them (sec. 3), and no explanation of why persons, of all things, have this worth (sec. 4) |
| **Atheists know morality fine** | agreed: the argument is about grounding, not knowledge. Rom 2:14-15 |
| **Which God?** | the argument gives a personal, good ground of morality. Not the full Christian God, but close to it |

## 6. Notes

*Mine.*

- The moral argument is really several: ontological (what *makes* moral facts true), epistemological (how we *know* them: Linville's first), and from dignity (what *kind* of thing has worth: his second). Worth keeping them apart.
- The dignity argument fits the Orthodox anthropology best. The image of God is the ground of human worth, and the image is in the person, not in a list of capacities. See [[Image and Likeness]], [[Being Made in the Image of God]].
- The evolutionary argument parallels Plantinga's EAAN almost exactly. Same structure: unguided selection aims at fitness, not truth.
- Ties to [[Is Ought]]: Hume's gap is a problem for naturalism (no "ought" in nature), not for a view where the good is the source of nature.
