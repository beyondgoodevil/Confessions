> "I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5)

> "Wherefore let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall." (1 Cor 10:12)

Nussbaum's question is the Greeks' question: **how much luck can a good human life live with, and how much should it?** By "luck" (*tuche*) she means not randomness but whatever happens to us rather than being done by us. She follows the question through three tragedians, Plato and Aristotle. Her thesis is that tragedy shows goodness as vulnerable. Plato built a heroic philosophy to make it invulnerable, and half-admitted the cost. Aristotle came back to a humanly limited, fragile goodness, and he is right.

Her opponent throughout is not just Plato but **Kant**. For Kant, one value, the good will, is immune to luck, and everything else is mere "happiness". She thinks this has blinded modern readers to Greek tragedy, which then looks "primitive".

## 1. The Vine and the Gem (ch. 1)

- Pindar (*Nemean* 8): "human excellence grows like a vine tree, fed by the green dew, raised up, among wise men and just, to the liquid sky." A praise-poet assumes goodness is the agent's own. Yet he pictures it as a plant: it needs good stock, weather, keepers and friends. "We have all kinds of needs for those we love."
- The image also suggests that the *beauty* of human excellence lies partly in its vulnerability. "The tenderness of a plant is not the dazzling hardness of a gem." Odysseus chose an ageing Penelope over Calypso's unchanging splendour.
- **Three problems of luck:**
  1. The good life seems to need **vulnerable components**: friends, love, politics, possessions.
  2. Values can **conflict**, through no fault of ours.
  3. The **passions** tie us to the world of risk and can disorder us from within.
- **Two pictures of practical reason.** She draws them as a table:
  - (A) the hunter, trapper and binder, soul hard, intellect "pure sunlight", trust only in the immutable, a solitary good;
  - (B) the plant, both active and receptive, soul "soft, porous, though with a definite structure", intellect "flowing water, given and received", the good life lived with friends.
  - B is not the opposite of A but A balanced with what A shuns.
- She argues for reading tragedy as philosophy. A whole play shows deliberation *searching* for what matters rather than handing us a labelled example. And our emotional responses are part of how we come to know.

## 2. Tragic Conflict: Aulis and the Seventh Gate (ch. 2)

**The philosophers' "solutions"**
- Socrates in the *Euthyphro*: conflicting divine demands would mean "the pious and impious would be one and the same", so at most one claim is real.
- Sartre's young man (the Resistance or his mother): principles fail, so improvise "without regret".
- Hare: rewrite the rule to include the exception ("Don't lie, except to the enemy in wartime").
- Kant: "a conflict of duties and obligations is inconceivable"; only the stronger "ground" was ever a duty.
- Nussbaum: "this is not how it *feels*", and Aeschylus shows why the feeling is right.

**Agamemnon at Aulis**
- Zeus commands the war, which is just, against a breach of hospitality. Artemis, for no stated fault of his, becalms the fleet. The men are starving. Calchas: only Iphigenia's death will free them. "Which of these is without evils?" Either way he does wrong, and the sacrifice is plainly the lesser wrong.
- The Chorus does not chiefly blame the *choice*. They blame what happens next. Having decided, he says: "it is right and holy that I should desire with exceedingly impassioned passion the sacrifice". He "put on the yoke-strap of necessity" and "changed his mind to the all-daring". He has her lifted "like a goat" and gagged with "the voiceless power of the bridle". The omen of eagles eating a pregnant hare is fulfilled: he kills his child as if she were an animal.
- The Chorus remembers what he refuses to see: her saffron robes, the "pitiful arrow from her eye", her singing at his table.
- **Eteocles** in *Seven against Thebes* finds his brother at the seventh gate and arms "eagerly". The women: "do not become similar in passion to a person called by the worst names." He sees conflict only because he has spent his life pretending Thebans have no families, all "sown" from earth.

**What the Choruses teach**
- Some wrongs done under necessity still need **punishment and remorse**: "a man's dark blood once it has fallen to the ground, who by incantation can call it up again?" Orestes' Furies are "at his most sane" moment.
- We can be blamed for our **passions and perception**, not only our acts: Agamemnon's "willing boldness of temper" (*tharsos hekousion*, 803).
- The good agent sees the conflict as a conflict, imagines both sides, chooses, and then feels **remorse**, not merely "regret". (Her example: a defence lawyer allows only "regret".)
- ***Pathei mathos***, "learning through suffering", means more than suffering as a means to knowing. The suffering is itself part of the knowledge. "Agamemnon knows that Iphigenia is his child all through… He doesn't really know that she is his daughter."
- Tragedy also shows *why* the theories are tempting: Agamemnon's own move from horror to "May all turn out well" is the philosophers' move.

## 3. Antigone: Two Simplifiers (ch. 3)

- The play is about practical wisdom (it ends: "practical wisdom is by far the chief part of happiness"). Its key word is ***deinon***: wonderful, terrible, strange. "Many things are *deinon*, but none more *deinon* than man."
- **Creon** reduces all value to the city. "Just" means "well-disposed to the city". Friends and enemies are defined civically, and Haemon's bride is a field to plough. His city is a ship, a tool for mastering chance.
- **Antigone** reduces all value to duties to the family dead: "my life is long since dead, to serve the dead". She loves Polynices as a *duty*, with "no personal memory". She makes Zeus say only what she wants; the Chorus calls her *autonomos*, "a law to herself".
- Neither is a lover. "Not one of the gods, not one human being escapes the power of *eros*", yet these two do. Haemon and Ismene are the lovers.
- Antigone's choice is the better one: the unwritten law of burial outranks a ruler's decree, and her piety harms no one but herself. And at the end she *mourns*, comparing herself to Niobe. She admits the conflict.
- **Against Hegel**: he was right that both are one-sided, but wrong that the answer is a synthesis that abolishes conflict. Real distinct values must be able to collide. Creon learns only when he loses his son: "To yield is terrible. But to stand against… is also terrible." He concludes, "it is best to hold to the established laws to the end of life."

## 4. Plato: Goodness Without Fragility? (chs. 4–7)

**The *Protagoras*: a science of measurement**
- The tragedians left us at the mercy of luck. Socrates offers a ***techne***, an art like Prometheus' "numbering, chief of all the stratagems". "The *techne* of measurement would have cancelled the effect of the appearance… thus saving our life."
- If all values are quantities of **one thing**, conflict disappears: you lose a smaller amount of the same, not something different. Akrasia (weakness of will) disappears too, as an optical illusion of nearness. Pleasure enters as a "place-holder" for the single measure, not because Socrates is a hedonist.

**Plato's anti-tragic theatre (Interlude 1)**
- The dialogue is drama "purged of theater's characteristic appeal to powerful emotion, a pure crystalline theater of the intellect". Creon learns through grief; Plato's interlocutor learns through contradiction.

**The *Republic*: the standpoint of perfection** (ch. 5)
- True value is what a perfect, needless being would still choose: activities that are stable, pure and not "relative to" our pains. That is mathematics and contemplation above all. Eudoxus prayed to be burnt up like Phaethon if he could only stand by the sun and learn its size.
- Nussbaum takes Plato's longing seriously. Even Nietzsche's "last man" is the man who no longer wants to transcend himself. But she shows its price: "the denial that the body, therefore gender, is of any ethical significance at all".

**The *Symposium*: the ladder and Alcibiades** (ch. 6)
- Diotima's ascent asks us to see beauty in a body, a soul, a law and a proof as one homogeneous "sea", so the choice between a beloved and a theorem becomes "n measures of water" against "n + 100". She quotes Vlastos: on this view "the individual, in the uniqueness and integrity of his or her individuality, will never be the object of our love".
- Then **Alcibiades** arrives drunk and speaks of *one* person. Socrates is a Silenus: plain outside, god-statues within. Alcibiades has "the lover's understanding": particular, gained through sense, emotion and intellect together, knowing *how* to treat this one. But Socrates has become "stone", unaroused all night beside him, unreachable. "He is stone; and he also turns others to stone."
- Plato's design: "you can't" have both. "The sight of reason begins to see clearly when the sight of the eyes begins to grow dim." She calls the dialogue a "comic tragedy of choice". Alcibiades' death haunts its telling: he went on to deface the herms, and was shot with arrows.

**The *Phaedrus*: the recantation** (ch. 7)
- Socrates covers his head and gives a speech against *eros*, then uncovers it and recants: "some kinds of madness can be responsible for the greatest of goods for us". Lovers come to know each other's "habits and ways" and "the trace within themselves of the nature of their own god".
- On her reading, Plato here criticises his own middle-period view, and the passions get more than an instrumental role.

## 5. Aristotle: The Fragility of the Good Human Life (chs. 8–13)

**Method** (ch. 8)
- "Set down the appearances (*phainomena*) and, first working through the puzzles, show if possible the truth of all the beliefs we hold" (*NE* VII.1). Philosophy stays within human experience and language.
- Heraclitus in the kitchen by the stove: "Come in. Don't be afraid. There are gods here too." And from Aristotle's lost *On the Good*: "You must remember that you are a human being: not only in living well, but also in doing philosophy."

**Practical wisdom** (ch. 10)
- "That practical wisdom is not scientific understanding is obvious" (*NE* VI.8). The measure is the person of practical wisdom, and "the discernment rests with perception". Particulars come first. Her example is Hecuba in *Trojan Women* burying Astyanax on Hector's shield: perception through grief teaches her about the gods and revises her whole conception of the good.

**Priam** (ch. 11)
- *NE* I.10: "many reversals… come about in the course of a life; and it is possible for the person who was most especially going well to encounter great calamities in old age, as in the stories told about Priam… nobody calls him *eudaimon*."
- Kant-minded interpreters read this as "happiness" being lost while "virtue" is safe. Nussbaum argues the text means more. Virtue is a state that exists for the sake of **activity**, and catastrophe can block the activity. So the good person "will never become wretched", but he can lose *eudaimonia* itself.
- **The gods** (*NE* X.8): would they make contracts, be brave, give money? "Isn't that commendation vulgar, since they have no base appetites?" Human virtues are *constituted* by need and limit. "Lack of limit is itself a limit."

**Friendship and the city** (ch. 12)
- Man is a "political animal". *Philia* needs mutuality, affection and **separateness**, so it is the most vulnerable good, and still no one would choose to live without friends while having every other good. She treats *NE* X.6–8 (the contemplative life as highest) as an un-Aristotelian, Platonic fragment.

**Pity and fear** (Interlude 2)
- Pity requires believing that a good person can suffer serious, undeserved harm, and that we ourselves are exposed. For the Platonist it can only be delusion. For Aristotle, pity and fear are **sources of "clarification"**. That is her reading of *katharsis*.

**The *Hecuba*: goodness corrupted** (ch. 13)
- Hecuba, queen of fallen Troy, has trusted her guest-friend Polymestor with her last son. He murders the boy for gold. She had said that "*nomos*" (law, convention) rules even the gods, "if *nomos* is destroyed… there is nothing else like it in human life."
- Betrayed, she learns a "newly-learned *nomos*" (the word also means "melody"): revenge. She blinds Polymestor and kills his sons. He crawls in "on all fours, a mountain beast". She is prophesied to become a dog with fiery eyes.
- This inverts the *Eumenides*, where dog-like Furies became women welcomed into the city by trust. Here a woman becomes a dog through broken trust. "We can become dogs or gods, existing without trust."
- Against Nietzsche: revenge is not only the vice of the weak. The **noble** are *more* exposed, "because it is the noble person… who has unsuspiciously staked a world on the faith and care of others".

## 7. Assessment

*Mine.*

**She is right against Kant and against the stone**
- The best thing in the book is Chapter 2. Kant's "a conflict of duties is inconceivable" fits a legal code, not a life. The Church knew this before Kant. St Basil's canon 13 says soldiers who kill in war are not murderers, yet advises them to abstain from Communion for three years, "since their hands are not clean". That is exactly Nussbaum's "remainder": a deed that may be necessary still leaves a wound that needs repentance. Orthodox moral practice works with **economy** and **penance**, not Hare's tidy exceptions. See [[Economia]].
- The Chorus blaming Agamemnon's *desire*, not only his act, is closer to the Gospel than to Kant: "whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart" (Matt 5:28). The Fathers also judge the inner movement, the *logismos* and the consent. See [[How a Thought Becomes a Sin: The Stages of Temptation]] and [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- *Pathei mathos* has a Christian form. Compunction (*penthos*) and tears are knowledge, not just feeling. Climacus calls it "joy-making mourning". Christ wept at Lazarus' tomb *knowing* He would raise him (John 11:35). Grief there is not ignorance but true sight. See [[The Ladder of Divine Ascent]].
- The ladder that turns Socrates to stone is not Christian ascent. Saints are not stones: Silouan weeps for the whole world, and Paul is "beside himself". The Christian hope is to love *more* particularly, not less: "I have called thee by thy name" (Isa 43:1). See [[Eros and Agape: Plato, Nygren, Dionysius and the Fathers]].

**But "fragile" is not the last word**
- She wants to read the Greeks "not through the lens of Christian beliefs", and in a footnote says Nietzsche was right that a post-Christian culture can learn its intuitions from them. That is fair as a method. But it means she never asks the Christian question: **what if the Gem became a Vine?**
- Christ takes Pindar's own image: "I am the vine." The answer to fragile goodness is neither Plato's invulnerable stone nor Aristotle's well-tended but finally unprotected plant. It is to be **grafted** into a Vine who is God Himself, who took on the plant's whole vulnerability. He hungered, wept, was betrayed by a friend (Hecuba's exact wound) and was killed. "Lack of limit is itself a limit," says Aristotle about the gods. The Incarnation is God taking limit without ceasing to be God. So courage, generosity and grief are no longer "vulgar" to ascribe to God: they are found in Gethsemane. See [[On the Incarnation: Main Argument]] and [[Being Made in the Image of God]].
- **Luck becomes providence, without tragedy being erased.** "All things work together for good to them that love God" (Rom 8:28) does not say each thing *is* good. Job and the Cross keep the tragic real. But it denies that *tuche* has the last word. Priam lost his *eudaimonia*. The martyrs, in the worst "luck", are called *makarioi*, blessed, because blessedness is communion with God, which no tyrant can take: "who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" (Rom 8:35). Stump's reading of Job makes the same move. See [[Wandering in Darkness]] and [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].
- Yet the Christian is **not** a Stoic or Platonic invulnerable self. "Let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall." The desert Fathers held that no one is safe until death. Climacus' monks fall after decades, and Peter denies. Hecuba's corruption is the Orthodox fear of the fall of the righteous. The difference is that the remedy is not stability of character alone but **repentance and grace**, which can return even a fallen person to the vine. See [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]].

**Hecuba and the end of revenge**
- Euripides shows that betrayal can turn the noble into a beast, and the *Eumenides* shows a city taming the Furies by trust. Christianity adds the one thing neither play has: **forgiveness** that does not wait for restored trust. Christ, betrayed, says "Father, forgive them" before anyone repents. Hecuba's "newly-learned *nomos*" of revenge meets a newer law: "Recompense to no man evil for evil… Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord" (Rom 12:17–19). The noble person stays human after betrayal not by trusting less, but by entrusting judgement to God. See [[Justice in the Oresteia: From Vendetta to the Court?]], [[Hamlet: Revenge, the Ghost and Purgatory]] and [[1 Corinthians 5:12-13: God Judges, Not We]].
- Hecuba's claim that *nomos* is "only human" yet the stable ground of everything is where her faith breaks. Paul answers: the law is "written in their hearts" (Rom 2:15) by the Logos, not invented by the city. Human conventions can be destroyed; the image of God in a person can be defaced but not erased. See [[Is Ought]] and [[The Euthyphro Dilemma and the Orthodox Answer]].

**What I take from it**
- Three temptations stay alive in every Christian life: Agamemnon's turn from horror to "May all turn out well"; Creon's and Antigone's simplifying away the other claim; and Socrates' wish to be stone. The Orthodox way is the Chorus's: see both sides, choose, grieve, repent, and keep loving particular persons, *in* Christ rather than by escaping the human.
- Nussbaum's plant needs a better gardener than the Greeks had: "I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman" (John 15:1).
