Augustine said that if the Platonists had lived again, with a few changes of words and opinions they would have become Christians (*True Religion* 4.7). For most of Christian history, Platonism was the philosophy the Church found most congenial, and it shaped the Fathers' language. The Hampton and Kenney volume traces it from the NT to today and argues it's not an accidental alliance but a lasting **"Christian Platonic" tradition**.

## 1. What Platonism Is

Not just Plato's dialogues but a family of convictions, roughly:
1. **Transcendence**: the highest reality is beyond the physical and the changing.
2. **The Good** is the source of being and intelligibility. See [[The Good in Plato]].
    - **The Good "above essence"** (*Republic* VI): above the existence that involves having an essence (complexity), not above existence altogether. But the more the first principle's simplicity is stressed, the harder it is to see how it explains anything.
    - Augustine: "because God is good, we exist; and insofar as we exist, we are good."
3. **Participation**: things are what they are by sharing in a higher reality.
4. **The intelligible** is more real than the sensible; the world is an image of something higher.
5. The **soul** is akin to the divine and can ascend to it through purification and contemplation.
6. **Teleology**: everything aims at the Good.

- **Middle Platonism**: **Alcinous**'s *Handbook* (*Didaskalikos*) ch. 10 merges the Demiurge, the Unmoved Mover and the Good. **Numenius** (*On the Good*) separates a first god (the Good, absolutely simple) from a second (the Demiurge, intellect, "double": contemplating and making). Xenocrates and **Polemon** had already "demythologised" the *Timaeus*: the Demiurge becomes a divine intellect acting eternally, using the contents of its own mind as the pattern (Polemon: "the cosmos is God").
- **Plotinus**: the One's "internal" and "external" activity: Intellect is the One's external activity, Soul Intellect's. **Proclus**: participable **Henads** for each intelligible; "otherness" that isn't "difference".
- **Theology** (*theologia*): in Plato it meant mythology (what philosophy goes beyond); in Aristotle a science. Dionysius: affirmative theology follows the **procession** (noblest names first), negative theology the return. Boethius calls it *theologica*. Medieval: "no sacred Scripture is exempt from the rules of the liberal arts".

## 2. Why the Fathers Liked It

- A **transcendent** God, not a god within nature (against Stoic materialism and the Epicureans).
    - **Divine attributes**: Jewish and Christian apologists argued God is incorporeal (so present everywhere), immutable (so trustworthy), eternal, impassible (so His victory is assured).
- An immaterial **soul**, accountable and immortal (against the Epicureans).
- A **moral** universe, ordered by goodness, where evil is privation, not a rival principle.
- **Ascent** to God through purification: compatible with asceticism.
- The *Timaeus*: a good maker forming the world because He is good and "without envy". Read as a parallel to Genesis. See [[The Forms and the Demiurge]].
- Justin: Plato borrowed from Moses. Clement: philosophy was to the Greeks what the Law was to the Jews, a schoolmaster to Christ.
- **Allegory** (*allegoresis*): finding "another" meaning than the literal.

## 3. What They Changed

| Platonist idea | Christian correction |
|---|---|
| the world is **eternal** (or matter is) | creation **out of nothing**, with a beginning. See [[Creation out of Nothing]] |
| the **Forms** exist on their own | the Forms are **God's ideas** (Philo, Augustine), or the **logoi** in the Word (Maximus) |
| the One **emanates** by necessity | God creates **freely**, by will |
| the highest principle is **impersonal** | God is three **Persons**; the Trinity isn't a hierarchy of One, Mind, Soul. Nicaea against subordinationism |
| the **soul pre-exists** and falls into a body | soul and body created together (Origen's pre-existence condemned 553) |
| the body is a **prison**; salvation is escape | the body is good, will **rise**, and the Word **became flesh** |
| **transmigration** | one life, one judgement |
| union by **one's own** ascent | by **grace**, through Christ and the Church |
| only philosophers ascend | the Gospel is for all, the simple included |

The **Incarnation** and **resurrection** are where Platonism had to break. "The Word was made flesh" was Augustine's point: the Platonists told him of the Word but not of His becoming flesh (*Confessions* VII.9). See [[Augustine's Confessions: Memory, Time and the Restless Heart]].

- **Creation**: Zimmerman: Plotinus gives a real philosophical account of **creation** (production of a thing in its whole substance, dependent for continued existence), even though eternal.
    - The **Creator–creature** distinction "competed with, and perhaps rendered redundant", the Platonic intelligible–sensible distinction. Gregory of Nyssa calls each, in different places, "the highest division of all beings".
- **The Forms**:
    - **Athenagoras**: the Son is the Father's mind, who "came forth to be form and act (*idea kai energeia*) for all material things".
    - **Origen** refuted the divine Ideas as separate (*On First Principles* II.2.6).
    - **Maximus**: "He who is beyond all beings does not know beings in a manner derived from beings; He knows them as His own wills."
- **The Trinity**: Plotinus's three hypostases can't be "counted" since the One is beyond substance and quantity: a problem the Cappadocians faced for the Trinity.
    - **Cyril** of Alexandria's anti-Platonism is implicit anti-Arianism: Platonism resembles Arianism (subordinate hypostases).
    - "Second God" was proscribed by Gregory's time, not because it implied inequality but because it sounded polytheistic. Gregory: second in order needn't mean lesser, as the second Adam isn't less than the first.

## 4. History in Brief

**Antiquity**
- **Philo** of Alexandria: Jewish Platonism, the Logos, the Forms as God's thoughts.
- **Justin Martyr**, **Clement**, **Origen**: Alexandrian Christian Platonism. Origen goes furthest (pre-existence, apokatastasis).
- **The Cappadocians**: Gregory of Nyssa especially: infinite God, endless ascent (*epektasis*), apophaticism.
- **Augustine**: "the books of the Platonists" lead him to see God as immaterial; Platonism is the way into Christianity for him. See [[Augustine's City of God]] (books VIII–X on the Platonists).
    - Kenney: the Platonic ascents of *Confessions* VII reach eternal being but can't sustain the soul there: Scripture and Christ are needed. God is "definitively made known as a beneficent Father, whose Son was incarnate". Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis* (Phil 3:13) "in a metaphysical key".
- **Dionysius**: Proclus's Neoplatonism Christianised: hierarchies, procession and return, the divine names. See [[St. Dionysius the Areopagite]].
    - His pseudonym links him to Athens = philosophy (Acts 17:34). Creatures participate in God's "rays" of goodness, not His transcendent divinity (Tollefsen).
- **Maximus**: the logoi; corrects Origenism. See [[Maximus the Confessor as a European Philosopher]].
    - Tollefsen: "basically in the Platonic tradition", except for recent creation, God's will to create for His creatures' sake, and the threefold Incarnation (in creation, Scripture, Christ). Meyendorff calls him "the real founder of Byzantine theology". The key to the *logoi* is **prayer**, not secular learning (a Platonic motif: purification before contemplation).
- **Boethius**: *Consolation of Philosophy*.

**Middle Ages**
- West: Eriugena, the School of Chartres, Bonaventure; Aquinas combines Aristotle with Dionysian Platonism (participation).
    - **Early Franciscans** (Schumacher), the more "Augustinian" school: following Hugh of St Cher, the soul has a "**substantial unitability**" to the body: crucial for the resurrection and the value of the body. They used **Avicenna** to read Augustine and Aristotle.
    - Aquinas grants the Platonists "relative truth": things diversified by participation in being are caused by one First Being who has being most perfectly. The **transcendentals** (*communia*, *transcendentia*).
- East: Psellos, Italos (condemned 1082 for too much Platonism), Palamas against Barlaam.
    - **Byzantium** (Tollefsen): Andrew Louth's "**diffused Platonism**" (thinkers who criticised "the Greeks" while sharing their notions) vs "formal Platonism".
    - "Outside wisdom" (Greek learning) vs inside wisdom (Scripture and the Fathers).

**Renaissance**
- **Ficino**'s *Platonic Theology*, the Florentine Academy, the "ancient theology" (*prisca theologia*: Hermes, Zoroaster, Pythagoras, Plato as precursors). See [[Emergence of Magic and Neoplatonism in the Renaissance]].
    - Gersh: **Ficino** writes "an image of Plato" close to "the truth of Christianity"; commentaries on Plato, Plotinus, Dionysius; a Christian Platonism alongside university Aristotelianism, to the 17th century.
- **Nicholas of Cusa**: God's separation from creation results from His **indistinction** (*indistincta distinctio*), "Not-Other".
- **Radical Reformation** and Hermeticism: Paracelsus (microcosm/macrocosm), **Weigel** (God as "darkness"; Eckhart's God vs Godhead), **Böhme** (life as struggle of contrary impulses; the *Ungrund*, groundless abyss of freedom).
- **Cambridge Platonists** (17th c.): against Hobbes's materialism.
    - **John Smith**: averting attention from the senses lets "the light of the Divine Word" fall on us.
    - **Cudworth**: against "Epicurean" atheism, "Plastic Nature" mediating between matter and mind (like the World Soul).
- **Early modern** (Hedley): **Descartes**'s "if I err, I am alive" (from Augustine); **Malebranche**: occasionalism and "vision in God": all knowledge is revealed; **Leibniz**: monads in preestablished harmony; **Berkeley**: immaterialism (Leibniz: "much that is correct and close to my own view").

**Modern**
- Coleridge, the Romantics; C. S. Lewis ("It's all in Plato, all in Plato: bless me, what *do* they teach them at these schools!" *The Last Battle*); Iris Murdoch; Hart, Perl and others today.
    - **Romanticism**: Schelling (the Father as the abyss, "nothing", from which the creative will emerges), Hegel (art reconciles sensible and intelligible: Plotinus more than Plato), Coleridge (Newman: "a liberty of speculation no Christian can tolerate"), Wordsworth, Blake.
    - **20th century**: Platonism mostly as a "tendency" woven with other threads; Yeats, Ginsberg; "Pop Platonism" in fantasy and sci-fi.

## 5. For and Against

**Critics**
- **Harnack** (19th c.): the "Hellenisation" of the Gospel: the simple message of Jesus buried under Greek metaphysics.
- **Protestant** and **biblical theology** critiques: the Hebrew vs the Greek mind; dynamic vs static; resurrection vs immortality.
- **Orthodox** critics (Florovsky, Romanides): the Fathers didn't Platonise Christianity; they **Christianised Hellenism**. Florovsky's slogan: "Christian Hellenism". The essentials are baptised and changed beyond recognition.
- **Heidegger** and postmodernism: Platonism as "onto-theology", forgetting being. See [[Heidegger and the God Beyond Being]].
    - **Heidegger**'s "second beginning" as "freedom from metaphysics"; Caputo.
- **Nietzsche** called Christianity "Platonism for the people" and made life itself art over chaos.

**Defenders**
- Without something like Platonic realism (truth, goodness, beauty, the transcendent), Christianity has no metaphysics and is left with nominalism and then secularism. See [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]].
- The Hampton/Kenney case: the alliance is natural: both see the world as a **gift and image** of a transcendent Good.
    - The volume has three parts: (1) **Theme** (core concepts: the Good, the One, participation, theology, allegory...), (2) **History** (antiquity to the 20th century), (3) **Engagements** (natural science, environment, aesthetics, materialism).
    - *Christian Platonism* names "the **transcendentalist foundation** of orthodox Christian theology, essential to its exegesis, doctrines, and sacramental practices", "the underlying transcendentalism of Nicene Christianity".
    - Their summary: the central message of Christian Platonism isn't an abstract, intellectualising tendency but "a focus on the **incarnational, participatory, and sacramental** character of being, which calls us back to its motive force in **love**".
    - **Pickstock** (closing): Christian Platonism and the sacramental.
- **Natural science** (Davison and Sherman): God causes creatures *in esse* (continuing to be), not just *in fieri* (coming to be). Aquinas: "the power of the higher cause is the power of the power of the lower cause"; "the dignity of causality is imparted even to creatures": secondary causes. Mathematical objects as "in the mind of God" solves both where they are and how they affect the world. **Eriugena**: all growing things are alive; the soul's relation to the body persists even through death.
- **Environment** (Hampton): the "secularisation thesis" questioned; Lynn White's critique of Christianity's role in ecological crisis answered: meaning isn't human-dependent; all things participate in meaning through the **divine ideas**. **Traherne**: "Nature teacheth nothing but the Truth"; "My Senses were Informers to my Heart, / The Conduits of His Glory". Since created and uncreated forms differ, nature's meaning can't be simply mapped onto ours: against anthropocentrism.
- **Aesthetics** (Viladesau): the Forms placed in the Logos; "likeness" means representation of the person (*hypostasis*), creating presence in both directions (cf. icons). Beauty involves *ekstasis*: self-forgetful absorption, both "ascent" and "horizontal" communion. Against Nietzsche: beauty as "**delight in form**" (Aquinas, Pasternak's Zhivago), coextensive with being; the "**sacramentality**" of all things.
- **Materialism** (Taliaferro): against physicalism and eliminativism (Dennett); Christian materialists (Corcoran, Merricks: "how to live forever without saving your soul"); defence of mind–body dualism (Ryle's caricature answered).

## 6. Notes

*Mine.*

- Florovsky's line is the right one for Orthodoxy: the Fathers used Platonic language but changed its content: creation, persons, Incarnation. The essence–energies distinction is a good example: it uses Neoplatonic structures (the One beyond being, its activity) to say something non-Neoplatonic (a personal God freely present). See [[Aristotle East and West]], [[Neoplatonism: Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus]].
- The danger points are the same ones the councils condemned: Origenism, and a "One" with no room for persons (see [[Neoplatonic Idea of Divine Simplicity]]).
