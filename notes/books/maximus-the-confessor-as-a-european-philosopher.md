St. Maximus (c. 580–662) is often called the greatest Byzantine theologian. This collection argues he's also a major **philosopher**, with an original metaphysics that offers an alternative to the main line of Western thought. Four parts: ontology, epistemology, anthropology, and Maximus in dialogue.

## 1. Life

- Possibly a civil servant in Constantinople (the Greek *Life*), or a Palestinian monk (a hostile Syriac *Life*). Became a monk; lived in North Africa.
- Opposed **monoenergism** and **monothelitism** (one energy, one will in Christ), the imperial compromise to reconcile the Miaphysites.
- Arrested, tried, his **tongue cut out and right hand cut off** so he couldn't speak or write; exiled to Georgia, died 662. Hence "Confessor".
- Vindicated at the **Sixth Ecumenical Council** (680–681): Christ has two natural wills and two energies. See [[Canons of the Sixth Ecumenical Council]].
- Main works: *Ambigua* (difficulties in Gregory Nazianzen and Dionysius), *Questions to Thalassius*, *Mystagogy*, *Four Hundred Chapters on Love*, *Disputation with Pyrrhus*.

## 2. The Logoi

- The **Logos** (the Word) contains the **logoi** (principles, reasons, "words") of all created things: God's will and intention for each thing.
- "The one Logos is many logoi, and the many logoi are one Logos." The one and the many reconciled in the Word. See [[The Problem of the One and the Many]].
- The logoi aren't Platonic Forms existing on their own, but **God's wills**, freely chosen. Each creature's logos is its true nature and its end.
    - God knows creatures "as His own acts of will" (Tanev).
    - Each being's *logos* is **threefold**: essence, power, energy (Tanev).
    - "According to nature" means "according to the *logos* of nature": God's intention for the creature.
- "What was God doing before creation?" (Augustine's question): Maximus answers with the *logoi* (Manoussakis).
- Knowing creation truly (*natural contemplation*, *theoria physike*) means seeing the logoi in things: the world as a book of the Word.
    - **Harrington**: Rémi Brague's "**inclusion**": taking in foreign objects (texts, cultures) without absorbing them: distinctively European, and needed for scientific objectivity. In the *Questions to Thalassius*, contemplating the *logoi* of nature is a means to union with God, but Maximus "clears a space for the independent use of intellect that will later become the space of modern science".

## 3. Motion: Genesis, Movement, Rest (Part I)

- Against **Origenism**, which taught: original rest (*stasis*) of minds in God → movement (*kinēsis*) as a fall → becoming (*genesis*) into bodies. Motion is a fall from unity.
- Maximus reverses the triad: **genesis → kinesis → stasis**. Creatures are made, then **move** by nature toward God, then come to **rest** in Him. Motion isn't sin; it's the natural desire of a creature for its end.
- Aristotle's influence: motion as actualising potency (Mantzanas's chapter). See [[Aristotle's Physics: Nature, Change and the Four Causes]].
    - The key difference from Aristotle: for Maximus motion is primarily a **returning motion**: creation moving back to its source.
    - Three kinds: motion **according to nature**, **returning** motion, motion **against nature** (sin).
    - Von Balthasar: Maximus took Gregory of Nyssa's axiom that finite being is characterised by **interval** (*diastēma*) and therefore by motion: "time and space are the expression of finitude itself".
- "**Nothing that is in motion has stopped before arriving at its object of desire**" (*Amb.* 7). The end of natural activity is "**repose** at the cause of created beings' motion".
- The triad **being, well-being, ever-well-being**.
- The end is **"ever-moving rest"** (*stasis aeikinētos*): rest that isn't stillness but endless movement in God. Like Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis*.
- Skliris's "three antinomies": **eschatological teleology** (the end comes from the future, God's goal, not from the past); **free dialectic** (the dialectic of being is fulfilled freely, not by necessity, unlike Hegel); **metaphysics of the resurrection** (the final state is bodily and personal).
    - Maximus offers tools to go beyond Hegelian dialectic: a "**free dialectic**" where the synthesis isn't necessary but freely given.
    - An "**Oriental voluntarism**" alternative to the Augustinian one that began Western voluntarism: will and energy belong to **nature**, which is good insofar as it is, and so must be saved entire (unlike Plotinus, for whom evil = non-being = matter).
    - *Logos*, *tropos* and *end* can't be defined alone: each "breaks into" the others. The *tropos* is "a hypostatic response in a dialogue" with God's *logos*.
    - Maximus's triads have a "**horseshoe**" form: the extremes meet, the centre mediates.
    - An "**asymmetrical**" synthesis: Christology has the first and last word (the end is present in the beginning: eschatology).
    - Theology "of God for His own sake" (negation, transcendence) is completed by Christology "of Christ for the sake of beings" (affirmation, presence).
- Manoussakis puts Maximus between **Anaxagoras** (Mind moving things) and **Kierkegaard** (movement of the self toward God).
    - ***Kinēsis*** is a nodal point where cosmology, anthropology and theology meet. All Presocratic philosophy is a philosophy of movement.
    - Anaxagoras's answer to "why is there something?": "because it is best": the world exists because moved toward perfection by *Nous*.
    - Plato made forms and participants static; Maximus makes participation **dynamic**.
    - **Kierkegaard**'s "moment" (*Augenblick*) and his idea of "repetition"; Heidegger: Kierkegaard first comprehended the moment. Both Maximus and Kierkegaard reject Origen's account of the fall from rest.

## 4. Holomerism (Tollefsen)

- The relation of **whole and parts** runs through everything: the universal and the particulars, the Church and the faithful, Christ and the members.
- The whole is in each part without being divided, and each part is in the whole. A metaphysics where unity doesn't swallow difference.
- The **circle** image: God is the centre from which the principles of beings stretch out like radii.
- Two movements: **expansion** (*diastolē*: from genus to species to individuals) and **contraction** (*systolē*: gathering back).
- Universals don't have their *logos* of being "by themselves" apart from particulars. All differences are present in the genus **potentially**; each species actualises one.
- Human beings share with angels and animals, so the human *logos* is delimited by the *logoi* of other creatures too.

**The system of relations** (Markov)
- God is "immanently active in bringing things together"; the relations among creatures are determined by **divine energy** and belong to their **essences**, not just accidents.
- "Relation" is the measure of union among beings and finally with God.
- God isn't intelligible; the soul can reach a "**simple union**" beyond discursive thought only with "limitless movement".
- Maximus doesn't arrange relations in a hierarchy of being as Dionysius does; even **marriage** becomes part of Christ's hypostatic life.

## 5. Language and Apophasis (Part II)

- Constas: Scripture and the world are both **words** of the Word; language is a mode of incarnation.
    - For Maximus, language is rooted in logic, and logic in ontology: an "**onto-logic**".
    - Against readings of Gregory of Nyssa's spirituality as a "mysticism of the gap" (Blowers: Gregory risked grounding rational beings' stability only on their own uncertain moral movement): Maximus grounds it in the *logoi*.
    - What seems "above" is really "**ahead**": reality coming to us from the future. Scripture's "shadow, image, and truth" (Heb 10:1).
- Wood: Christ is "**both mere man and naked God**": the Incarnation itself is the logic of apophasis: God is revealed **in** hiddenness, most hidden when most revealed.
    - "Out of his infinite longing for human beings, he has become truly and according to nature the very thing for which he longed" (*Amb. Th.* 5).
    - How can the natures stay unmixed yet their activities be "innovated"? The **logos/tropos** distinction (sec. 6).
    - Tabor: the disciples hadn't "cast off carnal life" but "were raised", their senses supplanted "by the activity of the Spirit".
    - "When we say the Divine 'exists', we do not say it exists in a certain way." We negate both affirmation and negation, because Christ is **both** by nature. In Christ God is both the symbol and the symbolised.
- **Depraz**: **negative theology** (God invisible, inexpressible, incomprehensible: negations of predicates) vs **apophaticism** (direct experience beyond concepts toward union).
    - Maximus gives "form" to Nyssa's distinction between mystical and symbolic theology, and "dialectises" Dionysius's duality of affirmative and negative.
    - **Yannaras** (*On the Absence and Unknowability of God*) and **Marion** (*God without Being*) radicalise this with Heidegger.
- See [[Apophatic and Cataphatic Theology]].

## 6. Person, Will and Nature (Part III)

**Logos and tropos** (Louth)
- Every nature has a **logos of being** (what it is, fixed) and a **tropos of existence** (how it exists, its mode). Change and newness happen at the level of **mode**, not nature.
    - *Logos* here means roughly "definition" (as in Plato).
    - Persons **exist** (*hyparxis*, *hyphistanai*: hence *hypostasis*); natures **are** (*ousia*, *einai*).
    - "**Every innovation takes place in relation to the mode of what is being innovated, not in relation to its principle of nature, because when a principle is innovated it results in the destruction of the nature.**"
- In Christ, human nature is unchanged in its **logos**, but exists in a new **mode**: united to the divine Person. That's how deification works: our nature isn't changed into another nature; its **mode** is transformed.
- The person is where the tropos lives: the ground of a later ontology of the person. See [[Nature and Person Distinction in Christology]], [[Action Is Not Proper to the Person]].

**The person** (Kapriev, Podbielski)
- "**Individual**" (Latin *individuum*, Greek *atomon*, "indivisible") belongs to the **natural** order (division of genus and species), not the hypostatic. Leontius's *enhypostaton* keeps the distinction. Christ can't be called an "individual" because He doesn't fall under the division from genus to species.
- Hypostasis: what exists "by itself", an essence with unique properties.
- Two conceptual chains: **essence/nature – necessity – law** (*logos* of nature) vs **hypostasis/person – relation – freedom – love – grace** (*tropos*). Kapriev asks whether these can really be opposed (a debate with **Zizioulas**: see [[Being as Communion]]).
- Deification is achieved in history: salvation (recovery of nature) is the first level; deification gives a **supernatural mode** of existence.
- *Prosōpon* never lost its meaning "**face**". "Face to face" (1 Cor 13:12); "beholding with unveiled face the glory of the Lord, we are transformed into the same image" (2 Cor 3:18): seeing God's glory changes who we are.

**The natural and gnomic will**
- **Natural will** (*thelēma physikon*): the nature's inborn desire for its good, for being, for God. Christ has a human natural will.
- **Gnomic will** (*gnōmē*): deliberation, hesitating between options: the will of a person who doesn't yet clearly see the good. It comes from ignorance after the Fall.
- Christ has **no gnomic will**: not because He lacks a human will but because He never wavers. So Christ's human will freely and naturally follows the divine.
- Freedom in the highest sense is **not choice between options** but freely willing the good without hesitation. Harper's chapter compares this to modern **compatibilism**.
    - *Gnōmē* and *hexis* are both "inner striving" (*orexis endiathetos*): expressions of human freedom (Kapriev).
    - Maximus pairs the corrupting laws of nature with the "**spiritual laws of nature**" renewed in Christ (Harper).
    - Departing from Aristotle and Evagrius, he makes **love** the primary virtue and the very means of attaining a consubstantial mode of being (Harper).
- Gethsemane: "not as I will, but as thou wilt" (Matt 26:39) shows two wills, the human freely aligning with the divine. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]].

**Man as mediator** (Tanev, Kochaczyk-Bonińska)
- Five divisions: uncreated/created, intelligible/sensible, heaven/earth, paradise/inhabited world, male/female. Man was called to unite them; Christ does it, starting from the last (born of a virgin) to the first (uniting creation to God).
- Man as **co-creator** and priest of creation. See [[Image and Likeness]].
    - The human vocation: all that is divine in man and the world "is active, and all that is human joins in this activity". Tanev compares this with Actor-Network Theory and innovation studies.
- **The sexes** (Kochaczyk-Bonińska): two "sins": the first, blameworthy: the fall from good to evil by choice; the second, blameless: the change from incorruption to corruption, which follows.
    - Sexual reproduction transmits the changed (mortal) nature.
    - Christ "has done away with the division of nature into male and female, which human nature in no way needed for generation" (*Amb.* 41): in Christ a human is "simply a human person according to the divine purpose".

## 7. In Dialogue (Part IV)

- **Loudovikos**: "analogical ecstasis": Maximus compared with Plotinus, Heidegger and Lacan on desire and the self going out of itself.
    - Dionysius's **synergy**: "every hierarchical order is elevated toward divine synergy according to its own analogy." A reciprocity of **ecstatic love** between God and man.
    - Plotinus: individual existence is a loss of Being, "a trace of life and intellect".
    - Heidegger: man fulfils his essence as ecstasis answering Being's call. Lacan: "it is not really the Other that I love but his Desire for me."
    - Maximus: God creating means God **wants** the world, opening Himself in incomprehensible ecstatic love and giving creation its own identity.
- **Coyle**: Maximian echoes in Bonaventure's exemplarism.
    - To be a creature is to have "a constitutive and determinative difference". Difference isn't from the Fall but the centre of an "**erotics of incarnation**": God wishes "always and in all things to accomplish the mystery of his embodiment" (*Amb.* 7).
    - "Man will remain wholly man in soul and body... but will become wholly God in soul and body owing to grace."
    - Bonaventure: the Good is "the first name of God"; in the Incarnation "the Exemplar becomes the Image".
- **Cvetković**: God's oneness as a **unity of persons**.
    - Maximus quotes Gregory Nazianzen almost verbatim but avoids identifying hypostases with **properties**.
    - The analogy between Christ's hypostasis/natures and the Trinity is "the key for understanding the structure of reality".
- **Steiris**: Maximus and al-Farabi on imagination.
    - For Maximus, the mind perceives sensibles through **representation** (*phantasia*); mental images aren't certain knowledge.
    - Imagination is tied to the fragmented **gnomic will**, a post-Fall condition.
    - Al-Farabi rationalised **prophecy** through a perfected imagination; the difference is the role of imagination and prophecy.

## 8. Notes

*Mine.*

- **Logos and tropos** is probably the single most useful idea here: it explains theosis without pantheism (nature remains, mode changes), and the Incarnation without confusion.
- The **genesis–kinesis–stasis** reversal shows how the Fathers used Platonic and Aristotelian material but changed the story: motion is good, the end is ahead, and the body is included. See [[Christian Platonism]].
- The **gnomic will** idea answers the "free will = ability to choose evil" assumption: the saints and Christ are **more** free, not less, for not wavering.
- Read with Lossky ch. 7 and Bradshaw ch. 8.
