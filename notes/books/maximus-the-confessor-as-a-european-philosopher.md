St. Maximus (c. 580–662) is often called the greatest Byzantine theologian. This collection argues he's also a major **philosopher**, with an original metaphysics that offers an alternative to the main line of Western thought. Four parts: ontology, epistemology, anthropology, and Maximus in dialogue.

## 1. Life

- Possibly a civil servant in Constantinople (the Greek *Life*), or a Palestinian monk (a hostile Syriac *Life*). Became a monk; lived in North Africa.
- Opposed **monoenergism** and **monothelitism** (one energy, one will in Christ), the imperial compromise to reconcile the Miaphysites.
- Arrested, tried, his **tongue cut out and right hand cut off** so he couldn't speak or write; exiled to Georgia, died 662. Hence "Confessor".
- Vindicated at the **Sixth Ecumenical Council** (680–681): Christ has two natural wills and two energies. See [[Canons of the Sixth Ecumenical Council]].
- Main works: *Ambigua* (difficulties in Gregory Nazianzen and Dionysius), *Questions to Thalassius*, *Mystagogy*, *Four Hundred Chapters on Love*, *Disputation with Pyrrhus*.

## 2. The Logoi

- The **Logos** (the Word) contains the **logoi** (principles, reasons, "words") of all created things: God's will and intention for each thing.
- "The one Logos is many logoi, and the many logoi are one Logos." The one and the many reconciled in the Word. See [[The Problem of the One and the Many]].
- The logoi aren't Platonic Forms existing on their own, but **God's wills**, freely chosen. Each creature's logos is its true nature and its end.
- Knowing creation truly (*natural contemplation*, *theoria physike*) means seeing the logoi in things: the world as a book of the Word.

## 3. Motion: Genesis, Movement, Rest (Part I)

- Against **Origenism**, which taught: original rest (*stasis*) of minds in God → movement (*kinēsis*) as a fall → becoming (*genesis*) into bodies. Motion is a fall from unity.
- Maximus reverses the triad: **genesis → kinesis → stasis**. Creatures are made, then **move** by nature toward God, then come to **rest** in Him. Motion isn't sin; it's the natural desire of a creature for its end.
- Aristotle's influence: motion as actualising potency (Mantzanas's chapter). See [[Aristotle's Physics: Nature, Change and the Four Causes]].
- The end is **"ever-moving rest"** (*stasis aeikinētos*): rest that isn't stillness but endless movement in God. Like Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis*.
- Skliris's "three antinomies": **eschatological teleology** (the end comes from the future, God's goal, not from the past); **free dialectic** (the dialectic of being is fulfilled freely, not by necessity, unlike Hegel); **metaphysics of the resurrection** (the final state is bodily and personal).
- Manoussakis puts Maximus between **Anaxagoras** (Mind moving things) and **Kierkegaard** (movement of the self toward God).

## 4. Holomerism (Tollefsen)

- The relation of **whole and parts** runs through everything: the universal and the particulars, the Church and the faithful, Christ and the members.
- The whole is in each part without being divided, and each part is in the whole. A metaphysics where unity doesn't swallow difference.

## 5. Language and Apophasis (Part II)

- Constas: Scripture and the world are both **words** of the Word; language is a mode of incarnation.
- Wood: Christ is "**both mere man and naked God**": the Incarnation itself is the logic of apophasis: God is revealed **in** hiddenness, most hidden when most revealed.
- See [[Apophatic and Cataphatic Theology]].

## 6. Person, Will and Nature (Part III)

**Logos and tropos** (Louth)
- Every nature has a **logos of being** (what it is, fixed) and a **tropos of existence** (how it exists, its mode). Change and newness happen at the level of **mode**, not nature.
- In Christ, human nature is unchanged in its **logos**, but exists in a new **mode**: united to the divine Person. That's how deification works: our nature isn't changed into another nature; its **mode** is transformed.
- The person is where the tropos lives: the ground of a later ontology of the person. See [[Nature and Person Distinction in Christology]], [[Action Is Not Proper to the Person]].

**The natural and gnomic will**
- **Natural will** (*thelēma physikon*): the nature's inborn desire for its good, for being, for God. Christ has a human natural will.
- **Gnomic will** (*gnōmē*): deliberation, hesitating between options: the will of a person who doesn't yet clearly see the good. It comes from ignorance after the Fall.
- Christ has **no gnomic will**: not because He lacks a human will but because He never wavers. So Christ's human will freely and naturally follows the divine.
- Freedom in the highest sense is **not choice between options** but freely willing the good without hesitation. Harper's chapter compares this to modern **compatibilism**.
- Gethsemane: "not as I will, but as thou wilt" (Matt 26:39) shows two wills, the human freely aligning with the divine. See [[Synergy: Grace and Free Will]].

**Man as mediator** (Tanev, Kochaczyk-Bonińska)
- Five divisions: uncreated/created, intelligible/sensible, heaven/earth, paradise/inhabited world, male/female. Man was called to unite them; Christ does it, starting from the last (born of a virgin) to the first (uniting creation to God).
- Man as **co-creator** and priest of creation. See [[Image and Likeness]].

## 7. In Dialogue (Part IV)

- **Loudovikos**: "analogical ecstasis": Maximus compared with Plotinus, Heidegger and Lacan on desire and the self going out of itself.
- **Coyle**: Maximian echoes in Bonaventure's exemplarism.
- **Cvetković**: God's oneness as a **unity of persons**.
- **Steiris**: Maximus and al-Farabi on imagination.

## 8. Notes

*Mine.*

- **Logos and tropos** is probably the single most useful idea here: it explains theosis without pantheism (nature remains, mode changes), and the Incarnation without confusion.
- The **genesis–kinesis–stasis** reversal shows how the Fathers used Platonic and Aristotelian material but changed the story: motion is good, the end is ahead, and the body is included. See [[Christian Platonism]].
- The **gnomic will** idea answers the "free will = ability to choose evil" assumption: the saints and Christ are **more** free, not less, for not wavering.
- Read with Lossky ch. 7 and Bradshaw ch. 8.
