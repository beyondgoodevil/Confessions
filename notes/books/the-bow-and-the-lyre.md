This is the late Odyssey book by Seth Benardete, and it answers his first book on the *Iliad* ([[Achilles and Hector: The Homeric Hero]]). The title comes from the moment Odysseus strings his bow "as a man skilled in the lyre" strings a new peg and plucks it so it "sings" (Od. 21.406–11). The bow is action and killing, and the lyre is song and thought. The question is whether they can be one. The book's larger question is in the preface: **did Homer already do what Plato did?** If so, the "ancient quarrel between poetry and philosophy" would look very different. Benardete reads the *Odyssey* almost line by line. I've followed his chapters, because the argument only works in sequence. Here's how it goes:

### 1. The Preface: Did the Poets Get There First?

- For forty years Benardete had used Plato as "a map or grid" for reading Homer, Aeschylus and Sophocles: Plato "seemed to me to have given the arguments for what Homer, Aeschylus, and Sophocles had only shown. **Logos, one might say, opened the way to the understanding of paradigm**" (p. xi).
- He assumed the map came first. But "an occasional hit can well be artless, but a pattern of success makes one suspect that the dice are loaded." Maybe Plato learned from the poets. His teacher **Leo Strauss** had often suggested this, and "this book... was written to explore the possibility he indicated" (p. xiv).
- **The key idea is Socrates' "second sailing"**: in the *Phaedo*, Socrates gives up looking at the beings directly and turns to *logoi*, speeches. Greek poetry rests on telling "lies like the truth" (Hesiod's Muses, Od. 19.203). You can't lie knowingly unless you know the truth. So the poets may have taken the indirect route before Socrates did.
- **The example**: Odysseus' lying tale to Penelope as a Cretan (19.171–202), followed by Homer's own simile: her cheeks "melted" with tears like snow on the mountains (19.203–8).
  - Homer's lie is in the **speech**: a cheek isn't snow. Odysseus' lie is in the **speaker**: he's himself and someone else.
  - "The poet, then, **divides what is necessarily one and unites what is necessarily two**" (p. xiii). Impersonation makes one into two, and metaphor makes two into one. This is **poetic dialectic**, and the plot of a poem is "the disclosure of impossibilities or apparent impossibilities".
  - Oedipus' name means both knowledge and lameness. Odysseus has two names, "No one" (which he gives himself) and "Odysseus" (which his grandfather gave him), and "the plot of the Odyssey connects them".
- **The gods are the peak of poetic dialectic.** When Athena pulls Achilles' hair, is that his second thoughts made into a separate being? The gods are "both the spurious other of a one and the spurious one of a two".

### 2. Theodicy, Politics, Telemachus (ch. 1, pp. 1–16)

**Theodicy.**
- The *Odyssey* is **harsher than the *Iliad***. Nobody in the *Iliad* dies in pain or gets eaten by dogs; in the *Odyssey* Eurymachus feels pain as he dies, and Melanthius is cut up and fed to the dogs. Telemachus refuses the twelve slave girls "a clean death". The *Iliad* "consoles us more by what it hides".
- **Two choices.** Achilles chooses glory over going home; Odysseus chooses home over Calypso's immortality. Since glory is "a weaker version" of what Odysseus refused, Achilles would have taken Calypso's offer, and Odysseus would have sailed home from Troy. In Hades, Achilles seems to tell Odysseus he chose wrong.
- **Anonymity and mind.** The *Iliad* begins with Achilles' name and father; the *Odyssey* begins with "the man" who "saw the cities of many men, and came to know their mind". Odysseus' experiences are his genealogy. His heart laughs at the pun of *outis* ("No one") and *mētis* ("mind"): he is both. But his first story (the Lotus-eaters) affirms **memory**, the opposite of anonymous mind. So "**Odysseus chooses to be human and remain incomplete**" (p. 4), caught between a return and a second departure.
- **Philosopher-kings.** Odysseus' old gentle rule was a coincidence of power and wisdom. Now his wisdom includes "the knowledge of nature" (10.303), and he restores his rule by terror. "**Homer seems to have reflected on the Platonic possibility of philosopher-kings**, and either condemned it in advance of its explicit formulation or confirmed the necessity that its realization remain a devout wish" (p. 4).
- **Homer vs the Muse.** Homer's proem blames the men's own folly for eating the Sun's cattle; that's a **cosmic** god punishing injustice. The Muse then starts the story with the **Olympians**, Poseidon, Zeus and Athena. Odysseus, in choosing home, "chooses the Olympian gods".
- **Zeus' complaint** (1.32–43): men blame the gods for their evils, but they suffer "beyond their fate" through their own recklessness. Zeus picks the odd example of Aegisthus, who was warned. The poem confirms the first half of the complaint absolutely: almost everyone blames Zeus. The whole *Odyssey* becomes "a knockdown proof of Olympian theodicy", ending with Laertes' cry that the gods still exist (24.351). (See [[The Problem of Evil and Theodicy]].)

**Politics.**
- Odysseus knew he would return alone, and Poseidon's anger is used up in a single storm. So why the extra seven years? The timing serves **Telemachus**: he must grow up and feel his position. "**The Odyssey serves the Telemacheia**", not the other way round (p. 8).
- The suitors are "a bunch of bumbling braggarts". The real issue is **succession**. Penelope's twenty-year strategy was to dazzle the suitors so that it looked as if the household, not the city, were at stake. Telemachus doesn't know this and resents his mother.
- **Ithaca has lost a generation.** Everyone who went with Odysseus died, and no assembly has met for twenty years. Odysseus' rule was so good it "could seal up Ithaca"; gratitude doesn't survive a generation. The suitors, whose older brothers died with Odysseus, "contain in themselves the general disaffection of the people".
- Benardete compares Plato's *Laws*: three judges of quarrelling brothers. One kills the bad, one makes the good rule the bad, and one reconciles them all by law. Odysseus does the first, and the gods force the third on him (24.541–48).
- **Eupeithes' charge** is unanswerable: "First, he went and lost his ships and his people, and then he returned and killed the best of the Cephallenians" (24.427–29). The seven years with Calypso may have saved Odysseus from his own people.

**Telemachus.**
- Athena comes as "Mentes" at the moment Telemachus is imagining his father, and leaves her spear among Odysseus' weapons; "imagination and sight seem to merge".
- She sees right through the suitors' hubris before we see any evidence. That transparency could encourage men to fuse imagination and evidence "in becoming a terrifying certainty". When Odysseus later begs among them to tell the just from the lawless, almost all of them turn out decent enough except Antinous.
- In her story, Odysseus once went to Ephyra for **arrow poison**, and he never took his bow to war (21.38–41). "**We really are in a postheroic world**" (p. 13). In Ithaca neither Odysseus nor Telemachus is called a hero.
- Telemachus learns from Athena that "no glory is attached to the reacquisition of one's property". He defends Phemius' sad song against his mother because he wants to be the subject of the next song.
- **The singers are responsible.** Zeus' complaint may be aimed above all at the singers, "who establish the models for understanding divine agency". Mentor wishes that no king will ever be gentle again. "He gets his wish."

### 3. Pattern and Will: Nestor, Helen and Menelaus (ch. 2, pp. 17–31)

**Nestor.**
- Pylos is the only palace **without a singer**. No one weeps there. Nestor's account of the returns from Troy is a **strict theodicy**: the gods sorted the just from the unjust, and his own safe return proves his wisdom and justice. His accounts have **no speeches of others and no similes**.
- **Why similes matter.** Benardete uses Horace's Cleopatra ode, where a simile turns sympathy from victor to victim. A simile is "a two-way carrier of meaning". When Menelaus compares the suitors to fawns left in a lion's lair, he undercuts his own point. "The two ways poetry has of imitation, impersonation and image making, virtually preclude the possibility of poetry's adoption of a strict morality." Poetry gives "a perspective beyond good and evil".
- **Nestor's second account** gives him away. Read in order, the key event is Menelaus delayed at Sunium by **piety**, burying his helmsman. "Not those who stayed behind with Agamemnon but those who abandoned him sealed his fate." Telemachus falls silent and never speaks again at Pylos.
- Athena turns from manifest at Troy to "vivid" at Pylos to someone who arrives at the sacrifice "in just the way anyone else does". In the end she is only "a voice".

**Helen and Menelaus.**
- The theme of Sparta is **appearance**. Menelaus resents the gap between mortal and immortal possessions. He casually admits he'd have depopulated one of his own cities to resettle Odysseus near him: the perfect proof of Zeus' complaint.
- **Helen's drug.** It takes away all grief, so that you wouldn't weep that day even if your parents died or your son were killed before your eyes. "There is perhaps in the entire Odyssey no single action that shows such a deep and immediate understanding of human things as **Helen's drugging of everyone**" (p. 27). It mimics Odysseus' real toughness and does what the Muses do in Hesiod: it makes men forget their sorrows.
- **Helen as poet.** She saw through Odysseus' beggar disguise in Troy. She "seems to have all the traits of the poet": coolness, vividness and "insight into the hiddenness of things". Her only rival is Odysseus, whose eyes stay like horn while he pities Penelope.
- **Menelaus and Proteus.** Proteus changes into everything, "all of becoming is nothing but appearance", yet he can't see through a sealskin. "Becoming is an illusion that the will by itself can overcome." Menelaus is promised Elysium. So Sparta offers two disjunctions, **being/becoming** and **reality/appearance**. Odysseus' wisdom "must be of a different order if Odysseus rejects the immortality Menelaus is granted".

### 4. Odysseus' Choice (ch. 3, pp. 33–44)

- **Calypso's island** is paradise: trees, birds, springs, cedar smell and a goddess singing. Odysseus sits weeping, looking at the sea. "**To look at paradise and to be in paradise are altogether different**"; "there seems to be no place for man" (p. 35). Hermes notes there are no sacrifices there: no city, no gods. Maybe what made it pall was "the absence of mind".
- **Three readings of the seven years:**
  1. **Empty time and punishment.** The Sun's 350 cows and 350 sheep stand for the days and nights of a year (Aristotle), and Odysseus has to make up the "rent in time".
  2. **A cooling-off period** for the Ithacans' resentment.
  3. **A penitentiary**, a *sōphronistērion* (Plato's word), to teach him that man should be blind and not wander to see for himself. "'Calypso' means 'Concealer.' **It looks like an anagram of 'Cyclops'**" (p. 37).
- **Odysseus is never compared to anyone.** Telemachus is compared to Orestes and Penelope to the heroines of old, but no one ever matches Odysseus with a figure of the past, and he never cites one. Two experiences define him and can't be expressed: the seven hidden years and the future journey inland.
- **Homer on poetry.** Steering by the stars, Odysseus watches the Bear, "which they also call the Wagon; she turns in place and keeps a sharp lookout for Orion" (5.272–7). There's a neutral account (a name) and an Olympian account (a bear literally watching a hunter). Weaken one and you weaken the other, and "a possible truth" becomes "an image".
- **Soliloquies.** There are six in Book 5, and only four in the rest of the poem. The split between a man and his heart (*thumos*) becomes crucial for Plato's account of the soul.
- **The storm speech.** Odysseus wishes he'd died at Troy fighting over Achilles' corpse, so that he'd have had burial and glory (5.299–312). He throws away his whole life for "the emptiest day... in the emptiest time of his life". Yet he knows from Hades that glory is nothing. "**In choosing the uncertainty of mortality, Odysseus chooses self-opacity**" (p. 40). He "chooses the human that does not exist at all if there are not gods": burial is at the centre of his choice.
- **Ino and compassion.** Ino was a mortal who became a goddess, and her pity comes from her former humanity. Odysseus pleads for pity everywhere until he gets home, and never once after: "the very first inference we can draw from Odysseus's rejection of immortality is his rejection of compassion".
- **The two extremes.** His last choice before sleeping is to go inland and risk **being eaten by beasts** rather than freeze on the beach. So within his choice of the human there is "only the divine and the bestial... **There is no third**" (p. 43).
- **Name and scar.** The thicket he crawls into is described in the same lines as the boar's lair where he got his scar (5.476–82; 19.440–2). His name comes from *odussomai*, "to be angry at" or "to be hated by". Unlike Achilles' wrath (*mēnis*), "the anger of Odysseus... is inseparable from who he is".

### 5. Among the Phaeacians (ch. 4, pp. 45–61)

**Shame.**
- The Phaeacians are a city less than a generation old. They fled from the Cyclopes and lost their fighting spirit almost overnight. Their wall has no guards, and they have no dealings with anyone: "without error or suffering". (Benardete links them to Corcyra in Thucydides.) Zeus' task for Odysseus is to **turn them from reliance on the gods to concern for a human being**. "Odysseus single-handedly converts the Phaeacians to the human."
- **The lion with a loincloth.** Homer compares the naked Odysseus approaching Nausicaa to a starving lion, but Odysseus covers his genitals with a branch. "**He is a lion with a loincloth**... The defective shows itself in hiding itself. **Shame is a transparent concealment**" (p. 47).
  - The same word *mēdea* means "understanding" at 6.12 and "genitals" at 6.219.
  - Something has to cover our incompleteness without making us complete. That something, as Herodotus suggests, is **law**.
- **Gods visible in speech.** Homer says Nausicaa is like Artemis "as if we had seen Artemis". The Olympians are always invisible but "partake of visibility through speech". Demodocus is blind.

**Paradise.**
- Alcinous' palace has **gold and silver dogs** made by Hephaistos, "deathless and ageless", and an orchard where fruit never fails. These are the two forms of what Odysseus rejected: "deathlessness means either intelligent artifacts or perpetual becoming without decay". He sees the evidence for his choice **after** making it. That's the right order, as the *Sophist* hints: "otherwise time would be superfluous".
- **"We human beings."** Covering for Nausicaa, Odysseus says "we human beings are very resentful and censorious" (*dusēloi*, 7.307). It's the only time in Homer anyone says "we human beings". His own name means "hated / angry", so "**Every man is and is not Odysseus**" (p. 52).

**Pride.**
- Odysseus tempts Alcinous with **glory**: send me home and you'll be famous. The Phaeacians have epithets for everything, but nobody has heard of them.
- **Demodocus' three songs.** Odysseus weeps at the first (the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles), enjoys the second (Ares and Aphrodite), and weeps openly at the third (the wooden horse), which he requested. Among the Phaeacians "he is the Trojan horse", and Homer calls him "sacker of cities" for the first time (8.3).
- **Insult.** Euryalus mocks him as a trader, "mindful of freight", and Odysseus answers with a speech on **charm in speech** versus empty good looks, then throws the discus. "Insult establishes the absolute measure of wrong". Alcinous backs down and suggests gifts, as Poseidon did in the song.
- **Ares and Aphrodite** is the *Iliad* in miniature: adultery and a husband's right, art beating speed. The gods laugh twice, first at right triumphing over might, then at Hermes saying he'd gladly be caught with Aphrodite. Natural right (Eros) against legal right. Hephaistos isn't laughing: in effect he wishes he'd never been born.
- **The tears simile.** At the horse song Odysseus weeps "as a wife weeps over her husband fallen before his city" while the enemy drive her off into slavery (8.523–31). The victor weeps like the victim. "**He has beaten everything and everyone, but for what?**" (p. 58).
- **"Tell me your name."** Alcinous asks for his name, since no one is nameless. "What impresses Alcinous about Odysseus is that he is a nobody who both claims to be a somebody and has convinced them that he is."

### 6. Odysseus' Own Story (ch. 5, pp. 63–101)

Odysseus has had seven years to shape the story; it's "his understanding of his experience". He tells nine episodes, with Circe at the centre. The first four are told without foreknowledge; for the last four he has been told in advance what will happen. "**Everything seems real in Odysseus's lies, and nothing seems real when he is telling the truth**." Benardete compares Socrates' "goat-stag" images in the *Republic*: "The impossible is the sole access into the unprecedented" (p. 64).

**Memory and Mind.**
- **"Beautiful" and "sweet."** Listening to a singer is the most beautiful thing, and seeing your country is the sweetest (9.2–36). Odysseus mentions his parents but **not Penelope**.
- **Ismaros.** He sacks the Cicones' city, loses control of his men, and blames Zeus, "exactly what Zeus complained men always do".
- **Lotus-eaters, then Cyclops.** First he chooses **memory** and tradition, dragging his men away by force; then he discovers anonymous **mind**. "He thus makes tradition the condition for mind." In Platonic terms: "**One can go into the light of the sun only if one goes first into the twilight of the Cave**" (p. 66). The story is like "an exploded diagram" of Odysseus. Behind it is one question: "**What was the good he found in the bad?**" (p. 67)
- **Curiosity.** The goat island already told him the Cyclopes have no ships or arts, and he brings Maron's strong wine because he suspected a lawless giant. So why go? Out of **disinterested curiosity** about whether men can be just without the city, "to run risks for knowledge". Homer imagines a Herodotus before there was one. (Zenodotus' variant reading, *nomos* "law" for *noos* "mind" in line 1.3, "does not have to be true in order to be true.")
- **Order without justice.** Inside the cave everything is in perfect order, with the lambs sorted by age and the pails swimming with whey. Odysseus assumes order means justice, and learns "that **law and order can be apart**". "**Cannibalism is the strict consequence of Cyclopean tidiness**" (p. 74). It's the only mistake Odysseus ever admits: he should have left.
- **The sword.** Odysseus is about to stab the Cyclops when "another *thumos*" checks him: they'd be trapped behind the stone. Compare Achilles reaching for his sword and Athena pulling his hair: Achilles needs the gods to stop him, while Odysseus' second thoughts seem his own. "Necessity overwhelms justice."
- **The pun works only through a question.** The Cyclopes ask "Surely no one (*mē tis*) is killing you?" and Polyphemus answers "No one (*Outis*) is killing me." The link between *outis* and *mētis* appears only in a question expecting "no" and a grammatically irregular condition. "'No one' names the other." Then **anger** reasserts itself: Odysseus shouts his real name and "sacker of cities". The Cyclopes now know man is dangerous, and Polyphemus prays to his father, so Odysseus' pride extends the reach of the Olympians.

**Nature.**
- **Aeolus** rules a "city" that is one incestuous family. Odysseus steers for nine days without telling his men about the bag of winds and curses **sleep**: "Mind fused with will dispenses with the body." Aeolus throws him out because a failure with perfect means proves divine hatred. "To be fallible is the greatest disgrace to life." From here they must row, the proverbial **"second sailing"**: "the feasible replaces the ideal."
- **The Laestrygonians** are a city with an assembly and a king, and worse than the Cyclops. Odysseus narrates it neutrally and moored outside without warning his men. Benardete suspects he blamed Zeus "to cover up his own responsibility", "and in a terrible way, Zeus is once more proved right".
- **The turn.** After the disaster Odysseus calls his men "friends" for the first time, shares out the stag, divides command, draws lots, and names his men (Eurylochus, Polites "Citizen", Elpenor). Then he goes alone to rescue his men from Circe, despite Eurylochus' refusal: "the first time Odysseus does anything that does not involve his own advantage".
- **The moly.** "This completely just and daring action... is instantly rewarded. **Hermes reveals to him that things have natures**. From every point of view... this revelation is **the peak of the Odyssey**" (p. 84). Hermes "showed me its nature (*phusis*)" (10.303), the only time the word appears in Homer. In McCrorie's version, "although the roots were black its blossom was milklike. / *Molu* the Gods call it, hard for a death-bound / man to uproot".
  - What saves him is not a magic herb but **knowledge**: root and flower belong together despite contrary colours. Circe's pigs keep a human mind (*noos*) in a pig's body. Odysseus learns that mind and shape belong together, so Menelaus' world of pure becoming "must have been an illusion".
  - Benardete links this to Heraclitus (day and night are one; the way up and down are one) and the *kosmos*: "**Homer was the first, as far as we know, to have come to an understanding of this philosophic principle, to which he gave the name 'nature'**" (p. 87).
  - So Calypso's offer "cannot be genuine": he couldn't survive being made deathless and still be himself. Circe, who knows, doesn't offer it.
  - He compares Aristotle's *Politics*: man as rational and political, but needing law against his bestiality, with cannibalism and incest in political and non-political settings.
- **A god learns from a man.** In Circe's bed Odysseus refuses to eat until his men are freed. Circe doesn't understand; to her the men are livestock. Only when she sees them weeping and clasping his hands does she pity them. "This is an extraordinary thing; **a god learns from a man**" (p. 89).

**Hades.**
- Hearing he must go to Hades, Odysseus wants to die. Elpenor's is the first soul to "go down to Hades" in the poem. Odysseus seems to think the soul is just breath. Hades separates mind and shape (the soul keeps the looks, the mind vanishes), and it serves as a "lawful equivalent" of the knowledge of nature: it forbids man to be eaten by beasts, and so requires burial.
- **The oar and the winnowing fan.** Teiresias' prophecy:
  - Odysseus will walk inland with an oar until someone takes it for a winnowing fan. McCrorie: "you're holding a winnowing tool on your bright arm, / then you should drive the well-made oar in the ground there", and sacrifice to Poseidon (11.121–31).
  - He will establish a god among people who can't see what the god rules. "The oar will be a sign with a displaced significance", like "No one".
  - So Odysseus is destined "to extend the Olympian gods into a region where the sign of their being will cease to point to their being".
- **The suitors.** They "eat up your livelihood" (*biotos*, which can also mean "life"). Odysseus must take it literally to take out on them what he couldn't on Polyphemus. The suitors never say *thnētoi*, "mortals", and threaten people with the bogeyman King Echetus instead of Hades: they don't believe in Hades.
- **Achilles, Heracles and the low point.** Odysseus can't embrace his mother. "Hades is a place of eternal frustration": Agamemnon, Achilles and Ajax still nurse their grievances. Odysseus' choice "now appears to have been of neither home nor mortality but of Hades".
  - Heracles' ghost is indistinguishable from the pictures on his belt, while "Heracles himself" is among the gods. Heracles killed Odysseus' guest-friend Iphitus, who gave him the bow.
  - "**The gods reward injustice with immortality**... This is the low point of Odysseus's narrative" (p. 98).

**Destiny.**
- Neither Teiresias nor Circe mentions **Calypso**. She is "the personification... of the difference between story and life", "the lie of poetry" and perhaps "the lie in the soul".
- **The Sirens** offer to tell him everything that happened at Troy and everything on earth. Odysseus can resist immortality but not omniscience: "his **strongest and deepest desire is not for home but for knowledge**... Justice is not at the heart of his nature" (p. 99). "He is going to die not knowing" (p. 100).
- **Three limits**: he can't know everything (the Sirens), he can't beat evil (he arms uselessly against Scylla), and he can't overrule the majority (Eurylochus at Thrinacia).
- **The Sun's cattle.** His starving men are in the end "helpless and innocent". The Sun threatens to shine in Hades, so Zeus destroys the ship.

### 7. Odysseus' Lies (ch. 6, pp. 103–16)

- **Death and rebirth.** The Phaeacian ship carries him home asleep, "most like death". He wakes not recognising Ithaca and takes a stranger's word that he's home: "hearsay thus replaces memory". He also forgets his scar.
- **Odysseus and Oedipus.** Benardete compares Oedipus: two names, knowledge and anger. Oedipus "could figure out man in time" but not birth and death, "at which the sacred... intrudes". Odysseus too must fit "the knower" into "the king", by force.
- **The stone ship.** Zeus lets Poseidon turn the Phaeacian ship to stone as a permanent warning. "The essence of this new relation will be one of terror" (p. 105). The Phaeacians are punished not for any injustice but because "they put man ahead of god". This points to the suitors: "The signs of the gods have become as hidden as the gods themselves."
- **Herodotus' Helen.** The Egyptian story says Helen was in Egypt all along and the Trojans couldn't give her back. Yet Troy was destroyed "since the divine was arranging... that it become manifest to men that for acts of great injustice the punishment of the gods is equally great" (Hdt. 2.120). Killing Antinous alone wouldn't do; the principle "Fear the gods" needs a big demonstration and a poet to spread it. "**The Odyssey displays Homer's self-censorship**" (p. 107): a poem written to make Zeus look kindly on singers.
- **The first lie** (to Athena as a shepherd) is about a Cretan who killed Idomeneus' son over booty, refusing to "gratify" Idomeneus. It's the *Iliad* rewritten with Achilles winning, and the Trojan War as "a drawn-out pirate raid". "Odysseus through his lies emerges as a free man, someone whom we could imagine choosing another life."
- **The belly.** After Antinous hits him with a stool, Odysseus says there's no grief in being hit over property, but Antinous struck him "on account of my wretched belly" (17.470–4). His **dignity**, not his property, is what's at stake. Benardete compares Glaucon refusing the "city of pigs". A suitor says the beggar might be a god "surveying the hubris and the lawabidingness of human beings". "The highest of the high has taken on the guise of the lowest of the low in order to vindicate human dignity as such."
- **Unforgiving justice.** The slave girls slept with the suitors after three years, and most never knew Odysseus. "Justice, if it is not confined to broad daylight but seeks instead to comprehend the secrets of the dark, is of necessity unforgiving" (p. 110).
- **The Cretan tale to Eumaeus** combines "Helen" (insight into hearts and intentions) with "Nestor" (a moral pattern in events). Zeus and "gods" occur eighteen times each. The second tale (the borrowed cloak) shows how divine dreams are invented: "'God' is just a way of hiding from others one's own lack of caution" (p. 116). So there are two Odysseuses: the agent of revenge and the man "who finds it so easy to make up stories about gods".

### 8. Nonfated Things (ch. 7, pp. 117–30)

- **Theoclymenus.** His name means "he who hearkens to gods". Only we know who he is, and if you cut him out nothing changes. His prophecy to the suitors is "quasi-Biblical": "the walls and beams are sprinkled with blood... the sun has been lost from the sky" (20.351–7). It's the only time *noeō* ("perceive") is used of the future. He "embodies the future rule of the prophet, who will be the sole intermediary between gods and men once the gods have completed their withdrawal". "**He is a trace of providence**" (p. 120).
- **Eumaeus.** Homer addresses him directly fifteen times, as he does Patroclus in the *Iliad*. In Plato's *Statesman* the swineherd keeps pace with the king almost to the last cut. Odysseus promises him and Philoitius wives, wealth and houses next to his: "Odysseus democratizes the regime". The price is **Melanthius**, tortured and fed to the dogs, which breaks the principle behind burial, "hatred is to stop with death". "**The Odyssey is primarily for him**" (p. 124).
- **Two kinds of killing.** The suitors are simply killed, with indignity only in Homer's simile of gasping fish. The slaves are punished in kind. "It is only on the level of a story that the suitors are punished."
- **The slave girls and the bitch simile.** Seeing the maids going laughing to the suitors, his heart "howls" like a bitch guarding pups from a man she doesn't recognise. He tells his heart to endure, as it did in the Cyclops' cave (20.17–21).
  - Is the killing then cold calculation (necessity, avoiding exile)? Or anger posing as reason?
  - Benardete leaves both open. Homer's splitting of Odysseus into heart and mind shows "**the unblinking gaze of Homer**, who would in this way be distinguishing the bow from the lyre" (p. 126).
- **Melanthius and Irus.** Odysseus considers killing Melanthius for a kick, and considers killing the beggar Irus. "I am the best inferior there ever was." "**No body (*outidanos*) is No one (*outis*)**" (p. 128).
- **The scar.** Odysseus takes after his grandfather **Autolycus**, the thief and oath-twister to whom Hermes gave his gifts. Autolycus named him "Odysseus", as one hated by many. So what Odysseus made of himself falls away "and what he was from the start reemerges and confirms his name as his fate". This makes Eumaeus, who knew him as a boy, "the most appropriate audience".

### 9. The Suitors and the City (ch. 8, pp. 131–41)

- **The suitors' crime doesn't add up.** Homer's justification is that "they were the first to contrive unseemly deeds" (20.394). But wooing a presumed widow isn't a crime, and they plot against Telemachus only after he threatens them, a threat Athena put in his head.
  - "**The ideality of right is experientially the bestial**" (p. 131): killing is repeatedly assimilated to eating.
  - The suitors are also absurd: whoever wins Penelope leaves the palace and still has to kill Telemachus. "The wooing of Penelope is a Sisyphean labor." They would have done better to form an oligarchy and ignore her. Penelope's distraction of them is "the highest achievement of enchantment that can possibly be imagined".
- **Two possible solutions.** Either Odysseus is subordinated to a divine plan (the withdrawal of the gods and a new piety), or Odysseus is central and finds "a new kind of justice... no longer dependent on... piety". He kills the priest Leodes, the one suitor Homer calls just. "In the Odyssey, the just is no longer beautiful": "**The Odyssey demythologizes the Iliad**" (p. 134). Penelope is no Helen, and Odysseus has split Ares from Aphrodite.
- **"I am not any god."** Odysseus says to Telemachus *ou tis toi theos eimi*, "I am not any god, but your father" (16.187). It can be heard as "I am the god No one."
- **After the killing**, Odysseus makes no plans for Penelope or the town and goes on "a murderous rampage" until Zeus and Athena stop him. The town assembly settles it:
  - **Eupeithes** makes the case for vengeance and is met with universal pity.
  - **Medon**, who hid under a chair, claims he saw a god like Mentor beside Odysseus. Nothing in Homer's narrative matches. This fusion of opinion and sight is Plato's *phantasia*: "**It is the highest achievement of the Cave**" (p. 139). Pity is driven out by fear.
  - **Halitherses** adds guilt: "These things occurred, friends, by your own baseness (*kakotēs*)." Before this, *kakotēs* always meant misery that happens to you; now it is internalised.
  - "**It brings an end to Zeus's complaint**, with which the Odyssey began" (p. 139). It also explains how the gods can withdraw and still uphold right.
- **The settlement.** Zeus' speech on the oath is grammatically confused: it ends with the gods, not the Ithacans, swearing. He promises a forgetting stronger than Helen's drug. The political solution consists of "two kinds of divine terror, one by hearsay, the other manifest": guilt for the unarmed, law for those who must be disarmed. "Despite what Zeus says, Ithaca will never be as it was."

### 10. Recognitions (ch. 9, pp. 143–52)

- **Penelope** can't identify the killer with her husband: a god could have killed the suitors, and a man could have deceived her. Athena restores his youthful beauty, which makes recognising the **real** (aged) Odysseus impossible.
  - She tests him with the bed, and what convinces her is not his craft but his **anger**.
  - She explains herself by defending **Helen**: she wouldn't have gone had she known the consequences. Radical distrust is the only defence against seductive speech, and "the most seductive speeches she ever heard were his".
  - She accepts him once she learns he must leave again. His last order to her is "Do not look at anyone, and do not ask any questions", the same words Athena used about the Phaeacians.
- **Homer restores Hades** (Book 24). Benardete compares the *Republic*: Socrates first censors Achilles' line "I'd rather be a serf" and later gives it to the man who has climbed out of the Cave.
  - Odysseus had accepted death "while rejecting the law and its meaning, for which Hades can be said to stand". So Homer repairs it with **burial and song**.
  - Achilles' funeral, with the Muses' dirge, games and tomb, shows how "Hades consoles in the face of the impossible".
  - The suitors join the heroes; they are "the last vestiges of the heroic world".
  - Amphimedon tells "a neater story" in which Penelope planned everything. Agamemnon praises Penelope and predicts a song of praise for her and a hateful one for Clytaemestra. "The Odyssey seems to comprise the two songs."
- **Laertes.** Odysseus tests his old father cruelly and stops only when Laertes pours ashes on his head. He wants to be known "as he is" and to be genuinely missed, "doglike devotion" like Argus'. But "loyalty and knowledge are as far apart from one another as the unquestioned is from the result of questioning". Laertes needs signs (the scar, the trees) and never acknowledges him as the killer. Last line of the book: "He should now know that **his destiny is to establish belief and not knowledge**" (p. 152).

### 11. Compared With Achilles and Hector

- In 1955 Benardete read the *Iliad* as an experiment in immortality: the hero wants to be a god and ends as a thing, "a work of art". In 1997 the *Odyssey* is about the man who **refuses** that. Odysseus sees the golden dogs and chooses to stay a person, though Benardete is now far less sentimental about what that choice costs: self-opacity, no compassion, terror at home.
- The old book already said that Odysseus refuses immortality "on the very grounds which Achilles would have given for acceptance"; the new one builds a whole reading on it.
- The method has changed too. In 1985 he admitted he had looked at epithets instead of "the larger units of action"; here it's all action and the order of telling. That's the "eidetic analysis" Michael Davis mentions.

### 12. Assessment

- **Strengths.**
  - There's no other reading of the *Odyssey* that takes **Odysseus' own narration** so seriously as a selected, shaped account, distinct from Homer's.
  - The best things are the close observations: Nestor's lack of similes, the six soliloquies in Book 5, Odysseus pleading for pity everywhere but home, the only "we human beings", the only *phusis*, *kakotēs* turning inward, the pun working only in a question.
  - The thesis that the moly is the philosophical centre (nature as the unity of mind and shape) is the boldest and most persuasive part of the book, and it explains why Calypso's offer can't really tempt him.
  - His refusal to whitewash the ending (the slave girls, Melanthius, the political fragility) is a needed corrective to readings that treat the *Odyssey* as a simple triumph of justice. Martin Sitte's review (*Review of Metaphysics*, 1998) presents it as an introduction to "Homeric dialectic" and to the possibility that the poets foreshadowed Socrates' turn to the human things.
- **Weaknesses.**
  - It's almost **unfalsifiable**. Every coincidence, omission and grammatical oddity carries a meaning, and there's no control. He says himself that "it could seem forced and willful if Plato was always there ahead of me".
  - He hardly engages with oral composition, the analyst/unitarian debate, or archaeology, and there's no secondary literature to speak of.
  - Several readings are clever rather than convincing: Calypso as an anagram of Cyclops, *pukinon* as a joke about a "dense" mind, Zeus' tangled syntax.
  - The **Straussian** frame (an esoteric Homer who sees through the gods and writes a salutary theology of terror for the many) is assumed rather than argued. It turns the poem's piety into "popular rhetoric", which is a big claim to rest on hints.
  - The prose is very dense and aphoristic. The book is about 150 pages but reads like 500.
- **What I take from it.** The *Odyssey* is not a simple story of a good man coming home. It's about what the human is (between beast and god), what can and can't be known, and how a city holds together after a war that killed a generation. And there's a real tension between the man who wants to know (the Sirens, the Cyclops' cave) and the man who must rule (the suitors, the slave girls).

### 13. Orthodox Assessment

- **Zeus' complaint and St James.** The poem starts with Zeus saying men blame the gods for evils they bring on themselves, and Benardete makes its whole plot the answer, ending with the people admitting their own *kakotēs*. Christianity says the same thing more directly: "Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God... But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust" (James 1:13–14). St Basil's homily *That God Is Not the Author of Evil* is a whole sermon on it. But for Benardete, guilt is a political instrument: terror internalised, so that the gods can withdraw. In the Church, admitting guilt is the beginning of **repentance**, a change of mind, not terror (see [[Repentance as Metanoia: A Change of Mind, Not a Payment]]).
- **The gods withdraw, God draws near.** Benardete's *Odyssey* traces the **occlusion of the gods**: manifest at Troy, vivid at Pylos, then a voice, then hearsay, prophets (Theoclymenus) and "belief and not knowledge". Biblical revelation runs the other way: God "who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son" (Heb. 1:1–2). The apostles' witness is not hearsay but "that which we have seen with our eyes... and our hands have handled" (1 John 1:1). Where Homer's gods hide behind disguises, the Incarnation is God making himself seen and touched.
- **Nature, mind and body.** Benardete's best idea, that the moly teaches that **mind and shape belong together**, so that a shade in Hades without mind is not the man and Calypso's deathlessness can't be genuine, is very close to the patristic teaching that man is soul and body together. That's why the Fathers insist on the **resurrection of the body**, not the survival of a ghost or the immortality of a golden statue (Gregory of Nyssa, *On the Soul and the Resurrection*; see [[On the Soul and the Resurrection]]). Odysseus refuses a false immortality; the Gospel offers a true one. And unlike Calypso's offer, deification (*theōsis*) doesn't make man a different kind of thing; it perfects the human (see [[Theosis: Becoming by Grace What God Is by Nature]]).
- **Compassion.** Benardete notes that Odysseus' refusal of immortality goes with a refusal of compassion, and that Ino pities him because she was once human. Circe learns pity from a man. The Christian version is the God who became man so that he could be "touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Heb. 4:15), and the Christian ideal is the opposite of Odysseus at home: mercy to the guilty, not doglike tests of loyalty.
- **The Sirens and the mast.** Benardete finds Odysseus' deepest desire to be knowledge, not home. St Clement of Alexandria read the Sirens episode as a Christian allegory: sail past the Sirens of pleasure bound to the **wood** (the Cross), and you'll come safely to harbour, with the Word as pilot and the Holy Spirit bringing you in (*Exhortation to the Greeks* 12). The Fathers agree with Odysseus that man desires to know, but they place the fulfilment of that desire in knowing God, not in the Sirens' omniscience (see [[How Philosophers Saved Homer: Allegory from the Stoics to the Byzantines]]).
- **Burial and Hades.** Benardete shows how much depends on the law of burial and how Odysseus breaks it with Melanthius. For a Christian reader, the treatment of Melanthius and the slave girls is simply wrong, however necessary politically. Hades as "eternal frustration" is what Christ broke open in his descent (see [[Homer on Life and Death]] and [[The Shield of Achilles: A World on a War Shield]]).
- **A Straussian Homer?** The deepest disagreement is with the framework. If the wise poet secretly knows the gods are a political fiction and writes a "theology" of terror for the many, then religion is noble lying. The Fathers did sometimes treat Homer's gods as human inventions (Athanasius, *Against the Heathen*), but they never thought that true religion could be a tool for rulers. Benardete's Homer is a brilliant reader of the human soul; his "belief and not knowledge" is the opposite of the faith that "seeks understanding", let alone of the vision of God.

### Conclusion

*The Bow and the Lyre* reads the *Odyssey* as a philosophical poem, perhaps Plato's teacher. Odysseus chooses to remain mortal and incomplete, and Homer follows what that choice involves. Mind needs memory and a home before it can be free. Order isn't justice. A city can be worse than a cannibal. And Hermes shows Odysseus that things have natures, the first discovery of "nature". Then Odysseus goes home and has to act as king, not knower. His homecoming is founded on terror, guilt and a "vision" no one saw, and the gods withdraw into hearsay and belief. Benardete doesn't flatter Homer's hero, and he finds in Homer an "unblinking gaze" that sees all this. I'm not convinced by the esoteric frame, but the details stick, and after this book Odysseus' story can't be read as a simple tale of the good man coming home.
