"Physics" means the study of ***physis***, **nature**: things that move and change. Not physics in the modern sense but the philosophy of nature, what any change must involve. It's the foundation for the *Metaphysics* and for Aquinas's proofs, and Feser's whole argument depends on it. Eight books.

The *Critical Guide* (Leunissen) reads it in three blocks: **nature, chance, teleology and art**; **kinēsis** (change, process, motion); and the argument of Book VIII. Names in brackets below (Lennox, Kelsey…) are the authors of its chapters.

## Book I: The Principles of Change

- *Methodos* = a disciplined line of inquiry (Lennox).
- Parmenides said change is impossible: something would have to come from **what is not**. See [[The Presocratics: Being and Becoming]].
- Aristotle: every change involves **three** principles:
    1. a **subject** (*hypokeimenon*) that persists: the **matter**
    2. a **form** acquired
    3. a **privation**: the lack of that form beforehand
- The unmusical man becomes musical: the man persists, musicality is acquired, unmusicality is lost. Nothing comes from sheer nothing; it comes from what is **potentially** so.
- **Substantial change** (a new thing coming to be) needs a subject too: ultimately **prime matter**, pure potentiality.
    - Two requirements on change (Henry, on I.5–7): a **subject** it proceeds from, and something that **survives**.
    - In generation "the subject changes **as a whole**" (Gill); blood in animal generation can't satisfy survival.
    - The Presocratics reduced generation to alteration because they thought all change needs a persisting subject.

## Book II: Nature and the Causes

**Nature** (ch. 1): "a principle of motion and rest in that to which it belongs primarily". Natural things move from **within** (a seed grows, a stone falls); artefacts only from outside (a bed has no inner tendency to become a bed; if you planted one, a tree would come up, not a bed).
- "**Man is born from man, but not bed from bed**" (Antiphon: plant a bed and a tree grows): so form is more nature than matter (Henry).
- The definition is "a kind of instruction for how to interpret the phenomena": a thing's nature shows in what it **does** (Kelsey).
- The physician who is ill and cures himself: the principle is in him, but not **as** patient: art, not nature (Stavrianeas).
- "**If nothing impedes**" means "if nothing **external** impedes" (Stavrianeas).
- Nature is both **matter** and **form**, but more form: a thing is what it is by its form.
    - Form is nature "more than" matter because form is a principle by being an **end** (Kelsey).
    - **Homonymy**: matter surviving a substance's death is "the same only in name" (a dead hand is a hand homonymously) (Stavrianeas).
- Natural things are like the **snub** (concavity in a nose: form in a particular matter), not the **concave** (abstractable like maths). So the natural scientist must know form **and** matter (Lennox).
    - "What is separable and what it is, it is the work of first philosophy to determine" (194b14-15).
    - The "what is it?" question rests on a **causal** inquiry: how ends relate to materials and their movements.

**The four causes** (ch. 3): four answers to "why?"

| Cause | Answers | Example: a house |
|---|---|---|
| **material** | out of what? | bricks, timber |
| **formal** | what is it? its essence, structure | the plan, the form of house |
| **efficient** | from what source of change? | the builder (really, the art of building) |
| **final** | for the sake of what? | shelter |

In nature, the formal, efficient and final often coincide: the form of the oak is what the acorn grows toward, and an oak produced the acorn.
- The student of nature will know **all four causes**; formal and final causes are identified: form is a "moving principle" because it's an end (Kelsey).

**Chance** (ch. 4–6): a coincidental cause: something that happens *as if* for an end, but not for it. A man goes to the market and by chance meets his debtor. Chance presupposes ends; it can't be the basic explanation.
- Modern "objective chance" = undetermined; Aristotle's chance isn't that. His opponents resemble the "impious" in Plato's *Laws* X (Allen).
- **Luck**: an outcome that would have been a fitting end but wasn't aimed at (meeting your debtor in the market).
- **Spontaneity**: "the stone that struck the man did not fall for the sake of striking him; it fell spontaneously, because it might have fallen by an agent for that purpose."
- "In vain" is said when what is for another's sake fails to achieve it.

**Teleology** (ch. 8): does nature act for an end, or by necessity (Empedocles: rain falls by necessity, and if it makes corn grow that's coincidence)?
- **Winter-rain argument** (II.8, Henry): regular winter rain isn't chance; it belongs to the sun's cycle. Summer rain comes from violent "recoil" of hot and cold.
- Aristotle: things that happen **always or for the most part** can't be by chance. Teeth come in regularly, sharp at the front for cutting, flat at the back for grinding. So nature acts **for the sake of** something.
    - **Empedocles' teeth**: fit for grinding only "by chance", but surviving because they help survival: an early selection argument, which Aristotle rejects (Bolton).
    - "**Olive-headed vines**": if Empedocles' chance monsters were real, plants would show them too. They don't (Henry).
- "Nature does nothing in vain."
- Nature doesn't deliberate; neither does art (a skilled craftsman doesn't deliberate). Teleology without conscious design.
    - "It is absurd to suppose that purpose is not present because we do not observe the agent deliberating."
    - In Plato (*Phaedo*, *Timaeus*) things are explained by an intelligent agent's **choice of the best**. Aristotle naturalises final causality: no deliberator needed (Bolton).
    - Many philosophers of biology (and Kantian readers) hold only "**as if**" teleology. Aristotle: deliberation isn't essential even to art.
- The **craft analogy** runs **from artefacts to nature**: "if artificial products are for the sake of an end, so clearly also are natural products" (Witt).
- The **argument from mistakes**: art makes mistakes, so does nature (monsters); mistakes presuppose ends.
- To deny that nature is an end "does away with nature and the things due to it altogether" (Kelsey).

## Book III: Motion and the Infinite

**Motion** (*kinēsis*): "**the actuality of the potential as such**". Change is a potency being actualised, while it's still in process. The bricks being built into a house.
- Every motion needs a mover that's actual in the relevant way: fire heats because it's actually hot.
- "Actualisation of the potential as such" looks circular; two interpretations of "actuality" here (Charles).
- The "**imperfective paradox**": changes, while going on, exist but aren't complete: housebuilding is complete only when the house is built.
- *Phys.* III.3: teaching and learning are one activity in the learner; "the teacher in teaching does not learn what he teaches".

**The infinite**: exists only **potentially**: you can always divide further or count further, but never have an actually infinite collection. The answer to Zeno.

## Book IV: Place, Void, Time

- **Place**: the inner boundary of the surrounding body.
- **Void**: doesn't exist. In a void there'd be no reason to move one way rather than another, and things would move infinitely fast.
- **Time**: "**the number of motion with respect to before and after**". No motion, no time. Time needs a soul to count it? Left as a puzzle. Augustine's discussion of time responds to this (see [[Augustine's Confessions: Memory, Time and the Restless Heart]]).

## Books V–VI: Kinds of Change and Continuity

- Kinds: substance (generation/destruction), quality (alteration), quantity (growth/diminution), place (locomotion).
- Motion, time and magnitude are **continuous**: infinitely divisible, not made of indivisible points. Zeno answered again.
- Is the *Physics* one continuous treatise? Book VIII's "**Potentiality Doctrine**" (points in a continuum exist only potentially until marked) conflicts with V–VI's account of a single change (Coope).
    - A thrown apple going up and back: Book VIII says it rested at the top, so two motions.

## Books VII–VIII: The First Mover

- **"Everything that is in motion is moved by something."** Nothing reduces itself from potency to act in the same respect.
    - "A moved thing must be moved by something distinct from it" (*Phys.* VII.1).
- **Perfection and habituation** (VII.3, Leunissen): gaining virtue isn't an **alteration** but a **perfection**: a circle is "most a circle" when it reaches its proper virtue.
    - Nothing that behaves one way by nature can be habituated otherwise (a stone thrown up 10,000 times won't learn to rise: *EN* II.1).
    - Virtuous **activity** (second actuality) produces virtuous **disposition** (first actuality), so teachers and lawgivers must make people do good acts.
    - **Music** forms character and judgement about "appropriate characters and good actions" (*Pol.* VIII.5). See [[Aristotle's Politics]].
- **Self-movers** (animals) are really one part moving another: the soul moves the body. See ch. 13 of the *Critical Guide*.
    - Within a self-mover there must be an **unmoved part** that moves and a moved part (Coope).
    - A moving thing is, as such, **incomplete** (*atelēs*); the first cause of motion must be active "in a complete way".
- A series of movers can't go to infinity in an essentially ordered chain (each moving only because moved). So there must be a **first unmoved mover**.
- Motion is **eternal** (Aristotle argues): there was no first change, since that change would need a prior change to start it. So the first mover must be eternal, **without parts or magnitude**, and cause motion everlastingly.
    - Democritus's principle for eternal motion: "things happened in the past as they happen now" (Falcon).
    - Aristotle commits to motion with no beginning, no end, and **no gaps**.
    - Result: (1) a first unmoved mover, (2) something eternally moved by it (the outer heaven).
    - **Projectiles**: moved by a series of movers in contact (the air). An infinite sequence of finite powers couldn't produce infinite motion: the first mover has **infinite power** and so **no magnitude**.
    - Change between opposites is indeterminate and must stop at its end; only circular motion can be eternal (Crubellier et al.).
    - *GC* II.10: since individuals can't be eternal, God "**filled up the whole**" by making coming-to-be continuous: eternal generation is "the nearest thing to *ousia*", "connecting being together".
    - So form is substance partly because it's **eternal** through circular generation (man begets man).
- *Metaphysics* Λ then identifies it as pure **actuality**, thought thinking itself, moving as the object of love. See [[The Unmoved Mover]], [[Aristotle and God as Pure Act]].
    - From the prime mover "nature and the heavens are suspended" (*Metaph.* Λ.7).
- The unmoved mover, pure form without matter, isn't technically part of the physical world: it belongs to "first philosophy" (Leunissen).

## Notes

*Mine.*

- Act and potency is Aristotle's answer to Parmenides and is behind almost everything in scholastic philosophy. Getting this right is what makes Aquinas's First Way make sense: it's about **here-and-now** causation, not a first moment in time. See [[The Last Superstition]].
- The **eternity of motion** is where Christians had to disagree: St. John Philoponus (6th c.) argued against it using Aristotle's own point that an actual infinite can't be traversed. That's the origin of the kalam argument. See [[Creation out of Nothing]].
- **Teleology without a designer** is an important distinction: Aristotle's final causes don't need a conscious designer *in* nature; Aquinas's Fifth Way argues they ultimately need an intellect *behind* nature.
- Modern science dropped formal and final causes, but biology still talks about functions. See [[Hume on Causation and Induction]] sec. 4.
- Energeia and its later history: [[Aristotle East and West]].
