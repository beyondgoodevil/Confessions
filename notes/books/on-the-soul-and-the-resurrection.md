Written c. 380, soon after the death of Gregory's brother **St. Basil**. Gregory visits their sister **St. Macrina**, abbess of a community in Pontus, who is dying. Grieving, he asks her about death, and she, from her deathbed, teaches him. Modelled on Plato's *Phaedo* (Socrates on his last day), but with a woman as the teacher and the resurrection as the goal. Gregory calls her "the Teacher".

- The dialogue is a case study in the meeting of Christianity and Greek culture. Basil studied in Athens with Gregory Nazianzen; with Gregory of Nyssa they're the three **Cappadocian Fathers**. Gregory of Nyssa had been a teacher of rhetoric and a church **reader**. He attended **Constantinople (381)**, where the Spirit's divinity was the main issue.
- Why a dialogue with Macrina as teacher? Perhaps it lets Gregory stage his own inner conflict: the former rhetor (Gregory) takes the Hellenic side, and Macrina, who led him into the Church, takes the Christian side.
- Background (Roth's introduction): Orphics and mystery cults believed in some immortality (likely reincarnation); Plato (*Phaedo*, *Republic*) made the immaterial soul the real person. The first Christians believed they'd **seen Jesus alive** after death; Paul concluded the resurrection body is **material** but different.

## 1. Does the Soul Survive?

- Gregory plays the sceptic: maybe the soul is just the body's harmony (as in the *Phaedo*), and when the body breaks up, it's gone. Epicureans, materialists. If the body is just the elements combined, the soul would be dispersed with them.
- What's most grievous in death? Medicine itself shows we fight death "by its arts".
- Macrina: the world shows an invisible **mind** ordering it; similarly the body shows an invisible soul. We know the soul exists from its operations, as we know God from His works.
- An argument from analogy: the soul is to the body as God is to the cosmos. Both are present without being material.
- If the soul can't be "anywhere" because it's unlike the elements, then the living body (a concurrence of elements) would have to be soulless too.

## 2. What Is the Soul?

- Macrina's definition: **"a created, living, intellectual essence, which by itself gives to the organic and sensitive body the power of life and of perceiving the sensible."**
- "Know thyself": the soul teaches us it's **immaterial and bodiless**, working by its own nature and revealing its motions through bodily organs.
    - Analogies: a jar called "empty" is full of **air**: it floats until pushed under; the **water-organ** (Ctesibius's *hydraulis*) uses air to make music through pipes. Invisible things work through visible.
- It's simple and non-spatial, so it isn't scattered when the body is. The soul is in all the elements of the body equally. What is "intelligible and without dimension is neither contracted nor dispersed".
- It's the **image of God**: it can't be fully defined, just as God can't. We know the mind **exists** from its activity, but what it is in its essence we know only by removing what sense shows: "our mind is the same as the divine nature" in this respect, known by negation. See [[Image and Likeness]].

## 3. The Emotions

- Are anger and desire part of the soul? Plato said yes (three parts). Macrina: **no, not of its essence**. They're movements we share with animals, added "from outside" after the Fall (the "garments of skin", Gen 3:21).
    - She starts from the classical definition, man as **rational animal**: the definition doesn't mention the emotions, so they aren't essential.
    - The **perceptive, nutritive and growing** faculties are in us but don't change the definition of the soul; nor do anger and desire.
    - Faculties "on the boundary of the soul", which can go either way (anger, fear), "have become attached to the soul **from outside**", "because no such mark appears on the **archetypal beauty**" (God, of whom the soul is the image).
- They aren't evil in themselves: used well, they become virtues (desire becomes love of God, anger becomes courage against sin). Knowledge of the good leads to virtue (cf. Plato's *Meno*). Love is the one that remains. See [[The Virtues and the Eight Passions]].
- Moses is the example: he rose above anger and desire and was still fully human.
- Macrina distrusts rhetorical art: "we are often suspicious of the truth itself if presented with this kind of art."

## 4. The Soul After Death

- **Hades** isn't on "the dark side of the earth": the earth is a **sphere**, lit on all sides in turn; and Hades isn't a place at all, since souls are incorporeal. In Scripture and among unbelievers it means "**the transition to the invisible and unseen**" (*a-idēs*).
- Phil 2:10, "every knee... of things in heaven, in earth, and under the earth": Macrina reads these as **states**: "heavenly" = angelic and bodiless; "earthly" = in the body; "subterranean" = souls separated from the body.
- The soul goes on being united to the **elements** of its body even when scattered. It knows its own, like a painter knowing his own colours, or a potter recognising his pots among others.
    - **Potter analogy**: worked pieces of clay mixed into unworked clay can be distinguished "even more surely". The soul knows its own body after dissolution "because of the signs which persist in the remnants".
- That's how the same body can be raised: the soul keeps a relation to "its" matter. Its continuing link with the elements "both is and may properly be called 'resurrection'". See [[Personal Identity and the Resurrection]].
- The rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16): the "chasm" isn't spatial but a difference in disposition. "Abraham's bosom" is figurative, like a sheltered "bosom" of the sea: the calm harbour where souls who sailed virtuously through life moor.

## 5. Purification

- Souls attached to material things after death **suffer**, because they have to be torn away from them. Like pulling a rope out of mud, or gold out of ore through fire.
    - Modelled on the *Phaedo* (81a-d), where souls attached to bodily pleasures can't escape. But Macrina replaces "body" with "**flesh**" in Paul's sense: sinful human nature, body and soul.
    - We should free ourselves from the flesh's hold now, "so that after death we may not need another death to cleanse us from the remains of the fleshly glue".
- The pain isn't punishment imposed from outside but the **experience of separation** from what one clung to, in the presence of God. "The divine judgment does not primarily bring punishment on sinners" but separates good from evil.
- Divine fire is the same fire that purifies and that burns. See [[Hell as the River of Fire]].
- **Why is it painful?** In proportion to the attachment. The more evil has grown into the soul, the more painful its removal.
    - Every nature attracts what's proper to it; the human is proper to God, "because it bears the imitation of its Archetype", so the soul is drawn to God.
    - **The rope in the mud**: the rope is pulled hard because of the mud: what's alien to God must be "**scraped off forcibly**" since it has grown onto the soul.
    - Advice: keep the soul pure, or at least limit failures to "moderate lapses which are easily cured".
- **Memory and hope**: deceived by hope's imitation of beauty, the soul feels shame in memory: "civil war in the soul". But in God, neither hope nor memory is needed: what is seen needn't be remembered, and "why does one still hope for what he has?" (Rom 8:24).
- Macrina "equates God with the Form of the Beautiful". When the hoped-for comes, everything grows quiet except **love**, "not finding anything to take its place" (cf. 1 Cor 13:13).
- Note in Roth: Origen and Plotinus studied under the same teacher (Ammonius Saccas).

## 6. The End: Apokatastasis

- Evil is **not real being**: it's a privation, and can't be infinite. Eventually it runs out.
- At the end, God is "all in all" (1 Cor 15:28): every soul purified, evil gone. Even, it seems, those most deeply in it.
    - Once freed from "the shameful garment of his debts", a person enters freedom. "Those released from evil will be in the divine Nature, so that 'God may be all in all'": the apostle "seems to teach the complete annihilation of evil". "He who will be 'in all' will not be in what does not exist."
    - Participants in God keep growing: "the fountain of good things flows unfailingly"; each receives more and becomes able to receive more (*epektasis*).
- This is Gregory's famous **restoration of all things** (*apokatastasis*). Contested ever since:
    - defenders (Hart, *That All Shall Be Saved*; Ramelli): it's the plain teaching here
    - others: Gregory means the restoration of **human nature**, not every person; or it's his personal speculation (*theologoumenon*)
    - the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553) condemned Origenist apokatastasis; Gregory was never condemned and is a saint. See [[The Fifth Ecumenical Council]].

## 7. Against Reincarnation

- Pagans "partly attained to the doctrine of the resurrection" without agreeing with it.
- Macrina rejects **transmigration** (Plato, Pythagoreans): a soul passing into animals or plants. Absurd consequences: eating a lettuce could be cannibalism; souls would have no proper bodies.
    - And how should one live? Either afraid of harming everything (all are relatives), or harsh to humans as no different from beasts.
- She also rejects the **pre-existence** of souls (Origen): souls falling into bodies as punishment. (Roth: the critique applies also to Origen's *On First Principles*.)
    - If heaven is "immutable", how can passion arise there? It makes evil the beginning of every life, which is "impious".
    - When would the soul enter? At the marriage act? Would it wander "like a homeless refugee in a kind of Limbo" waiting for a body? If life began by chance, it would end by chance: no providence.

## 8. The Origin of the Soul

- Creation **from nothing** (Heb 11:3): there's no eternal matter equal to God in unbegottenness (pagan philosophers generally assumed one), nor is creation of the same kind as God. How God creates is "unattainable" to inquiry.
- Soul and body come into being **together**, at conception. The seed has the soul's power as the seed of a plant has the whole plant: "just as a sprouting seed grows gradually to its goal, so when a human being is formed the power of the soul appears according to the measure of the bodily stature."
- Neither pre-existence nor later creation.
- What has no composition (the soul) has no dissolution.

## 9. The Resurrection

- Scripture (Ezek 37, 1 Cor 15) teaches the body will rise. It also says the succession of generations will one day **stop**: the number of humans will be complete.
- Objection: bodies change constantly, and decompose and become part of other things. Which body rises?
    - The **Heraclitus** objection (Gregory pressing it): the body is always flowing, like a flame: "you cannot take hold of the same flame twice". Which body rises: the infant's, the old man's, the sick, the maimed? Macrina: "Bravely you have attacked the doctrine of the resurrection by the art which they call rhetoric."
    - In the life to come there's no eating, marrying (Matt 22:30; "the kingdom of God is not meat and drink", Rom 14:17): so objections based on present bodily functions are "pointless".
- Answer: the soul's relation to its **form** (*eidos*) and elements; God reassembles the same person. But the risen body is changed: **"sown in corruption; raised in incorruption"** (1 Cor 15:42).
- The seed analogy (1 Cor 15:37): the risen body is to the present as the plant is to the seed: the same, and transformed. "Does not farming teach you that a man is a fool if he measures the divine nature by his own measure?"
    - The "bastard plants" image: the genuine seed has remained immature, smothered by unnatural growth (the passions), and is restored in the resurrection to its original form.
- The risen body has no need of the "garments of skin": no age, sickness, sex for procreation. Humanity is restored to its first state, the image. (The "garments of skin", probably following Origen, are a denser materiality put on after the Fall, to be laid aside.)

## Notes

*Mine.*

- Macrina as teacher of a bishop is striking for the 4th century. Gregory also wrote her *Life*.
- The Platonist framework is clear, but the corrections are what matter: the soul created, not eternal; body and soul together; resurrection, not escape from the body.
- The purification chapters are the patristic basis for the Orthodox idea of the "river of fire", where heaven and hell are the same divine presence experienced differently.
