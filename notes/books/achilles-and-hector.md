Seth Benardete (1930–2001) was a student of Leo Strauss at Chicago and is best known for his late book on the *Odyssey*, *The Bow and the Lyre*. This is his first book, written in 1955 when he was 25. Michael Davis' preface tells the story: Benardete "had all these notes, but nothing made any sense". Then he read the *Iliad* straight through in three days in a stove-heated room in a Florence pensione, saw that it followed the structure of **Diotima's speech** in Plato's *Symposium*, and wrote the dissertation in a month. The book has two halves. **Style** looks at what all the heroes share, mostly through epithets and similes. **Plot** reads the poem as "the tragedy of Achilles". Benardete's one big claim: **the hero wants to be a god, and the nearest he can get is to become a thing**. Here's how the argument goes:

### 1. The Method: Epithets Mean Something (Part I, Introduction)

- **The problem.** The *Iliad* is too long to be just the story of Achilles. A short tragedy would do that. Why the catalogue, Diomedes, the Deception of Zeus? An ancient commentator (Porphyry, quoted p. 3) answers: Homer called it the *Iliad*, not the *Achilleid*, because he wanted to show "all heroes, and what sort of men they were". So Achilles is "a hero in a world of heroes", and we need the others to measure him.
- **Formulae.** Homer needed a style that was "rapid, easy and spacious", steering "between the whirlpool of anarchic invention and the rock of listless monotony" (p. 5). Formulae solve part of this. "They are stage directions" for walking, speaking, falling and dying.
- **Epithets** do more. Benardete thinks Homer kept some uses of each epithet **neutral** (to show it's always part of the hero) and made others **pregnant** with meaning in context. Both are needed. His footnote: "It was Milman Parry's error" not to see that an epithet being irrelevant in some places doesn't make it irrelevant everywhere (p. 6 n. 3).
- **Example: *amymōn*, "blameless".** For Achilles it means the best. For Aegisthus in the *Odyssey* it means the lowest kind of good breeding. "Godlike Pandarus", "strong and blameless son of Lycaon", is ironic: he's a fine archer and a fool. When the dying Patroclus calls Achilles "blameless", it's as if he forgives Achilles for sending him to die. To deny all this "deprives the Iliad of half its effect and turns it into a scrapheap" (p. 9).
- He compares Virgil's *pius Aeneas*: sometimes obviously apt (burying his nurse), sometimes not (throwing a spear), but even then it tells you Aeneas is pious "no matter what he does".
- He admits he's done things backwards, explaining "first what a hearer would come to appreciate last". He even says that if the epithets were properly understood, "the plot of the Iliad would necessarily follow" (p. 7).

### 2. Men and Human Beings (Part I, ch. I)

- **Andres vs anthrōpoi.** *Anthrōpoi* ("human beings") are men and women together, the anonymous crowd, past generations and future ones. *Andres* are men in the fighting sense, "he-men". The heroes never call themselves *anthrōpoi*. The singular *anthrōpos* occurs only three times in the *Iliad*, always in Homer's own voice.
- **Odysseus' tact** (Book 9). Repeating Agamemnon's oath that he never touched Briseis, "as is the way of human beings, men and women", Odysseus changes it to "either of men or of women" so as not to put Achilles among mere humans.
- **"Deeds are done by *andres*, words are spoken by *anthrōpoi*"** (p. 13). The heroes despise words, but depend on later humans for their fame. Later humans see them as "demigods" (*hēmitheoi*, only once in the *Iliad*).
- **Heroes and gods are the same kind of thing on a different scale.** Achilles is a *theios anēr*. "*Theios anthrōpos* would be unthinkable."
- **Two laments.** Odysseus (*Od.* 18) says "the earth nurtures nothing weaker than a human being", because the gods can take away your luck at any time. Zeus (*Il.* 17), pitying Achilles' horses, says nothing is "more pitiful than a he-man". For Zeus, "mortality and mortality alone makes for the misery of man" (p. 15). That is the difference between the two poems: the *Iliad* is man protected by the gods (at war), and the *Odyssey* is man without the gods. *Anthrōpos* is more common in the *Odyssey* (118 times against 70), *hērōs* in the *Iliad* (73 against 40). By the *Odyssey*, "hero" has become a country word a swineherd uses.

### 3. Achaeans and Trojans (Part I, ch. II)

- **The epithets differ in kind.** Trojans are "high-spirited" (*megathymoi*, *hyperthymoi*) and "tamers of horses". Achaeans are "well-greaved", "bronze-coated", "long-haired". You can see that the Achaeans are well-armed, but you can't see that the Trojans tame horses. Trojan epithets tell you what they **are**; Achaean ones only describe their **armour**, and they can take it off. Achaeans are many-sided, "polytropoi": "there is no Odysseus among the Trojans" (p. 21).
- **Achilles' sneer.** In Book 1 he calls the Achaeans *megathymoi*, meaning "great-spirited" so as to rouse them against Agamemnon. Agamemnon twists it to mean "greatly generous" (they'll give me a prize). The phrase never occurs of the Achaeans again.
- **High spirit rises and falls.** Trojans flourish on success and collapse in defeat. Their spirit is "all action and no recoil".
- **Silence vs noise.** The Achaeans go into battle in silence ("nor would you say they had speech"); the Trojans bleat like ewes "called from many lands". Silent men can hear orders. Their wave simile is "deliberately inexact": the Achaeans are "an ordered series of silent waves", something impossible in nature. The Trojans are "in the later Greek vocabulary, barbarians" (p. 24); Benardete quotes Brasidas in Thucydides. The same contrast, he says, comes back in Herodotus as Greeks and Persians.
- **Two kinds of shame (*aidōs*).**
  - **Military shame** is respect for your equals ("be ashamed before one another", Agamemnon and Ajax).
  - **Civil shame** is respect for those weaker than you. That's Hector, "ashamed before the Trojans, men and women both".
  - The Trojans as a body are moved only by civil shame, and only their best warriors at that. In the Doloneia, Nestor offers fame and a token prize; Hector offers Achilles' horses and chariot.
- He closes with **Lessing's *Laocoön*** on why Priam forbids the Trojans to weep at the funeral truce: "only the civilized Greeks can both weep and be brave, while the barbarous Trojans, in order to be brave, had to stifle all their humanity" (p. 28).

### 4. Authority and Power: The Sceptre (Part I, chs. III–V)

- **Lineage.** "Who is Achilles?" means "whose son?" He is Peleides, son of Peleus, grandson of Aeacus, great-grandson of Zeus. Odysseus is the opposite: the *Odyssey* introduces him by what he did, and he's only "Laertiades" again once he's back in Ithaca. As "No-one" to the Cyclops he is "speaking more truthfully" than when he gives his father's name, and the pun on *outis*/*mētis* makes his wisdom his anonymity.
- **The two histories of the sceptre.** When Achilles swears by it in Book 1 he describes it as a stick cut on a mountain, stripped of bark, carried by the judges. In Book 2 we get its pedigree: Hephaistos made it for Zeus, Zeus gave it to Hermes, then Pelops, Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon. Lessing saw the point. **"The conflict between them is between authority and power, between the gifts of nature and those of an heritage"** (p. 33). Benardete compares Bolingbroke and Richard II.
- Achilles flings the sceptre down: any stick would do. Agamemnon has to lean on it. **Odysseus** alone uses it both ways in Book 2: to the kings he says authority comes from power (Zeus will punish you); to the rank and file he says power comes from authority, and beats them with it.
- **The catalogue of ships is arranged around the quarrel.** Odysseus is in the centre with fourteen contingents on each side. Agamemnon's side has 732 ships, Achilles' side 442. Agamemnon's side is all place-names and wealth; Achilles' side is all stories about individual heroes.
- **Ancestral virtue.** Diomedes fights as "Tydeides" (son of Tydeus) and only becomes plain "Diomedes" when wounded and praying. "To surpass one's father unthinkable." In the chariot race Menelaus uses *aretē* to mean both the virtue of his horses and his own: "virtue and lineage are for Menelaus interchangeable" (p. 39).
- **Armour makes the man** (ch. V). Agamemnon has only five epithets, nearly all shared, and has to be built up piece by piece in his arming scene; his breastplate gets the simile, not him. "He is unpoetic." Hector needs no arming scene. Hector unarmed thinks he'd be killed "as if I were a woman". When Hector puts on Achilles' armour, Ares enters him. Patroclus fits Achilles' armour perfectly but can't lift his spear: "Had Patroclus been able to wield it, we would have had two Achilles" (p. 43).

### 5. Ajax, Speed, and Things (Part I, chs. VI–VIII)

- **Ajax** is a lion that "devours it himself", a sign that his excellence doesn't include loyalty. He is a winter river that carries away oaks and "much trash", killing nobodies; and an ass glutted on corn that boys can't beat out of a field. Benardete links this to Aristotle's "courage" of beasts. His stubbornness "lacks all nobility".
- **Achilles = the two Ajaxes.** Telamonian Ajax would never yield in close fighting, but can't match Achilles' speed; Oilean Ajax is fast at following a rout. "The shoulders belong to one, the legs to the other" (p. 47).
- **Swift-footed.** The epithet is placed with care. Achilles is "swift-footed" when he defies Agamemnon and becomes "Peleides" when Athena restrains him.
- *Aretē* is used only of men and horses in the *Iliad*. Horses unite strength and beauty, stamina and speed, which is "the miracle" of Achilles' excellence.
- The same horse simile is given to Paris and to Hector: beauty and power, the two poles, "fused in Achilles". Virtue also needs **willingness**: horses fly forward "not unwillingly", and slavery takes away "half of virtue". "One must be willing to die."
- **Hector the axe** (ch. VIII). Paris compares Hector's heart to an axe; it's the only time a hero is compared to something man-made. Only Achilles is *nēlēs*, "pitiless", a word otherwise used of the day of death and of bronze. "Hector and Achilles embody the ultimate ambition of a hero: to be no man at all" (p. 51).
- Patroclus tells Achilles that the grey sea and the steep rocks bore him, not Peleus and Thetis. The Achaeans are like a rock "by Homer's fiat", but **Achilles really is rock**. "He wears no disguises."

### 6. The Similes: Peace Glossing War (Part I, chs. IX–X)

- **The shield is an image of both poems** (p. 54). Hephaistos divides it into sky, earth and sea, and the peaceful tasks of men take up most of it. The *Iliad*'s war is explained by similes taken from the peaceful scenes of the shield; the *Odyssey* simply repeats those scenes. "**War cannot explain itself. It needs to be glossed by peace.**" "Peace needs no similes."
- **Books 12–13 as a sequence.** He follows the similes in order:
  - Hector is a lion and a whirlwind, "animal and thing". The Lapiths are two oaks, and the stones fly like snow. With Zeus' entry, farmers and shepherds appear in the similes.
  - Then the war becomes two men squabbling over a boundary, then an honest woman weighing out wool for her children's bread. "Seen by the eyes of peace, their war is foolish and of little worth."
  - "Homer does not glorify war... he whispers more and more insistently, 'there is a world elsewhere'" (p. 57, quoting *Coriolanus*).
  - In Book 13 the similes go **paradoxical**: things move and animals stand still. Hector "charges like a snow-covered mountain". When the gods intervene, nature can no longer supply the comparisons, and the heroes are compared to gods and alienated from nature.
- **Fire** (ch. X) is Achilles' and Hector's main simile. It's the exact image for wrath, because it consumes itself as it burns.
  - Homer's other name for fire is **Hephaistos**, who makes the heroes' weapons and is the fire they burn with: "He is their all."
  - Hector is also storm, river and sea; Achilles never is. Hector is "watered-down".
  - Achilles dreads drowning in the Scamander "like a young swineherd". He boasts of Zeus' lightning over rivers and forgets that his own mother is a sea-goddess. When Hephaistos beats the river, "it is the triumph of art over nature" (p. 63).

### 7. Heroic Ambition: Becoming Immortal, Becoming a Thing (Part I, ch. XI)

- **Hector** wishes he could be "honored as Athena and Apollo are honored", even born of Zeus and Hera. To affirm, boast and pray are one verb, *euchesthai*. Fame is the substitute for immortality (Sarpedon's speech in Book 12).
- **The horse is the most ambitious animal** (*thymos*). That's why Achilles has immortal horses and Dolon wants them. But compare **Argus**, Odysseus' dog, who dies at the sight of his master, with Achilles' horses, who weep for Patroclus and will outlast everyone. "Their immortality prevents their affection from ever being serious."
- **Speech.** Achilles' spear goes through Hector's throat but misses the windpipe so that he can speak. Achilles' horses speak only when Hera gives them a voice. "The horse is the measure of man's humanity."
- **The golden hounds.** Immortality is offered to Odysseus, not Achilles, and he refuses it. At Alcinous' palace he sees the gold and silver dogs Hephaistos made, "immortal and ageless forever". That's what Calypso was offering: "To be like a golden hound, a work of art, a thing forged on the anvil of Hephaestus, is immortality." Odysseus prefers "to remain mortal and human: to be a person and not a thing" (p. 67).

### 8. Coriolanus and the Gods (Part II, Introduction and ch. I)

- **Why Coriolanus.** We have lost a sense of heroic virtue because we have no living examples (no "Alcibiades or a Marlborough"). So Benardete glosses the *Iliad* with Shakespeare, whose characters say openly what Achilles only hints.
  - The play is "an experiment in immortality": Coriolanus becomes "a thing of blood", then a god to the Volscians, "a kind of nothing, titleless".
  - Point by point: the quarrel with Agamemnon is the tribunes, Briseis the consulship, the withdrawal the banishment, the embassy Cominius and Menenius, and Patroclus' death the mother's plea. "Achilles is Coriolanus: both are gods in their wrath" (p. 75).
  - But Achilles' ties are thinner (no wife, a divine mother, a distant father), so "Achilles cracks, turns monstrous, and dies" (p. 76).
- **The chariot race shows the three orders.** The order of entry is the order they **should** finish: Eumelus, Diomedes, Menelaus, Antilochus, Meriones. The result is scrambled by **art** (Antilochus' trick) and **providence** (Apollo and Athena). The lot order is wrong for everyone except Menelaus: "mediocrity is all that you can trust it to find out". "Providence and art stand very close to one another." Art is short-lived, and given time, nature wins.
- **The gods beautify death.** When the gods withdraw in Book 5, Trojans die flatly. When the gods are present, deaths get lion and pine similes. The one exception, Patroclus pulling Thestor out of his chariot "like a fish", happens with no god present, only Homer. The gods are "**the gilders of the heroic world**" (p. 80).
- **Book 6 is the godless book**, "the darkest in the Iliad" (p. 84).
  - Axylus, "a friend to human beings", dies with no one to save him. Agamemnon tells Menelaus to kill even the boy in the womb, and Homer calls it just: without the gods, "they become monsters".
  - Diomedes can't tell if Glaucus is a god (the only time a hero calls another *anthrōpos* to his face). Glaucus answers with the leaves simile and his ancestry, and they become "ancestral friends". "Their ancestral friendship replaces the gods."
  - Hector foresees Troy's fall but, unlike Agamemnon, sees no Zeus behind it.
  - In the *Iliad*, providence is despotic. In the *Odyssey* it is "kind and just", and failure is man's own fault.

### 9. The Plot: Helen, Fame, Patroclus (Part II, ch. II)

- **Book 7 changes the war.** Hector, despairing of Troy, challenges an Achaean to a duel for **immortal fame**: a tomb by the Hellespont that sailors will point to. The duel of Menelaus and Paris was in silence and in earnest, to decide Helen. Ajax and Hector exchange boasts, then gifts. Once Helen is "merely a theme for heroic exploits, right and wrong no longer apply".
- Helen herself knows it: Zeus gave her and Paris an evil fate "so that we might be the theme of song". Paris vanishes into Aphrodite's mist, and Aphrodite, wounded by Diomedes, never reappears.
- **Heros and eros.** Socrates in the *Cratylus* derives *hērōs* from *erōs*. The heroes are children of gods' love affairs, and their longing turns into the hope for a fictional immortality. Demodocus' song of Ares and Aphrodite caught in Hephaistos' net is the best "allegory" of the *Iliad*: war and love bound together by art.
- **The Achaean wall** is "the most glorious attempt to break out of the gods' influence, and it fails". Poseidon is jealous that its fame will outlast his own wall at Troy.
- **The three causes of the war**: Helen, then fame, then Patroclus. "From eros to *kleous eros* ('love of fame') to eros is the cycle of the Iliad" (p. 90). This is the Diotima structure (*Symposium* 206–209: love of the body, then love of honour, and so on).

### 10. The Embassy (Part II, ch. III)

- Agamemnon repeats his Book 2 speech about going home, this time meaning it, and the Achaeans stay seated. They're now held by **fame**, not by him. Diomedes can tell him off openly. Nestor splits Agamemnon's role: Diomedes gets leadership in war, Agamemnon keeps the council.
- **The lyre.** The envoys find Achilles singing "the famous deeds of men" (*klea andrōn*) on a lyre from Eetion's city.
  - He has become a minstrel, "unwillingly an *anthrōpos*". He's singing of another Achilles: "the shadow of what he was".
  - "**What made Achilles destroyed Achilles.** As soon as he reflects, he is lost, and he is bound to reflect, to pick up this lyre, once he stops fighting" (p. 94).
- **Why he refuses the gifts.** To accept a price is to let Agamemnon set his worth and to confuse "more" with "better". Half the promises can't be kept anyway (he'll never live to marry Agamemnon's daughter).
  - The embassy is really offering him **fame** in place of booty, and he isn't interested. He sees the choice his mother told him about, glory or a long life, as two equal options, because his idleness has let him reflect.
  - Above all he says "I think that I am honored by the will of Zeus" (9.608). He trusts Zeus as an extension of himself: "Achilles is a god. He wears the ring of Gyges."
  - "The true subject of the embassy is... Patroclus", who sits there silently.
- **Phoenix's story** casts Achilles in both roles. Phoenix slept with his father's concubine and was cursed (Agamemnon will sleep with Briseis, cursed by Achilles). He also nearly killed his father and was stopped by a god, then fled (Achilles and Athena in Book 1).
- **Meleager reversed.** Meleager defends his city, and his **wife Cleopatra** finally moves him. So in the parable Achilles is a Trojan, the Aetolians are the "high-spirited" Trojans, and **Cleopatra is Patroclus**: the name *Kleo-patra* is *Patro-klos* reversed.
  - The Trojans could have moved Hector this way, with family; nobody can move Achilles except Patroclus.
  - **Hector is "a civil Achilles, an Achilles who has not lost *aidos*"** (p. 101).
- **Achilles and the Cyclops.** Both live alone, consult only their *thymos*, and are huge (three men to bolt Achilles' door). Both are "pitiless" and "wild", both lawless toward their neighbours, and both ignore Zeus. "Polyphemus then is the brute perfection of Achilles, Achilles without weakness, without Patroclus" (p. 102).

### 11. The Deception of Zeus (Part II, ch. IV)

- Hera's plan relies entirely on **art**: Hephaistos' locked chamber, Aphrodite's girdle, Sleep bribed with a throne made by Hephaistos. Sleep refuses the throne. Zeus' desire won't wait for the bedroom, and the earth sprouts crocus and hyacinth under them. "Thus Hera is foiled by nature."
- **The gods can be irresponsible because they can't die.** Zeus gives up his son Sarpedon readily; Ares is easily calmed about his son. Achilles imitates their indifference but can't bear Patroclus' death. "He feels compassion more strongly than a god ever can." **"Achilles sums up heroic virtue, and shows that its price is eternal providence"** (p. 105).

### 12. Patroclus and Achilles' Guilt (Part II, chs. V–VI)

- Patroclus calls him *ainarete*, "accursed in your virtue": his virtue will profit no one, not even posterity.
- Achilles finds the "almost-perfect solution": send a surrogate in his armour. His one worry is that Patroclus might win **too much** glory (16.87–90).
- **Pedasus.** Patroclus' chariot has the only three-horse team in Homer: two immortal horses and the mortal Pedasus, taken at Eetion's city. The scholiast says it shows the hero is mortal and immortal mixed. Benardete: "Pedasus is Patroclus", the trace-horse whose death shows that divine rights can't be yoked to human duties.
- Homer **addresses Patroclus** directly ("Whom first, whom last did you kill, Patroclus?") as if to stand by him, since neither the gods nor Achilles do. The only other character he talks to this way is Menelaus.
- **Achilles' guilt.**
  - Waiting for Patroclus, Achilles misremembers his own warning: he now says he told Patroclus not to fight Hector, which he didn't. He leaves out the real motive, his jealousy.
  - "*Ton apolesa*" (18.82) can mean "I lost him" or "I killed him" (p. 112).
  - He is "an ambiguous trial tortured by the doubt of his innocence".
- **Achilles dies in Book 18.**
  - He lies "mightily in his might", the phrase used of the dead Cebriones and, in the *Odyssey*, of Achilles' own corpse.
  - Thetis and the Nereids come out of the sea as they will for his funeral, and she holds his head as mourners hold a corpse's head.
  - He's kept alive with nectar and ambrosia, the same treatment the gods give to preserve Patroclus' and Hector's corpses.
  - "**Achilles is a work of art.** He has more affinities with the golden hounds of Hephaestus than with the natural elements in his own lineage" (p. 113). The book opens with the Nereids (nature) and closes with the shield, "an ordered and fabricated world" ringed by Ocean, the one god Zeus can't summon.
  - From here he is "a puppet": "Achilles dies in Book Eighteen... he is resurrected by art and goaded on by the gods" (p. 114).
- **The reconciliation is self-deception.** He blames Briseis, wishes strife had perished, and refuses food as if fasting could prove his innocence. Odysseus' "we must bury him who dies... having a pitiless heart" is the irony: Achilles was pitiless when he should have been mild, and is mild when he should be hard.

### 13. The Exploits and the Duel (Part II, chs. VII–VIII)

- **The epithets track his descent.** On his return he's "Peleides" again, but in the slaughter of Book 20 he kills ten men in 35 lines without being named once. Homer judges him: "not at all sweet-tempered nor gentle of mood was the man" (20.467). Priam calls him *oulos anēr*, "that baleful man", a word used elsewhere only of Ares, fate and fire. More and more he is just "that man". His similes end with the smoke of a burning city.
- **"Hector is a civil Achilles"** (p. 121). Shame holds him outside the gates ("Hector, trusting to his strength, destroyed his people"), and he prefers his own renown even to Andromache. "In his desire to be the perfect hero, each has ceased to be a hero at all."
- **The dream chase.** "As in a dream one cannot pursue him who flees". Achilles' speed, his defining virtue, "is in the act belied". Only the gods (Apollo leaving, Athena deceiving) end it: "He does not prove himself superior to Hector, but only proves that the gods surpass both."
- **Lions and men.** Achilles takes a simile as his permanent identity: there are no oaths between lions and men. To him "a simile... were but an optative".
- **Hector dies in Achilles' armour**, almost "all made of bronze", struck where it doesn't cover him. **"Thus Patroclus is the means to the final identity of Achilles and Hector"** (p. 124).

### 14. The Games and Priam (Part II, chs. IX–X)

- **Psyche without phrenes.** Patroclus' ghost slips away like smoke. "Even in the house of Hades the soul and image are something, but there are not at all *phrenes*" (23.103–4). Death is the loss of the power to fulfil what you want, and Achilles is already like that: he can't disfigure Hector, whom Apollo and Aphrodite preserve.
- **The pyre won't light.** The winds don't hear Achilles' prayer and Iris has to fetch them. "Cause and effect, will and act, have been split apart, and only the gods hold them together."
- **Achilles doesn't compete.** He's called "swift-footed" most often in Book 23, when he can do nothing with it, and it's the only book where he's called "hero". He gives Agamemnon the spear prize without a contest. The prizes from **Eetion's city** (Pedasus, the lyre, Andromache, Chryseis, the iron discus) make it "Achilles' city of failure".
- **Priam.** Hermes, conductor of the dead, leads Priam "Peleion-wards" (*Pēleiōnade*, unique), a word that echoes "deathwards" and "to Hades". Achilles' tent is the underworld.
  - The odd simile of Priam as a murderer coming to a rich man's house fits **Patroclus**, who came to Peleus after killing a boy. So Priam comes as Patroclus, and Achilles is Peleus.
  - "Remember your father": Achilles weeps for Peleus (himself) and for Patroclus (Priam). "The corpse of Hector is mortal Achilles". *Quis utrumque recte norit, ambos noverit*: "whoever knows either rightly knows both" (p. 132).
  - **Pity for someone weaker** readmits him "into the family of men", and he can eat, sleep and lie with Briseis again. But Benardete insists it's Zeus, not Achilles, who brings it about.

### 15. Epilogue and the Later Note

- **"Achilles is his wrath, and his wrath is his fate."** He has no history; we only see him when he's about to die. The *Odyssey* can start anywhere ("at any point, goddess"), because any episode shows the same Odysseus.
- **Tragic hero vs comic hero.** "The tragic hero has character, the comic hero personality." The tragic hero can't lie, would never call himself "No-one", and "is forever trapped in the Cyclops' cave". "Achilles made his way into his death, Odysseus talked his way out of his." Achilles "is, in a sense, the real poet of the Iliad, and Homer nothing but his scribe" (p. 134).
- **The 1985 note.** He admits two errors:
  1. Defending the epithets instead of looking at "the larger units of action".
  2. Thinking a symbolic pattern was enough without a plausible sequence of action. He missed that Menelaus accepting Paris' challenge meant he no longer claimed Helen by law but had to prove his claim, and that Achilles "forced everyone to acknowledge the right of natural right".
  - "By reading Homer too poetically I did not read him poetically enough" (p. 135).
  - Davis' preface suggests the errors aren't so far from his later "eidetic analysis".

### 16. Assessment

- **Strengths.**
  - Benardete reads with amazing closeness: counts of epithets by book, the one unique phrase, a three-horse chariot, a suffix used only once. Many of these observations hold even if you don't buy his conclusions. Examples are the neutral/pregnant epithet distinction, the silent Achaeans, the godless Book 6, Homer addressing Patroclus, and the funeral imagery in Book 18.
  - Steven Berg's review (*Review of Metaphysics*, 2006) praises it as a guide to Homer's craft and thought, and notes that the questions of gods, fate and choice behind *The Bow and the Lyre* are already here.
  - His reading of the shield as an image of both poems, with similes as peace glossing war, fits nicely with Taplin's later "microcosm" reading (see [[The Shield of Achilles: A World on a War Shield]]).
- **Weaknesses.**
  - He tends to make every detail mean something, which is exactly what he later called his first error: "a symbolic pattern" without "a plausible sequence of actions". The Cleopatra/Patroclus name reversal is lovely, but "Pedasus is Patroclus" and "Achilles dies in Book 18" feel more like clever reading than Homer's intention.
  - He hardly engages oral-formulaic scholarship except for one jab at Parry.
  - The *Coriolanus* gloss helps sometimes but also pushes Achilles toward a Shakespearean self-divinization the *Iliad* doesn't quite state.
  - The style is aphoristic and hard to follow. Davis admits it's "a young man's book".
- **The best thing in it** is the thread from the golden hounds to Achilles as "a work of art". The hero's longing for immortality turns him into a thing, and only Odysseus, who refuses Calypso, chooses to be "a person and not a thing". That links the two poems in a way I hadn't seen before.
- **Orthodox angle.**
  - **Two kinds of deification.** Hector wishes to be honoured like Apollo, and Achilles behaves as if Zeus were his extension. That's deification by seizing. St Paul describes Christ as the exact opposite: being in the form of God, he "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped (*harpagmos*)" but emptied himself (Phil. 2:6–7). The fathers' *theōsis* is by grace and by humility, not by force. Achilles is the perfect picture of what the fathers would call the Adamic temptation: "ye shall be as gods" (Gen. 3:5) by one's own power.
  - **Thing or person.** Benardete's conclusion that the hero's immortality makes him a golden hound, "a thing and not a person", is close to the Christian distinction between immortality as mere endurance and eternal life as communion. In Orthodox theology what lasts is not fame, a shell, or a statue but the **person** in relationship with God (cf. the "image" restored in the "likeness"). Fame, the heroes' substitute, is the kind of immortality the Psalmist mocks in those who "call their lands after their own names" (Ps. 49:11), and yet "man being in honour abideth not" (49:12).
  - **Achilles redeemed by pity.** The one thing that makes Achilles human again is compassion for an enemy's father, prompted by God (Zeus). That fits the Gospel's "love your enemies" (Matt. 5:44) as the mark of being sons of the Father, though for Homer it's a moment of shared grief, not a command, and it doesn't last (Achilles warns Priam not to anger him).
  - **Shame.** Benardete's two kinds of *aidōs* map onto something real. The fathers distinguish worldly shame, fear of what people say (Hector's), from holy shame before God, which leads to repentance. Hector dies of the first kind.
  - **Providence.** Benardete's gods are "gilders", despotic in war and kind in peace. That's a long way from the Christian God, whose providence is neither arbitrary nor a beautifying fiction. But his insight that, without providence, heroic virtue becomes monstrous (the godless Book 6) is something a Christian reader can agree with for different reasons.

### Conclusion

*Achilles and Hector* reads the *Iliad* as one long experiment in becoming a god. The epithets and similes set up the heroic world: men versus human beings, Achaean discipline and Trojan spirit, authority and power, armour and the man, fire and Hephaistos. The plot then runs that world through Diotima's ladder, from love of Helen to love of fame to love of Patroclus. Achilles almost becomes a god in his wrath, but Patroclus' death shows him he isn't one. From Book 18 he is a corpse kept going by divine art, and he only becomes human again when he pities Priam and sees in Hector his own mortal self. Hector, the "civil Achilles", gets there from the other side, through shame. Benardete later said he'd read Homer "too poetically", and sometimes he did. But the central idea holds: the hero who wants to be immortal turns into a thing, and only someone who chooses to stay mortal, like Odysseus, stays a person.
