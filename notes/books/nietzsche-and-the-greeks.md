N. was a classics professor (Basel, appointed 1869 at 24, left about ten years later) before he was a philosopher, and the Greeks never go away. Berry is about what he took from them, Porter about what he *did* with them, Hatab about the contest.

N. = Nietzsche, GM = *Genealogy of Morals*, BT = *Birth of Tragedy*.

## 1. The Philologist

(Berry)

- Philology for N. = the **art of reading well**. Slowly, carefully, not making the text say what you want.
- He calls it *ephexis* in interpretation, holding back.
- He keeps this as his standard of honesty after leaving classics. His complaint about theologians and metaphysicians is that they read badly.

## 2. Against "Noble Simplicity"

(Porter, and some Berry)

- The picture N. inherited: **Winckelmann** (1755), the Greeks as "noble simplicity and tranquil grandeur". Serene, childlike, naked in the sunshine.
- Porter's good detail: Winckelmann coined that phrase about the **Laocoön**, a statue of a man in agony being killed by snakes.
- N.'s verdict: "The 'Hellenic' since Winckelmann: utter superficiality." Classicism chases "a wonderland that never existed".
- His counter-picture, from "Homer's Contest" (1872): the Greeks, "the most humane people" of antiquity, have a streak of cruelty, a "tiger-like pleasure in destruction". Why else all those battle scenes?
- Berry's version of the same point: human, not humane. Their greatness was in what they made of human nature as it is.

## 3. Antiquity as a Weapon

(Porter. This is his main thesis.)

- N.'s interest in the Greeks is always also about **modernity**. Antiquity is "a means for understanding ourselves".
- "Untimely" = acting against the present, on the present, for a future (from the second *Untimely Meditation*).
- Porter lists four ways to use the past against the present:
    1. Antiquity as ideal we fail to match.
    2. Antiquity as dead weight to overcome.
    3. A **dark counter-antiquity** that makes the pretty picture look false.
    4. Continuity between that dark past and a present that pretends to be past it.
- N. uses all four. 3 and 4 are his own.
- He rejects both things classical scholarship ran on: **humanism** (timeless human ideals) and **historicism** (Ranke, "as it really happened"). Historicism isn't the cure because it rests on the same ideals, and it flatters the present as the goal of history.
- So he isn't trying to give the *true* Greeks either. He thought that couldn't be recovered. The aim is diagnostic.

## 4. The Greeks in the Genealogy

(Porter again. Changed how I read GM I.)

- GM I: the nobles ("good" = brave, well-born, truthful, Theognis as witness) vs the slave revolt. Homeric Greeks are the clearest example of the noble type. Etymologies partly from his teacher Curtius, and racialised in a nasty way.
- But Porter thinks the account undermines itself on purpose:
    - The nobles are supposed to be pure doers with no reflection. As soon as they *call* themselves noble they are already representing themselves. They become "noble" only once they no longer are.
    - GM II 23: the Greek gods were used to keep **bad conscience** away. "Foolishness, not sin!" A god must have deluded him. The gods took on "not the punishment but, what is nobler, the guilt."
    - But that is a clever piece of rationalising. Inventing gods to mirror yourself and excuse yourself is exactly the kind of mental trick the "slaves" are supposed to specialise in. (Their version: a god who sacrifices himself for our guilt, which N. calls a "stroke of genius".)
- So is N. building an ideal or knocking one down? Porter: **both at once**. The dark Greeks destroy the classicist fantasy, and then the dark Greeks turn out to look like us.
- Culture = "spiritualisation of cruelty". Modern morality hasn't got rid of violence, only refined and renamed it.

Worth remembering when people quote the "blond beast" passages as if N. were simply cheering.

## 5. Homer

(Berry)

- Homer = N.'s **counter-ideal** to Christian morality. "Plato versus Homer" is his label for the whole opposition.
- Homeric values: strength, honour, beauty, luck.
- The gods affirm life instead of judging it. (But see Porter above on what that costs.)

## 6. The Contest

(Hatab)

- "Homer's Contest": the *agon* runs through everything Greek. Athletics, tragedy, rhetoric, politics, philosophy.
- It cultivates the destructive drives instead of denying them. You compete, you don't annihilate.
- Ostracism: remove whoever is too dominant so the contest can go on. Against "domination by one".
- Greeks gave the passions festivals and fixed days, "a moderate discharge", not "total annihilation".
- Hatab thinks this is where will to power comes from. See [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]].

## 7. Heraclitus

(Berry)

- N. admires him most of the early philosophers. First for his character: proud, solitary.
- Then the teaching: becoming, unity of opposites, and especially the **law / measure** (*logos*) in change.
- Berry argues against the usual claim that N. got a doctrine of radical flux (nothing stable at all) from Heraclitus:
    - The river fragments are about sameness *through* change.
    - Extreme flux comes from later reports and 19th-century handbooks (Zeller).
    - What N. stresses is order in becoming.

## 8. Pyrrho and the Sceptics

(Berry's own proposal.)

- The school closest to N.'s practice is **Pyrrhonian scepticism**:
    - *epochē*: suspend judgement where arguments balance
    - anti-dogmatism: the target is claiming to know how things really are
    - therapy: the goal is health, *ataraxia*, not a new theory
- Fits his praise of sceptics as the only honest philosophers, and his habit of setting perspectives against each other.
- Tension with Hatab? Hatab's N. wants perspectives to *fight*, which isn't very ataraxic.

## 9. Not Imitation

- No going back to Greece. The Greeks are a measure and a provocation.
- Berry: surpass them by action. Porter: use them to unsettle the present.

## 10. Questions

- Berry gives a fairly positive N. (he *learns* from the Greeks). Porter's N. is using them, and half-ironically. Which is the N. of GM?
- If the "noble Greeks" are a deliberate fiction, what happens to master morality as an ideal?
- Compare Plato's side of "Plato versus Homer": [[The Good in Plato]].
