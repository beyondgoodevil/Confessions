Before he was a philosopher, N. was a professor of Greek. He was appointed at Basel at 24 with no doctorate, taught Homer, the lyric poets, Plato and the "pre-Platonic" philosophers to classes of about eight students, and resigned ten years later. The Greeks never left him. They were the measure he held up to modern Europe, the weapon he used on it, and the mirror in which he kept finding his own problems. But they were never one thing. In 1871 they were Apollo and Dionysus; in 1872 they were a people of envy and contest; by 1888 Plato was "a coward in the face of reality", Thucydides his "recuperation", and the Romans had taken over half the praise. Through all of it two ideas stay fixed. First, the cheerful, marble Greece of German classicism is a fiction that hides how terrible the Greeks were and why they needed their beauty. Second, the Greeks matter only as something to be *surpassed*, not copied. Here's how it works:

N. = Nietzsche. BT = *Birth of Tragedy*, UM = *Untimely Meditations*, HA = *Human, All Too Human*, D = *Daybreak*, GS = *Gay Science*, Z = *Zarathustra*, BGE = *Beyond Good and Evil*, GM = *Genealogy of Morals*, TI = *Twilight of the Idols*, AC = *Antichrist*, EH = *Ecce Homo*, WP = *Will to Power* (the notebooks). PTAG = *Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks* (written 1873, unpublished), PPP = the Basel lectures *The Pre-Platonic Philosophers*. KSA = the critical edition, cited by volume and notebook number. Page numbers in brackets are to Bishop's *Nietzsche and Antiquity* unless marked.

### 1. The Professor of Greek

**Training.** Schulpforta (the elite boarding school, where he wrote a schoolboy essay on Alcibiades' speech in Plato's *Symposium*, his "favourite piece"), then Bonn and Leipzig under the Latinist **Friedrich Ritschl**. His published work as a student was on the sources of Diogenes Laertius, the poet Theognis, Homer's ancient biography, and the authenticity of the fragments of Democritus. He also compiled an index to 24 volumes of the *Rheinisches Museum*, the house journal of classical philology (Brobjer, p. 252).

**The chair.** In February 1869 Basel offered him the chair of Greek. He had neither a doctorate nor the *Habilitation*. Ritschl got Leipzig to award the doctorate on the strength of his published articles, without a dissertation or defence (Henrichs, p. 118). He took up the post in April at 24, one of the youngest full professors of classics on record. His inaugural lecture (28 May 1869) was on the Homeric Question.

**Teaching.** Henrichs gives the numbers (p. 117–18): his courses drew between two and nineteen students, eight on average, and some never ran because nobody came. He lectured on Greek lyric poets (at least six times), the Platonic dialogues (four times, 1871–79), the pre-Platonic philosophers, rhetoric, the history of Greek literature, Latin grammar, and Greek religion ("Der Gottesdienst der Griechen", given in 1875–76 to eight students and in 1877–78 to six). After *The Birth of Tragedy* (January 1872) the philosophy professors reportedly told students to stay away.

**The philosophy chair.** In January 1871 he applied for the vacant chair of philosophy at Basel, saying philosophy was his "true purpose". He wasn't even considered. It went to Rudolf Eucken, who later won the Nobel Prize for Literature (Henrichs p. 117; Brobjer p. 245).

**The Dioscuri.** Ritschl called N. and his fellow student **Erwin Rohde** "the Dioscuri", Castor and Pollux. When **Wilamowitz** attacked *The Birth of Tragedy* in May 1872 with a pamphlet called *Zukunftsphilologie!* ("Philology of the Future!", a sneer at Wagner's "music of the future"), Rohde answered with *Afterphilologie* (a pun on "pseudo-" and "anus"). They drifted apart after Rohde's engagement in 1876. Rohde became a model career academic and in 1890–94 published *Psyche*, a massive history of the Greek cult of souls that treats Apollo and Dionysus at length and never mentions N. Cardew calls it a "massive footnote" to BT (pp. 458–71). When Rohde died in 1898, N., by then mad, reportedly said "Rohde dead? Oh!" and cried.

**A sense of proportion.** Henrichs opens with N.'s obituary in the *New York Times* (26 August 1900). It lists "The Overman" among his books, credits him with Strauss's *The Old Faith and the New*, and says he taught "Oriental languages" (pp. 114–16). His identity as a classicist had already disappeared.

The question Henrichs leaves open is the right one for the whole note: could N. have become N. without the Greek chair?

### 2. Philology: The Art of Reading Well

**The inaugural contradiction.** In "Homer and Classical Philology" (1869) N. already felt the job pulling two ways. Art says life is worth *living*; science says it is worth *knowing*. When philology treats antiquity scientifically, it loses "the real fragrance" of the ancient world. The artists who love Greek beauty end up treating the philologists as "the real enemies and destroyers of antiquity" (Bishop, p. 443).

**"We Philologists" (1875).** N. planned a fifth *Untimely Meditation* on his own profession and filled notebooks with it.

- "The classicist must be a human being first" before he can be productive as a classicist (KSA 8, 3[62], Henrichs p. 119). The professors of his day hated this link between scholarship and life.
- "I think that 99 out of 100 philologists ought not to be such" (Cowan, PTAG intro, p. 7).
- A list of why antiquity gets "preference": ignorance of non-classical antiquity, a false humanist idealisation (he adds that "Indians and Chinese are certainly more humane"), scholarly arrogance, habit since Rome, the needs of the Church or its opponents, and the professional interest of a whole caste. Artists projected "proportion and sophrosyne" onto the whole ancient period (KSA 8, 3[4], Emden pp. 383–84).
- **The antinomy of philology:** we have always understood antiquity from the present, "and now we should understand the present from the perspective of antiquity?" (KSA 8, 3[62], Emden p. 385).
- "We are the multiplication of many pasts" (KSA 8, 3[69]). This, Emden argues (p. 387), is the seed of genealogy.

**"White philology."** Held's phrase (p. 418). Ritschl defined the goal of philology as "purification and cleansing" of texts. It is Winckelmann's aesthetic of purity applied to manuscripts. N.'s answer is BT 18's "Alexandrian man", basically a librarian and proof-reader who ruins his eyes on book dust.

**Reading well.** After he left philology, the method stayed as his standard of honesty.

- Philology means "the art of reading well", reading facts "without falsifying them by interpretation", "philology as *ephexis* in interpretation", holding back (AC 52).
- The mark of the theologian is the "incapacity for philology". He reads a German army's victory into the Psalms of David in a way "so audacious that a philologist can only tear his hair" (AC 52). D 84 says the same about Protestant pulpits: "I am right, for it is written."
- HA I 270: only when the art of correct reading reached its peak did science get "continuity and constancy".

**Porter's "three Homers"** (pp. 7–26) show what N. did with this method. There is the Homer of classicism, the ideal of "dreaming Greek"; the Homer of philology, the unsolvable crux of the Homeric Question; and the Homer who opens a window onto something older and darker. The inaugural lecture asked: "has a person been made out of a concept or a concept out of a person?" N.'s answer: "Homer" is a "personified concept". The lesson he took from the history of the Homeric tradition was that contradictions in a tradition are themselves a problem a *history of the tradition* can explain. Porter thinks that is already genealogy. He also notes that N. edited the *Certamen Homeri et Hesiodi* ("The Contest of Homer and Hesiod"), and "Homer's Contest" is its pendant (p. 26).

### 3. Against Winckelmann's Greeks

**The picture he inherited.** In 1755 **Johann Joachim Winckelmann** said the mark of Greek masterpieces was *"edle Einfalt und stille Größe"*, "a noble simplicity and quiet grandeur". Held pulls out the imagery (pp. 413–16):

- The famous example is the **Laocoön**, a priest being strangled by snakes. Winckelmann sees no rage in the face, only inner calm, just as "the depths of the sea always remain calm however much the surface might rage".
- Beauty is like the best spring water: "the less taste it has, the more healthful". The Apollo Belvedere's muscles flow "like melted glass in scarcely visible waves". A body is more beautiful the *whiter* it is.
- Stillness in the statue needs stillness in the viewer. Antiquity becomes stasis. Schiller wrote to Herder (4 November 1795) that the poet stays kin to "a distant, foreign and ideal age, since reality could do nothing but befoul him". Humboldt wanted antiquity to give him "the standpoint outside the world".

**N.'s verdict.**

- Goethe, Schiller and Winckelmann "failed to penetrate the essential core of Hellenism". Their Germany wasted fine words "flirting with 'Greek harmony,' 'Greek beauty,' 'Greek cheerfulness'" (BT 20).
- The German idealists had a "soft, good-natured, silver-glistering idealism" that wanted "noble gestures and a noble voice" (D 190).
- "The concept 'classical' as Winckelmann and Goethe coined it" didn't explain the Dionysian, it *excluded* it (KSA 13, 14[35], Ulfers/Cohen p. 426).
- Winckelmann's and Goethe's Greeks, like Victor Hugo's Orientals and Walter Scott's medieval Englishmen, will turn out to be false historically, though true "in modern terms" (KSA 13, 11[330], Held p. 413).
- Even Goethe "did not understand the Greeks", because he left out the orgiastic (TI "Ancients" 4).

**But not simply against.** Emden (pp. 379–80) shows the early N. was more of a classicist than his polemics admit. In the 1871 *Encyclopaedia of Classical Philology* he tells students to read Homer through Schiller, to keep Lessing's *Laokoon* and Goethe's *Italian Journey* (especially Sicily) as reference points, and to *become modern first* so they can feel what antiquity means. A note of 1870–71 even says we should follow Winckelmann's example (KSA 7, 7[66]). His target was the second-hand, epigonal classicism of the nineteenth-century educated middle class more than Winckelmann himself.

**The surface and the depth.** The real difference is how surface relates to depth.

- To Rohde (16 July 1872): this world of purity and beauty "did not fall from heaven".
- A note of 1870–71: "There is no beautiful surface without a terrible depth" (KSA 7, 7[91]).
- BT 3 asks what "monstrous need" the radiant Olympians answered. Siemens's point (p. 399) is that the Dionysian isn't meant to *replace* classical beauty. It explains it as an *achievement*, not a natural given.
- The mature form is the 1886 preface to GS (§4): the Greeks knew how to live by stopping "bravely at the surface, the fold, the skin". They were "superficial—out of profundity!"

Winckelmann put the calm *below* the raging surface. N. turns it over. The calm is the surface, and the depth is the storm. His image is Schopenhauer's boatman, sitting trustfully in his little boat on a howling sea (BT 1, Held p. 421).

Heidegger later said N. was "the first" to release the classical "from the misinterpretation of classicism and humanism" (*Nietzsche* I, p. 150, quoted by Siemens p. 395).

### 4. "Homer's Contest" and the Two Erides

"Homer's Contest" (1872) was the last of the *Five Prefaces to Five Unwritten Books* N. gave Cosima Wagner for Christmas 1872. It's about ten pages and probably the most important short text for his Greeks.

**Nature and humanity.** It opens by denying that "humanity" separates us from nature. Man "in his highest and noblest capacities, is wholly nature" with its "uncanny dual character". The terrifying abilities may be the soil everything human grows from.

**Cruelty.** The Greeks, "the most humane men of ancient times", have a "tigerish lust to annihilate".

- Alexander drags the defender of Gaza behind his chariot, "a revolting caricature" of Achilles with Hector's body.
- The Corcyrean civil war. Victorious cities killing every male and selling the women and children.
- Why does Greek sculpture keep showing war, and why did the whole Greek world exult in the battle scenes of the *Iliad*? "We should shudder if we were ever to understand them 'in Greek.'"

**Behind Homer.** Homer's "artistic deception" makes the colours milder and warmer. Step back past him and there's "only night and terror". Take Hesiod's *Theogony* (Uranos castrated, Cronos eating his children, the Titan wars), make it darker, and add Etruscan gloom. In such a world "combat is salvation; the cruelty of victory is the pinnacle of life's jubilation."

**Two answers.** To the question "what is a life of struggle and victory for?", one answer was **Orphic**: disgust with existence, life as punishment, "the identity of existence and guilt". N. says that isn't specifically Greek; there Greece is "at one with India and the Orient". The Hellenic answer was different.

**Hesiod's two Erides.** At the start of *Works and Days* (lines 11–26) Hesiod says there are two goddesses of strife. One drives people to wars of annihilation. The other, the good one, drives them to compete: potter envies potter, singer envies singer. "The Greek is envious, and he does not consider this quality a blemish but the gift of a beneficent godhead."

**Examples.**

- Aristotle's remark that even a dead man can spur a living one: **Xenophanes** attacking Homer out of a craving to take his place, "as again later with Plato".
- **Themistocles** couldn't sleep for thinking of Miltiades' laurels.
- An opponent of Pericles, asked who was the better wrestler: "Even when I throw him down, he denies that he fell" and convinces the people who watched.
- Pindar and Simonides "mistrustful and jealous". Sophists against sophists. Tragedy itself taught through a wrestling match of poets.
- **Plato's dialogues** are a contest with orators, sophists and dramatists. In effect he says: "Look, I too can do what my great rivals can do", then repudiates it all and "condemn[s] all imitative art. Only the contest made me a poet."

**Ostracism.** The Ephesians banished **Hermodorus**: "Among us, no one shall be the best; but if someone is, then let him be elsewhere and among others" (Heraclitus fr. 121, quoted by N.). Originally this wasn't a safety valve but a stimulant. It removed whoever towered over the rest "so that the contest of forces may reawaken". The Greek contest "desires, as a protection against the genius, another genius". Every talent must unfold itself in fighting.

**When the contest stops.** **Miltiades**, raised to a lonely peak by Marathon, used his fame to pursue a private vendetta against a Parian and died in disgrace. The gods' envy seizes the man without rivals and lures him into *hybris*. Athens after the empire and Sparta after Aegospotami did the same. Without envy and ambition, the Greek "becomes evil and cruel... vengeful and godless; in short, he became 'pre-Homeric.'"

**Readings.**

- **Hatab** (old notes): the contest cultivates the destructive drives instead of denying them. The Greeks gave the passions festivals, a "moderate discharge", not annihilation. He thinks will to power grows out of this. See [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]].
- **Acampora** (*Contesting Nietzsche* ch. 1) turns it into a typology. You can win by *forcing back* (*herabdrücken*), the annihilating desire, or by *rising above* (*erheben*) your opponent. Only the second is agon. An agon needs a tested quality, rules, judges, and no outside help. It "regulates without subjugating" and is the way values get "created, justified, and shared".
- Acampora's large-scale story: **Homer** is "late", a revaluer who made "a virtue of what Hesiod sees as a curse". His heroes can be greater than gods because gods can't risk their lives. **Socrates** is a revaluer who shut the contest down by moving the enemy inside. **Paul** completes it with an "evil enemy" who can only be destroyed.
- Acampora also notes that N. was thinking about the agon *before* he heard Burckhardt. Likelier sources are Grote's *History of Greece* and Ernst Curtius's lecture "Der Wettkampf" (1856).

PTAG then takes the contest into cosmology: Heraclitus' strife is "Hesiod's good Eris transformed into the cosmic principle" (section 8 below).

### 5. The Greek State, Burckhardt, and the Greeks of the Genealogy

The politics are in [[Nietzsche's Aristocratic Radicalism: The Greek State, Slavery and the Pyramid of Culture]]. The points that matter for the Greeks:

**"The Greek State".** Ruehl (pp. 79–97) shows it was cut from a draft of BT read at Tribschen in April 1871, then given to Cosima as the third of the *Five Prefaces*. She found it "not amusing at all". Wagner's polis was democratic Athens watching Aeschylus by the thousand. N.'s was closer to Sparta and Plato. Slavery "belongs to the essence of a culture".

**Burckhardt.** N. attended **Jacob Burckhardt**'s lectures *On the Study of History* in winter 1870–71. "Power is in itself evil" is Burckhardt's line. N. took over other ideas: without the tyrant Peisistratos, Athens would have had no tragedy; there is a "mysterious connection" between "battlefield and work of art". He even read the *Republic* as having grasped the aim of the state: "the Olympian existence and constantly renewed creation and preparation of genius" (KSA 1, 776). The lines run on to BGE 259 ("exploitation" as a basic organic function), AC 57 ("a high culture is a pyramid") and WP 287.

**The Commune.** In May 1871 the rumour came that the Communards had burned the Louvre. N.: "What is one's significance as a scholar in face of such earthquakes of culture!" He called it the worst day of his life (letter to Vischer, 27 May 1871; Ruehl p. 87).

**The Greeks in GM** (mostly Porter, from the old notes):

- The Greek nobles called themselves *esthlos*, "one who is, who possesses reality, who is actual, who is true" (GM I 5), with Theognis as witness.
- GM II 7: the Greeks were a "people of spectacles". They invented gods so that no suffering would go unwitnessed: suffering was a festival play for the gods. Homer made the Trojan War a show for them (Horkott, p. 316).
- GM II 23: Greek gods served to keep bad conscience away. "Foolishness, not sin!" A god must have deluded him. The gods took on "not the punishment but, what is nobler, the guilt".
- Porter's twist: inventing gods to excuse yourself is exactly the kind of clever rationalising the "slaves" are supposed to specialise in. So the dark Greeks both destroy the classicist fantasy and turn out to look like us. Culture is the "spiritualisation of cruelty".

**Race.** Martin (pp. 40–53) is the corrective to the "blond beast" quotations.

- D 272: there are no pure races, "only races that have *become* pure", and the Greeks are the model of a race and culture that became pure.
- A note of 1875 says the original inhabitants of Greece were partly of "Mongolian extraction", the coast "garnished with a Semitic strip". "What are 'pure-bred Greeks'?"
- The blond beast is a **lion**. N.'s examples are Homeric heroes, Vikings, Romans, Arabs, Japanese nobles. He adds that between the old Germanic peoples and modern Germans there is "scarcely an idea in common, let alone a blood relationship" (GM I 11).
- A note of 1885: "Where races are mixed, the source of great culture" (KSA 12, 1[153]).
- Martin's verdict: an "anti-anti-Semite", not a racist, but with a breeding programme that is still hard to stomach.

### 6. "Full of Gods": Polytheism and the Olympians

Henrichs (pp. 114–37) is the best guide here. His title is Thales' saying, "everything is full of gods".

**What N. thought the gods were.** The gods were "invented for the convenience of humans... to lighten their heavy conscience" (KSA 8, 5[150]). The Greek ones were "accumulations, layer upon layer, some firmly fused, others barely glued together" (5[113]), close to what Burkert says today about their Greek, Anatolian and Near Eastern layers.

**Against a monotheist origin.** The great Hellenist **Welcker** had concluded that the earliest Greeks worshipped Zeus alone. N.: "How Christian, to maintain with Welcker that the Greeks were originally monotheists" (5[114]). The Linear B tablets later proved N. right: Zeus, Hera and Dionysus are all there in the Bronze Age.

**Polytheism and intelligence.** "Greek polytheism requires much intelligence. Naturally you save on intelligence if you have only one god" (5[103]). Henrichs asks fairly how N. could believe that.

**HA I 114.** The Greeks made their gods mirror images of what they admired in themselves, so the gods ennobled them. Christianity is "barbaric, Asiatic, ignoble and un-Greek", because it humiliates people before a standard they can't meet. And: "Wherever the Olympian gods receded into the background, Greek life was more somber and more anxious."

**GS 143, "The greatest advantage of polytheism".** Inventing gods, heroes, *Übermenschen* and *Untermenschen* of every kind was "the inestimable preliminary exercise" for the individual's right to his own law. Monotheism produced the doctrine of "one normal human type". As far as Henrichs knows, this is N.'s last word on polytheism. See [[Nietzsche: The Overman]] for the later *Übermensch*.

**The Olympians as an answer.** BT 3 has the wisdom of **Silenus**: best never to be born, next best to die soon. The Olympians are the answer. Anyone who approaches them looking for "moral elevation, even for sanctity, for discarnate spirituality" will be disappointed. They are an Apollonian answer that let the Greeks bear existence and even love it.

**Two hats.** In his actual religion lectures (1875–78) N. was conventional. No Apollo/Dionysus polarity, no suffering god in the tragic hero, only standard handbook material on sacrifice and priesthoods. His one recurring interest was the priest as "a temporary incarnation of the god" (KGW II.5, 464). Henrichs thinks he was protecting his reputation after the BT scandal (pp. 131–33).

### 7. Apollo and the Foreign Dionysus

The details of BT are in [[Apollo and Dionysus: The Birth of Tragedy]]. Here are the parts about the *Greeks*.

**Henrichs' verdict** (p. 125): nothing BT says about the origins of tragedy, about Apollo and Dionysus as polar opposites, or about the suffering Dionysus as the tragic hero "stands up to scrutiny". He reads it as "an ingenious piece of fiction". Out of a crowded pantheon N. kept almost only two gods. At Delphi Apollo and Dionysus were paired brothers, not conceptual opposites. Henrichs even recasts BT as a Euripidean drama: three playwrights, two gods, one philosopher.

**Three Dionysian scenarios** (Henrichs pp. 128–30):

1. Apollo and Dionysus as art-gods: dream and image against intoxication and music.
2. An alternation of ages. A Dionysian Bronze Age, the Apollonian Homeric world, the Dionysian lyric poets, Apollonian Doric art, then tragedy as the union. Henrichs says there is no precedent for this "breathtakingly bold" revision of the myth of the ages.
3. **Dionysus Zagreus**, the god torn apart by the Titans and reborn (BT 10). "From the smile of this Dionysus originated the Olympian gods, and from his tears the human race." Every tragic hero is a mask of this one original hero. The 1871 lectures had already said that from the Titans' ashes come humans, "whence the dual nature of humans".

**A foreign god, re-made.** Biebuyck, Praet and Vanden Poel (pp. 151–69) show how carefully N. kept two Dionysuses apart.

- There was a barbarian Dionysus whose festivals elsewhere were "a horrible mixture of sensuality and cruelty" (BT 2). Apollo held up the Gorgon's head to him.
- Then, when "similar urges began to break forth from the deepest substratum of Hellenism itself", Apollo had to negotiate a "timely reconciliation" (BT 2). The boundaries were fixed and gifts exchanged, but "fundamentally, the chasm was not bridged".
- An earlier draft has Apollo wrapping the huge opponent "in the most refined spinnings", so that the Greek Dionysus comes out of the cocoon reborn: "Dionysus re-created by Apollo, saved from his Asian dismemberment" (KSA 1, 559).
- So the Greeks neither annihilated the foreign god (the barbarian way) nor turned their backs on him (the Orphic way). They absorbed him.
- N. used the same model for himself later as **incorporation** (*Einverleibung*): absorbing a thought until it becomes instinct.

**Rohde disagreed** in *Psyche*. Delphi tamed Dionysus "for city life". In Athens hardly a trace of Thracian ecstasy remained. Greek religion survived because "much was learnt while nothing was quite forgotten" (Cardew pp. 467–69).

**Orphism rejected.** N. thought Orphism was pre-Homeric and that Homer deliberately ignored it. What he rejected was its picture of life as "penalty" and "indebtedness" for old sins, to be paid off by asceticism and escape from the "circle of rebirth". That made individuation *permanent* instead of a temporary state, the exact opposite of his Dionysus (Biebuyck et al. pp. 153–55). **Pythagoras**, Orphism's reformer, "was no philosopher at all, but something else" (PPP), "melancholically compassionate" (PTAG 2). He was Plato's "secret, enviously looked-at ideal" (KSA 9, 4[287]), not Socrates. See [[Dionysus, Orpheus and the Mysteries: Dodds and the Irrational]].

**Early and late Dionysus.** Jaggard ("Dionysus versus Dionysus", pp. 277–94) argues N. hides how much he changed.

- BT's Dionysus is *metaphysical*: Schopenhauer's will, the primal One behind appearances. Its "metaphysical comfort" lets us feel for a moment that "we are really... primordial being itself" (BT 17). It affirms the world, but from outside it.
- The late Dionysus is *this-worldly*: becoming and will to power. TI "Ancients" 4 says the Greeks guaranteed themselves "eternal life, the eternal return of life" through the mysteries, with the sexual symbol "the venerable symbol par excellence". Christianity "threw filth on the origin".
- A note of 1883 is the bridge: "I have discovered Hellenism: they believed in the eternal recurrence! That is the belief of the mysteries!" (KSA 10, 8[15], Bishop p. 455). See [[Nietzsche: Eternal Recurrence]].
- The end of the line is EH: "Dionysus versus the Crucified" (see [[Dionysus versus the Crucified: Girard's Reading of Nietzsche]]). Then the Turin notes of January 1889: "among Indians I was Buddha, in Greece Dionysus."

### 8. The Tragic Age and the Pre-Platonic Philosophers

N. lectured on the early philosophers at Basel (probably first in summer 1872) and wrote PTAG in 1873. He had a clean copy made but never finished it. Both start from the same question. Not "what did they contribute to philosophy?" but "what do we learn from their philosophy *about the Greeks*?" (PPP ch. 1).

**Philosophy from health.**

- Physicians of culture are right to distrust philosophy: it makes the sick sicker.
- The Romans at their best lived without it; they weren't philosophers for the same reason they weren't artists.
- The Greeks began philosophising "in the midst of good fortune", at the peak of manhood, out of "the ardent joyousness of courageous and victorious maturity" (PTAG 1).
- If they had really been the "cheerful sensates" of the philistines, or the foggy dreamers of the romantics, philosophy would never have started in Greece.

**Borrowing.** On the Orient: "Nothing would be sillier than to claim an autochthonous development for the Greeks." Their genius was knowing how "to pick up the spear and throw it onward from the point where others had left it". But hunting for origins is pointless: "everywhere, the way to the beginnings leads to barbarism" (PTAG 1).

**Knowledge and life.** An unrestrained thirst for knowledge "barbarizes men just as much as a hatred of knowledge". The Greeks reined theirs in because "whatever they learned, they wanted to live through" (PTAG 1).

**The republic of creative minds.** The early philosophers are "monolithic", each hewn from one stone, thought and character bound by necessity. Schopenhauer's "republic of creative minds" is "each giant calling to his brother through the desolate intervals of time". "Other peoples have saints; the Greeks have sages." Where a people has a real culture, the philosopher is a star in its system; where it doesn't, he is "a comet, incalculable and therefore terror-inspiring" (PTAG 1).

**Personality.** Systems get refuted. The personality behind them is "forever irrefutable", and "it is possible to present the image of a man in three anecdotes" (PTAG preface). To Lou Salomé in 1882 N. said he had taught his Basel students in exactly this way: the system is dead, "but you cannot disprove the person behind it" (Brobjer p. 245).

**Pure types and Plato.** The pre-Platonics are "pure types". Plato is "the first mixed type on a grand scale". His theory of Ideas combines Socratic, Pythagorean and Heraclitean elements, and as a man he mixes the regal Heraclitus, the "melancholy compassionate and legislative Pythagoras" and the "psychologically acute dialectician" Socrates. Mixed types found sects that work against the city. The older philosophers worked, without knowing it, for "the healing and the purification of the whole". From Plato on, philosophers "became exiles, conspiring against their fatherland" (PTAG 2).

**The portraits.**

- **Thales.** "All is water" matters for three reasons. It talks about the origin of things; it does so without myth; and inside it is the thought "all things are one". Philosophy "leaps ahead on tiny toeholds" where calculating reason has to build a bridge. The Greek *sophos* is linked (falsely, but tellingly) to *sapio*, "I taste": philosophy discriminates what is worth knowing, "legislating greatness". Thales saw the unity of everything, "and when he went to communicate it, he found himself talking about water!" (PTAG 3).
- **Anaximander.** The first philosophical author, lapidary as a gravestone. His fragment, things perishing into their source "according to necessity, for they must pay penance... for their injustices", is "the enigmatic proclamation of a true pessimist". Coming-to-be is "an illegitimate emancipation from eternal being". His *apeiron* is like Kant's thing-in-itself. N. pictures him leaning out of a metaphysical fortress and asking every creature, "What is your existence worth?" (PTAG 4).
- **Heraclitus.** See section 9.
- **Parmenides.** "Formed of ice rather than fire". He had a moment of "purest absolutely bloodless abstraction", "un-Greek as no other" in the tragic age. "Whatever you do, do not be guided by your dull eyes" began the split of sense from thought that "especially since Plato, lies upon philosophy like a curse". Truth now lives in the emptiest concepts "as though in a house of cobwebs", and "a spider at least wants blood from its victims". His prayer: grant me one certainty, "if it be but a log's breadth on which to lie" (PTAG 9–11). N. also notes that *esse* originally meant "to breathe": being is a projection of our own living (PTAG 11).
- **Anaxagoras.** *Nous* starts a mechanical whirl that sorts chaos into cosmos without any clumsy teleology. Why did it start? Anaxagoras couldn't finish the sentence; Heraclitus had: "a game". "This seems to me to have been the final solution... that ever hovered on the lips of the Greeks." Anaxagoras points to Phidias' Parthenon as if to say: "Coming-to-be is not a moral but an esthetic phenomenon." His greatest disciple was **Pericles**, whose oratory was the *nous* whirl in a human being (PTAG 15–19).

**Why it stops.** Cowan's introduction quotes the notes. N. saw the early philosophers as forerunners of a "Greek Reformation" that never came. "Empedocles is the great Reformer who failed. When he did not succeed, only Socrates was left." And: "Socrates, let me confess it, is so close to me that I am almost constantly doing battle with him" (PTAG intro, pp. 13–14). A third note: "The last philosopher I call myself."

See also [[The Presocratics: Being and Becoming]] for the philosophers themselves.

### 9. Heraclitus: Justice, Play and the Child

Of all the Greeks, Heraclitus is the one N. never stopped praising. In EH he names him and the Stoics as the only forerunners of eternal recurrence (EH BT 3). In TI ("Reason" 2) he says Heraclitus was right that the "apparent" world is the only one; he only went wrong in blaming the senses.

**The man** (PPP ch. 10).

- Of royal Codrid descent, he gave his archonship to his brother and retired to the temple of Artemis.
- He refused an invitation from Darius.
- He played knucklebones with children and asked the gawking Ephesians whether that wasn't better than their politics (Diogenes Laertius 9.3).
- His pride had nothing religious about it, unlike Pythagoras's "superhuman self-glorification".
- "He is a star devoid of atmosphere" (PTAG 8). His method was "I searched myself", the proudest reading of Delphi's "Know thyself". He hated Homer ("deserved to be... beaten with rods"), the Mysteries, and purification with blood, which he compared to washing off filth with filth.

**Against Anaximander.** Anaximander saw coming-to-be as guilt to be atoned for. Heraclitus looked at the same world and saw "lawful order, unfailing certainties". "Not the punishment of what has come-to-be did I see, but the justification of that which is coming-into-being" (PTAG 5). N. borrowed **Rohde's** word for it: a *cosmodicy*, a justification of the world, set against "the teacher of the injustice of the world" (PPP, Gillham p. 146).

**Two negations.** He denied the two worlds, and he denied being: "I see nothing other than becoming." N. compares the feeling to an earthquake, when the ground stops being trustworthy. It took enormous strength to turn this into "sublimity and the feeling of blessed astonishment" (PTAG 5).

**Becoming and scale.** In the lecture N. supports Heraclitus with the physiologist **Karl Ernst von Baer**'s 1860 thought experiment. If our pulse and perception ran a thousand times faster, we would see a bullet in flight; a thousand times faster again, grass and flowers would seem as permanent as mountains. Slow us down and the sun races across the sky and the seasons pass in hours. Persistence is an effect of our scale. Helmholtz is cited for the sun running down. "There is no thing of which we may say, 'it is'" (PPP, pp. 60–62).

**Strife as justice.** Opposites are interlocked "like wrestlers of whom sometimes the one, sometimes the other is on top". Honey is bitter and sweet; the world is a mixed drink that has to be stirred. And this strife *is* justice. "It is Hesiod's good Eris transformed into the cosmic principle; it is the contest-idea of the Greek individual and the Greek state, taken from the gymnasium and the palaestra." Things are "the flash and spark of drawn swords" (PTAG 5). In the lecture: the idea of war-justice is "the first specifically Hellenic idea in philosophy" (PPP p. 64).

**Play.** The scholar **Jacob Bernays** had suggested that Heraclitus thought the world fell from the fire's "satiety" into many things by *hybris* ("satiety breeds insolence"). That would make him a pessimist after all, the "weeping philosopher". N. says "we must discard this entire assumption" (PPP p. 69); when Clement of Alexandria reads the world-fire as punishment of the wicked, he adds: "What a crude misunderstanding! The world process is catharsis." His alternative is the most famous passage in PTAG:

> "In this world only play, play as artists and children engage in it, exhibits coming-to-be and passing away... without any moral additive, in forever equal innocence." (PTAG 7)

- The ever-living fire builds and knocks down "like a child at the seashore". It is the fragment about the *aiōn* as "a child playing, moving counters" (fr. 52).
- "Not hybris but the ever self-renewing impulse to play calls new worlds into being."
- When it builds, it builds by inner laws: "necessity and play, war and justice" (PPP p. 73).
- To someone asking why fire becomes water and earth, he would only say: "It is a game. Don't take it so pathetically and—above all—don't make morality of it!" (PTAG 7).
- So there is no ethic of "thou shalt". "Man's character is his daimon" (fr. 119). Fools have wet souls.
- "At his core he is the opposite of a pessimist because he does not deny away sorrows and irrationality... This is genuinely Hellenic!" (PPP p. 74).

**Justice in history.** Gillham (pp. 139–50) connects this to UM II, written in the same year. There N. sets "justice as strength" against the historians' "objectivity". The just historian has "the outwardly tranquil but inwardly flashing eye of the artist" and is "tragically consumed by an impossible virtue". In the Heraclitus lecture the wise man feels like someone "in the middle of the ocean or during an earthquake" watching all things move. UM II 2 also contains the first sketch of eternal return, the Pythagorean belief that the same constellation of stars brings back the same events, which N. there dismisses.

**Berry's caution** (old notes). N. did not get a doctrine of total flux from Heraclitus. The river fragments are about sameness *through* change; extreme flux comes from later reports and handbooks like Zeller. What N. stresses is *law* and *measure* in becoming.

### 10. Democritus, the Sceptics and the Realists

**Democritus.** For a while he was the philosopher N. knew best. He spent 1867–68 on the authenticity of the fragments and planned a reconstruction of the system (Berry, pp. 98–113).

- In the lectures Democritus is "a pentathlete in ethics, physics, mathematics, music and the arts".
- His system is "of the greatest consequence": in it, "for the first time the collective, anthropomorphic, mythic view of the world has been overcome" (PPP p. 125).
- N. claimed the ethics were the "core".
- He believed the story that Plato wanted to burn Democritus's books and that later Christianity finished the job: "the worst malice of supernaturalism" (Brobjer p. 256).

**Berry's argument.** The sceptics' goal was *ataraxia*, freedom from disturbance, and N. despised that kind of tranquillity: "the moral cow and the fat contentment of the good conscience" (TI "Morality as Anti-Nature" 3). So how can he be a Pyrrhonist? Through Democritus' older ideal of ***euthumia***, "cheerfulness".

- Democritus' longest ethical fragment (B191) makes it a matter of moderation and proportion. Souls that swing over great distances are "neither stable nor cheerful".
- But it isn't calm. It is *eustathēs*, well-built, firmly planted. Vlastos calls it a "dynamic equilibrium".
- "Ease is the worst of all teachers for the young" (B178). "A life without feasts is like a long road without an inn" (B230).
- That is close to the "cheerful science" (*fröhliche Wissenschaft*) and GS 19's tree that needs storms.
- Late in life N. still described his way of thinking as "to a high degree Heraclitean, Democritean, and Protagorean" (WP 428).

**Pyrrho** (Berry, old notes). The school closest to N.'s practice is Pyrrhonian scepticism: *epochē*, suspending judgement where arguments balance; anti-dogmatism; and the aim of health, not a new theory. Berry links TI "Germans" 6, where "learning to see" means being able to defer judgement, to Sextus's definition of scepticism as an "ability" to set things in opposition. WP 437 calls Pyrrho the most original figure after the pre-Socratics. Hatab's N., who wants perspectives to *fight*, isn't very ataraxic. See [[Skepticism]].

**Epicurus.**

- BGE 7: N. knows "of nothing more poisonous" than the joke Epicurus made against the Platonists, calling them *Dionysiokolakes*. On the surface that means flatterers of Dionysius of Syracuse; underneath, actors.
- AC 58: "Epicurus would have won, for every respectable mind in the Roman Empire was an Epicurean". Then Paul came.
- In "The Wanderer and his Shadow" 295 ("Et in Arcadia ego") an Alpine evening makes N. think of Greek heroes in a Poussin landscape, and of "the inventor of an heroic-idyllic mode of philosophizing: Epicurus".

See [[Epicurus and Lucretius]].

**The sophists and Thucydides.** Brobjer shows N. had almost no interest in the sophists until he read Brochard's *Les sceptiques grecs* in 1888 (pp. 252–56). In his Plato lectures Thrasymachus is "greedy for money" and "completely defeated", and Callicles' equation of the pleasant with the good is refuted. So reading N. as a modern Callicles misses that he despised hedonism. In TI the sophists become "the culture of realists", which peaks in **Thucydides**. "My recuperation... from all Platonism was Thucydides every time." Thucydides and Machiavelli's *Prince* share "the unconditional will not to deceive themselves and to see reason in reality—not in 'reason'" (TI "Ancients" 2). A note of 1885: "the height attained in the disposition of a Democritus, Hippocrates, and Thucydides was not attained a second time" (KSA 11, 36[11]). Morley (pp. 27–39) adds that N. still called the Greeks "unhistorical", happily so: they knew how to forget. See [[Thucydides on Human Nature and Power]].

### 11. The Cynics and the Stoics

**The Cynics.** In EH N. says his books here and there reach "the highest thing that can be attained on earth—Cynicism". Branham (pp. 170–81) and Jensen (pp. 182–91) take him at his word.

- **Exile.** Diogenes, mocked for being exiled from Sinope: "that's how I became a philosopher!" N. writes for "those who have a right to call themselves homeless" (GS 377) and hates "a permanent domicile" (GS 295).
- **Asceticism for life.** HA II preface 5 describes his own regimen: "a minimum of life", independence among bad circumstances, "a certain amount of Cynicism, perhaps a certain amount of the doghouse" (the tub). Cynic *askēsis* is the mirror image of the Christian ascetic ideal: training the body *for* life as it is. In UM II 1 N. even says the happiness of the animal "is the living proof of the rightness of Cynicism".
- **"The first night of Diogenes."** A note of 1874 (KSA 7, 31[11]): all ancient philosophy aimed at simplicity of life, and modern philosophers "will come to nothing" until they dare to *live* differently. The story, from Plutarch and Diogenes Laertius, is of the young Diogenes miserable at night during an Athenian festival, until a mouse eating his crumbs shamed him into contentment. Its opposite is university philosophy, whose epitaph should be "It has hurt nobody" (UM III 8, borrowing Diogenes' jibe).
- **Shamelessness and laughter.** GS 107: "As long as you are in any way ashamed of yourselves you do not yet belong amongst us." BGE 26: cynicism is "the only form in which common souls come close to honesty". The higher man should listen when a "buffoon without shame" speaks. N.'s Diogenes is also the man with the lamp in daylight "looking for a human being", which became GS 125's madman. The last word is the postscript to *The Case of Wagner*: "the Cynic warns you—cave canem."

**The Stoics.** Elveton (pp. 192–204).

- N. read Epictetus seriously in 1881. They share a lot: ideas tested by what they do to life, hardness toward oneself, a kind of fatalism. BGE 227: "let us remain hard, we last Stoics!"
- But BGE 9: "'According to nature' you want to live? O you noble Stoics, what deceptive words these are!" Nature is "wasteful beyond measure, indifferent beyond measure". The Stoics wanted to stamp their morality onto it.
- The sharpest note is from 1881 (KSA 9, 15[55]). Stoicism is "a form of anesthesia", "turning oneself into stone as a weapon against suffering". What is the point of embracing a statue in winter if you've made yourself deaf to the cold? It "undervalues the value of pain". Against this: "Heroism is the strength to suffer pain and to add to it" (12[140]).
- GS 326: "We are not so badly off that we have to be as badly off as Stoics."

### 12. Socrates and Plato

The fuller Plato material is in [[The Good in Plato]] and [[Plato's Quarrel With the Poets: Republic X Read Three Ways]].

**Socrates as the turning point.** BT 15 makes Socrates "the one turning-point and vortex of so-called world history". TI "The Problem of Socrates" is the late version. McNeill (pp. 260–75) reads it against Alcibiades' speech in the *Symposium*, the speech N. wrote about at nineteen.

- **The dying words.** "Living—that means being sick a long time. I owe a rooster to the savior Asclepius." Wisdom appears "as a raven, inspired by a little whiff of carrion" (TI "Socrates" 1).
- **Plebeian, ugly, criminal?** Ugliness was "almost a refutation" among the Greeks. The physiognomist **Zopyrus** read Socrates' face as "a cave of bad appetites", and Socrates agreed, but said he had mastered them all (TI "Socrates" 3, 9).
- **Buffoon and hidden depth.** "Everything about him is exaggerated, buffo, a caricature; at the same time everything is concealed, ulterior, subterranean" (TI "Socrates" 4). That is Alcibiades' Silenus statue, ugly outside with little gods inside. Alcibiades says Socrates is the only man before whom he feels shame (*Symposium* 216b). McNeill's point: the ugliness Alcibiades saw was his own, mirrored.
- **Why he fascinated.** The dialectician was "the buffoon who got himself taken seriously". Socrates found "a new kind of agon", became "the first fencing master" for the young aristocrats, and "was also a great erotic" (TI "Socrates" 8).
- **The cure.** "Old Athens was coming to an end". Everywhere the instincts were "five paces from excess". "The impulses want to play the tyrant; one must invent a counter-tyrant who is stronger" (TI "Socrates" 9). Reason as tyrant. "To have to fight the instincts—that is the formula of decadence" (11). Yet he is "this most brilliant of all self-outwitters" (12).
- **Caesar.** McNeill's best find is that TI's definition of freedom uses the same words for **Julius Caesar**: the highest type is found "five steps from tyranny" (TI "Skirmishes" 38). BGE 200 names Alcibiades and Caesar as the "most beautiful" of the enigmatic men "predestined for victory and seduction" in an age of dissolution. So the attack hides admiration. A note of 1885: "I think that I sense that Socrates was deep—his irony was above all the necessity of appearing superficial" (KSA 11, 34[148]).

**Plato.** Brobjer (pp. 241–59) is the sober one.

- Plato is the philosopher N. cites most after Schopenhauer, and he knew the dialogues well. He lectured on all of them four times, mostly as a conscientious teacher summarising secondary literature.
- The lectures say: "The man is still more remarkable than his books." Plato was "not a systematic thinker" but "a political agitator" with a "tyrannic streak". The theory of Ideas is "an invaluable preparation for Kantian idealism".
- The early "inverted Platonism" note (1870–71): "the further away from true being, the more pure, beautiful, better it is. The life of appearance as goal" (KSA 7, 7[156]).
- In autumn 1887 he admitted: "Plato becomes a caricature with me" (KSA 12, 10[112]).

**Late Plato.** The judgements swing:

- In BGE's preface Plato is "the most beautiful growth of antiquity" but also author of the worst error, "the pure mind and the good in itself". Christianity is "Platonism for 'the people'".
- GM III 25: "Plato versus Homer: that is the complete, the genuine antagonism". Plato is "the great slanderer of life", Homer "the golden nature".
- TI "Ancients" 2: "Plato is a coward in the face of reality—consequently he flees into the ideal; Thucydides has himself under control." This Athenian "went to school with the Egyptians (or with the Jews in Egypt?)".
- To Overbeck (9 January 1887): "it's all Plato's fault; Plato is the greatest *malheur* of Europe."
- To Deussen, thanking him for an olive leaf from the Academy (16 November 1887): "Perhaps this old Plato is my true great opponent? But how proud I am to have such an opponent!"
- And to Overbeck in 1883, reading Teichmüller: "how badly I know Plato and how much Zarathustra *platonizes*."

**Lampert's other Plato** (pp. 205–19) takes BGE seriously.

- Plato is a "genuine philosopher", a commander and legislator who told his civilisation "we have to go that way". He was willing to lie for its good: TI's "pious fraud", which "neither Manu nor Plato nor Confucius" doubted their right to.
- BGE 28: under Plato's deathbed pillow was found "no 'Bible,' nor anything Egyptian, Pythagorean, or Platonic—but a volume of Aristophanes". How could Plato have endured the Greek life he said no to without an Aristophanes?
- BGE 190: the Platonic Socrates is "Plato in front, Plato in back, and in the middle Chimaira". That parodies *Iliad* 6.181, where the Chimaera is lion in front, serpent behind, goat in the middle.
- GS 372 is the kindest: all idealism so far was a sickness, "unless it was, as it was in the case of Plato, the caution of an overrich and dangerous health".
- So Plato is absolved of revenge but not of *recklessness*: he tied philosophy to a moral lie that could be captured by religion.

**The contest reading.** Moore (pp. 228–40) reads the hostility through "Homer's Contest". Plato's tyrannical streak (HA I 261: every great Greek thinker believed he had absolute truth and "became a tyrant") is what N. fights. He fights it with honesty about his own will to power, not with a rival dogma. HA I 261 also says "the period of the spiritual tyrant is over" and imagines instead "oligarchs of the spirit".

### 13. Greece or Rome? The Late Turn

Siemens (pp. 391–410) counts about 336 uses of *klassisch* in N. and finds a shift.

**Greece shows the other.** Early on, Rome teaches "how things became what they are"; Greece shows "how totally other it can be" (KSA 8, 5[64]). The "classical" age itself is moved earlier, to the tragic age of the sixth and fifth centuries, before the Persian Wars ended the contest of small cities.

**Rome takes over.** By 1888 the Greeks' manner is "too alien... too fluid to work in an imperative, in a 'classical' way" (TI "Ancients" 2). N. says his sense of style came from the Romans, Sallust and Horace, who get the most energy out of the fewest signs (TI "Ancients" 1). GM I 16 makes the great opposition "Rome against Judea".

**AC 59** is the lament: "The whole labor of the ancient world *in vain*." All the methods of science were already there: the art of reading well, natural science allied with mathematics, "the sense for facts, the last and most valuable of all the senses". And "the free eye before reality, the cautious hand", not as "brain drill" but "as body, as gesture, as instinct". "Why did the Greeks exist? Why the Romans?" Bishop (pp. 441–57) reads this as N.'s anti-Christianity returning to German classicism.

**Goethe.** Goethe's line "Classicism is healthy, Romanticism is sick" (Eckermann, 2 April 1829) runs into GS 370's distinction between art from overflowing strength and art from lack. TI "Skirmishes" 51 calls Goethe "the last German for whom I feel reverence", and says they "understand each other about the 'cross'". That alludes to the Venetian Epigram listing four things Goethe can't stand: tobacco smoke, bugs, garlic and the cross.

**Classical style.** In the late notes "classical" means: forcing "one's chaos to become form", "hardening, simplification", contempt for detail, logic, coldness toward feeling, a "pessimism of strength" (Siemens pp. 404–6; WP 849).

### 14. Not Imitation

**No going back.** None of this was a call to go back to Greece. In the PTAG period:

- "To get past Hellenism by means of deeds: that would be our task. But to do that, we first have to know what it was!" "Let us try to live in the manner of the ancients" and we'll come "a hundred miles closer to them than with all our learnedness."
- "My aim is to generate open enmity between our contemporary 'culture' and antiquity" (Cowan, pp. 5–6).

**Porter's four uses** of the past against the present (old notes): antiquity as an ideal we fail to match; as dead weight to overcome; as a *dark counter-antiquity* that makes the pretty picture look false; and as a dark past that the present only pretends to have left behind. N. uses all four, and the last two are his own. He rejects both pillars of classical scholarship. **Humanism** means timeless human ideals; **historicism** means Ranke's "as it really happened", which flatters the present as history's goal. "Untimely" means acting against the time, on the time, "for the benefit of a time to come" (UM II, foreword).

**Mimesis and homesickness.** By 1875 N. rejects mimesis outright. The creator "can certainly borrow from everywhere" (Siemens p. 396). The homesickness never fully goes: "One is no longer at home anywhere; at last one longs back for that place in which alone one can be at home... the Greek world!" (WP 419). But "longs back" is a confession, not a programme.

**Pindar.** The best figure for the whole relation is the line N. took from **Pindar**'s second Pythian ode, *genoi' hoios essi mathōn*: "become such as you are, having learned what that is". He had it inscribed on a little memorial to his friendship with Rohde in 1867 (Cardew p. 461). It became GS 270 and the subtitle of EH. In Pindar the line is a reproach to the tyrant Hieron, set between Ixion, who chased a cloud, and Rhadamanthys, the just judge (Hamilton pp. 54–68). For N. the Greeks were what the philologist learns. What one *becomes* has to be one's own. See [[Nietzsche: Amor Fati and Becoming What You Are]].

### Conclusion

N.'s Greeks are not a historian's Greeks. Henrichs is right that BT's history of religion doesn't survive scrutiny, Brobjer is right that his Plato is partly a caricature, and Martin is right that the "breeding" talk is ugly. But a few things he saw have lasted.

1. **The beauty was an achievement.** Greek serenity was built on top of a world of cruelty, envy and pessimism, not handed to a lucky, childlike people. "No beautiful surface without a terrible depth." Rohde's *Psyche* and later Dodds's *The Greeks and the Irrational* build on the same insight.
2. **The contest.** Envy, ambition and rivalry, the "good Eris", were the Greek machine for producing excellence, and they needed limits like ostracism to keep any one man from ending the game. This is his most usable political idea, and it is why the agonistic reading of N. exists.
3. **Philosophy as a way of life.** The pre-Platonics, the Cynics and even the Stoics are judged by how they lived, not just what they argued. "It has hurt nobody" is the worst thing you can say about a philosophy.
4. **The quarrel is about which Greeks.** Homer, Heraclitus, Democritus and Thucydides stand on one side; Pythagoras, Parmenides' bloodless abstraction, Socrates' tyrant-reason and Plato's other world on the other. For N., Western history was a contest between them that the second side won, through Christianity, "Platonism for the people".

What stays unclear is whether he wanted the first side to win next time, or a new contest in which someone could finally say, like Plato in "Homer's Contest", "I too can do what my great rivals can do... only better."
