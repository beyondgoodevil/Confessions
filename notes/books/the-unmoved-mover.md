*Metaphysics* IX (Theta) 8 and XII (Lambda) 6–9. Aristotle's first cause, which moves everything without being moved. Notes on how Perl reads this. Last section is mine.

## 1. Act and Potency

- **Potency** (*dynamis*): what a thing can be. Bronze can be a statue.
- **Act** (*energeia*): what it actually is.
- **Form is act.** Not a shape stuck on matter. It's the thing's being-at-work as the kind of thing it is.
- Matter = potency, what can take form.

## 2. Act Comes First

*Met.* IX 8. Three senses:

1. **In account.** You define a potency by its act. "Able to see" only makes sense through "seeing".
2. **In time.** The individual is potential first (child before man), *but* is always brought to act by something already actual. "Man begets man."
3. **In being** (*ousia*). Act is the end the potency is for. And eternal things are prior to perishable ones, and nothing eternal is merely potential.

Upshot: the first principle of everything can't be a seed, a chaos, a mere possibility. It has to be **fully actual**. (Against the old poets who put Night or Chaos first.)

## 3. The Unmoved Mover

- Motion is eternal → needs an eternal cause.
- If that cause itself changed it would have potency and need a further cause. So: **unmoved**, **pure act**, no matter.
- Pure act = **pure form**. Not the form *of* anything. Just form.
- Perl's emphasis: this isn't a weird extra object beyond the world. It is **reality itself**, being in the full sense. Everything else is being to a lesser degree.
- "God" (*theos*) works as a predicate here: *this* is what's divine. It's not an argument for the God of Abraham.

## 4. How It Moves Things

- It doesn't push. It moves "as being loved" (*hōs erōmenon*). Final cause.
- Every natural thing is trying to be actual in its own way: to live, reproduce, go round in a circle (the heavens).
- That is each thing imitating pure actuality as far as it can. So everything has "something divine" in it.

## 5. Thought Thinking Itself

- Its life is **intellection**, the best activity.
- It can't depend on an object outside itself (that would be potency). So it thinks itself: *noēsis noēseōs*.
- Usual complaint: a god gazing at his own navel, knowing nothing of the world.
- Perl says that misreads it:
    - In things without matter, intellect and its object are **the same** (*De Anima* III).
    - So the content of divine thought is the whole of intelligible reality, forms without matter.
    - "Thinks itself" = *is* the complete understanding of all that is.
- So Aristotle ends up close to Plato on intellect and forms (see [[The Forms and the Demiurge]]). And no "two worlds": the divine is the actuality that sensible things share in.

Still not sure this gets Aristotle's god to know *Socrates*, as opposed to "man". Perl's reading gives knowledge of forms, not individuals.

## 6. The Argument as a Proof: Feser

Added from Feser, *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (2017), ch. 1, "The Aristotelian Proof". Perl explains what Aristotle meant. Feser turns it into a 50-step argument and defends each step. Read his informal statement, the formal one, and the first replies to objections.

**Stage 1: to a purely actual actualizer**

1. Change is real. (Deny it and your denying is a change. And Parmenides was wrong that change needs something from nothing: the coffee's coldness was there *potentially*.)
2. Change = actualization of a potential.
3. A potential can only be actualized by something already actual. (A merely potential thing can't do anything.) This is the **principle of causality**.
4. A changing thing S exists at all, right now, only because *its* potential to exist is being actualized right now.
5. Whatever actualizes S either has its own existence actualized by something further, or is **purely actual**.
6. This is a **hierarchical** series (all members acting at once, each borrowing its power from the one before), and that kind can't go on forever.
7. So there is a purely actual actualizer.

The distinction that carries it:

| | Linear series (*per accidens*) | Hierarchical series (*per se*) |
|---|---|---|
| Example | father begets son begets grandson | hand moves stick moves stone; cup held by table held by floor held by earth... |
| Earlier members | can be gone | must all be acting now |
| Power | each has its own | later ones only pass on what the first gives |
| Can it be infinite? | maybe (Aristotle and Aquinas both allow an eternal world) | no. Nothing to pass on without a source |

So the proof is **not about a beginning of the universe**. It's about what keeps anything in being this instant. Feser says this is the commonest misreading. It also means "science says the universe might be eternal" doesn't touch it.

Not the same route as Aristotle's own, I think. Aristotle argues from eternal motion (sec. 3 above). Feser goes through the existence of the thing at each moment, which is closer to Aquinas.

**Stage 2: the attributes.** From "purely actual" alone:

| Attribute | Why |
|---|---|
| **One** | two would need something to tell them apart, i.e. something one has and the other lacks, i.e. an unactualized potential |
| **Immutable** | change needs potential |
| **Eternal** | in time means changeable |
| **Immaterial** | matter is changeable |
| **Perfect** | imperfection = unrealised potential |
| **Good** | badness is privation, a failure to be actual |
| **Omnipotent** | all power to actualize anything derives from it |
| **Intelligent** | what's in an effect is somehow in its cause (**proportionate causality**); the forms of things can't be in it materially, so they're in it the way forms are in a mind |
| **Omniscient** | the forms of *all* things are in it |

That's his answer in advance to "fine, a first cause, but why call it God?" (Pruss calls this the **Gap Problem**. See [[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]].)

**Objections he answers**

- *"If everything has a cause, what caused God?"* The premise was never "everything has a cause". It's "what goes from potential to actual has a cause". Something purely actual has no potential to be actualized, so the question doesn't arise. Not a special exemption.
- *Hume: I can imagine a thing popping into existence uncaused.* Imagining a cup appear isn't imagining it appear *uncaused*, as opposed to moved there (Anscombe). And if things could just pop up, why don't they?
- *Kant: causality only applies inside experience.* We learn it from experience, but the reason things need causes isn't that we experience them. It's that they're potential until made actual. Like learning geometry from ink drawings: it isn't only true of ink.
- *Russell: physics has no causes, just equations.* Proves too much ("law", "event", "explanation" aren't in the equations either). And particles have dispositions, which are causal.
- Also deals with Newton's law of inertia and with quantum mechanics (didn't read these closely).

**What I make of it**

- Premise 4 is where the weight is: that a thing's *continuing to exist* is a potential being actualized from outside at every moment. Someone could accept that changes need causes and deny that persistence does ("existential inertia"). Feser knows this objection and answers it later in the book.
- The intellect step leans on proportionate causality and on realism about forms. Not self-evident to a modern reader.
- Compare Hatab's Nietzsche: power needs resistance, so pure act would be no power at all. Feser's "omnipotent" row is the direct opposite. See [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]] sec. 7.

## 7. Orthodox Connections

*Mine, not Perl's.*

- **What can be kept.**
    - God has no unrealised potential, doesn't change, causes everything. St. John of Damascus and St. Maximus can call God act in this sense. See [[St. John of Damascus and St. Maximus the Confessor Describe God as Pure Act]].
    - Everything moving toward God by desire is all over Dionysius and Maximus.
- **What revelation changes.**
    - Aristotle's god doesn't create, doesn't love, doesn't know individuals (see my doubt above). The God of Scripture does all three. "God so loved the world."
    - World isn't eternal. If the first cause is *only* pure act with no distinction in it, the effect looks as necessary and eternal as the cause. See [[Thomism Leads to Eternal Creation]].
- **Essence and energies.**
    - Same Greek word, *energeia*. Orthodoxy: God's essence is unknowable, His energies are many, uncreated, and free.
    - So God is fully actual but His acts toward creatures aren't identical with His essence. This is the objection to Thomist *actus purus*. See [[God as Pure Act: RC vs EO]].
- **Trinity.** "Thought thinking itself" is one closed mind. The Christian God is three Persons. The Father knows Himself in the Son.
- Side note from the Nietzsche reading: Hatab says a power with no resistance left, pure actuality, wouldn't be power at all. That's the exact opposite intuition to Aristotle's. See [[Nietzsche: The Will to Power]].

**On Feser's proof specifically** (added with sec. 6):

- Stage 1 I don't see an Orthodox objection to. That creatures are held in being at every moment is patristic: "in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28), "upholding all things by the word of his power" (Heb 1:3). St. Maximus and St. John of Damascus say the same.
- The trouble is Stage 2, or what "purely actual" is then taken to imply. Feser's uniqueness step says there can be *no differentiating feature of any kind* in what is purely actual. Pushed all the way, that's absolute simplicity: no real distinction between essence and energies, or (the old worry) between the Persons except as relations. See [[God as Pure Act: RC vs EO]], [[Differences Between Gregory’s Dunamis and Thomistic Pure Act]].
- And if God's act of creating is identical with His necessary essence, is creation necessary? See [[Thomism Leads to Eternal Creation]], [[God Being Pure Act Makes Incarnation Impossible]].
- So the Orthodox response isn't "the argument fails". It's that "no unrealised potential" (true) is being read as "no distinctions" (not the same claim). God can be fully actual and have many real, uncreated energies.
- And a methodological point: this gets you to a God known by inference. The Fathers' main route is the other way, from God's self-revelation. See [[Natural Theology: Is It Legitimate]].
