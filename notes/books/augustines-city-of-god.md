Written 413–426, provoked by the **sack of Rome** by Alaric's Goths in **410**. Pagans said Rome fell because it had abandoned its gods for Christ. Augustine's answer grew into a 22-book theology of history. "The great and arduous work", he called it.

## The Central Idea

> "Two loves have made two cities: the earthly city by love of self, even to contempt of God; the heavenly city by love of God, even to contempt of self." (XIV.28)

- The **two cities** aren't Church and State, and aren't two places. They're two **communities defined by what they love**, mixed together in this world until the end ("intermingled", *permixtae*).
- The **heavenly city** is on **pilgrimage** (*peregrinatio*) in this world: it uses earthly goods, but its home is elsewhere.
- The **earthly city** seeks its own glory and dominion (*libido dominandi*, the lust to dominate). Rome is its great example: its virtues were "splendid vices", done for glory.

## Structure (O'Daly's chapter 5)

**Part I (I–X): against the pagans**

| Books | Argument |
|---|---|
| **I–V** | **"Where were the gods?"**: Rome suffered disasters while it worshipped them (the Gauls, Cannae, civil wars). The gods never gave moral teaching; their own stories are immoral. Christians who suffered in the sack (including consecrated virgins who were raped) lost nothing of their virtue. Rome's empire came from God's providence, not Fortune, rewarding Roman virtues with earthly glory (V) |
| **VI–X** | **Varro, Platonists and demons**: Varro's three theologies (mythical, natural, civil) all fail. The **Platonists** are the best philosophers, nearest to Christianity: God is incorporeal, creator, the source of knowledge and happiness (VIII). But they worship **demons** as mediators. The only true mediator is **Christ**, God and man (IX–X). Against Porphyry's theurgy |

**Part II (XI–XXII): the two cities**

| Books | Content |
|---|---|
| **XI–XIV** | **Origins**: creation; time begins with the world; the angels, and the fall of some: the first division into two cities. Evil is privation; the bad will has no efficient cause, only a "deficient" one. Man's creation; the **Fall** from pride; death; the **passions**, now disordered (lust no longer obeys the will: XIV). See [[Ancestral Sin vs Original Sin]] |
| **XV–XVIII** | **History**: Cain (founder of a city) and Abel (a pilgrim); Cain and Romulus both fratricides. Noah, Abraham, Israel, the prophets; the earthly empires (Assyria, Rome) in parallel. Prophecies of Christ, including the Sibyl |
| **XIX–XXII** | **Ends**: Varro's 288 possible philosophies of the highest good; all wrong because they seek happiness in this life. **Peace**: the "tranquillity of order". The Last Judgement (XX), hell's eternal punishment (XXI), the resurrection of the body and the **vision of God** (XXII). "There we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise." |

## Key Ideas

**Peace (XIX)**
- Everything seeks **peace**, even war (wars are waged for a peace one prefers). Peace is "the tranquillity of order".
- The earthly city seeks earthly peace; the heavenly city **uses** it while on pilgrimage, obeying laws that don't hinder worship of God.
- Without justice, "what are kingdoms but great robberies?" (IV.4): the pirate told Alexander he did with one ship what Alexander did with a fleet.

**The definition of a people**
- Cicero: a people is united by common agreement on **justice**. Augustine: then Rome was never a people, since there's no justice without worshipping the true God.
- Better definition: a people is "united by agreement on the things it **loves**". By that measure Rome was a people; its quality depends on what it loves.

**Providence and history**
- History isn't cycles (as the Greeks thought) but **linear**: creation, fall, Incarnation, judgement. Christ died once.
- God rules history but the outcome is hidden; earthly success isn't a sign of God's favour (against Eusebius's tendency to identify the Christian empire with God's Kingdom).

**Just war**
- War can be just as a sad necessity to restore peace and punish wrong. The foundation of later just-war theory.

## Later Uses

- **Medieval "political Augustinianism"**: the Church above the state; the papacy used it (wrongly, since Augustine's cities aren't institutions).
- **Luther's two kingdoms**; Calvin.
- Modern readings: Augustine as a "realist" about politics (Niebuhr), a critic of empire, a source for Christian political modesty.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The best answer in the tradition to "why do bad things happen to Christian societies?": earthly success was never promised. Fits Schmemann's point that the Church is the sacrament of the Kingdom, not one more earthly city. See [[For the Life of the World]].
- The East had a different political theology, the **symphonia** of Church and Empire (Justinian, Novel 6). It's more optimistic about a Christian empire than Augustine.
- XIV on the passions and XXII on the resurrection are very close to the Greek Fathers; XXI on hell and his view of grace and predestination less so.
- Compare Aristotle's *Politics*: the city's end is the good life; Augustine: the earthly city's end is peace; the true good life is in the heavenly city. See [[Aristotle's Politics]].

## O'Daly's Guide, Chapter by Chapter

**1. Cities Real and Desired**
- Constantine (Milvian Bridge, 312) favoured Christianity; c. 316 he confiscated **Donatist** churches, treating schism as a crime.
- The **Altar of Victory** debate (384): the pagan senator **Symmachus** pleaded for toleration: "you rule everything, but you preserve for each his own, and justice weighs more with you than power"; there are many roads to the great mystery. **Ambrose** replied that Rome won its empire by **military prowess**, not by its gods: "bravery laid low those whom religious observance could not budge."
- Poets idealised Rome: Claudian's goddess Roma; **Prudentius** made St. Lawrence's martyrdom a victory of Rome over its own paganism.
- Rome's history was compared to a human life from infancy to old age (Florus).

**2. The Making of the Book**
- Paganism was still alive even in Christian North Africa.
- The official **Marcellinus** asked Augustine to answer pagan critics; Augustine dedicates it to him ("this work, owed because of my promise to you").
- "This work occupied me for several years because many other matters intervened" (*Retractations*). Written 413–426.
- Jerome on the sack: "What is safe, if Rome perishes?"

**3. The Apologetic Tradition**
- Earlier apologists argued (1) the **antiquity** of Christian beliefs, (2) that Christians weren't harmful, (3) that they were good citizens. After Constantine the last two were superfluous.
- Celsus had argued Christianity corrupted ancient traditions. Lactantius distinguished philosophy from "true wisdom" in religion.

**4. The Theme of the Two Cities**
- Sources: the NT apocrypha (two metropolises), the Church as *civitas dei*; the **Pseudo-Clementines** (two kingdoms, present and future); and especially the Donatist **Tyconius**'s *Book of Rules* (OT prophecies refer either to Christ and the Church or to the devil; two bodies).
- Already in *On Catechising the Uninstructed* (c. 400): "**Let each of us ask what he loves, and he will find of which city he is a citizen.**"

**5. The Structure and Contents**
- *Opus* means "task" (from Cicero).
- Two parts: Books 1–10 (refutation: 1–5 against worship for earthly benefits, 6–10 for benefits after death) and 11–22 (the two cities' **origin**, **course**, and **appointed ends**, in groups of four).
- Book 10 is "arguably the key to the whole work".
- *Procursus* ("course"): Augustine may be the first to use it in the sense of "progress".

**6. Books 1–5: "Where Were the Gods?"**
- Book 1 stands apart: about the sack itself.
- **Cicero's definition** of a commonwealth (*res publica*, "the people's property"): based on "a common sense of what is right" (*iuris consensus*) and "shared utility". Rome never had true justice, so by that definition it was never a commonwealth.
- Troy fell (pagans said) because Laomedon cheated Apollo and Neptune, or because of Paris's adultery: so much for the gods' protection.
- **Varro**: knowing each god's function is needed for religion, as knowing doctors' and bakers' functions is for life. Augustine: polytheism just confuses the functions.
- **Fate and providence** (Book 5): God's foreknowledge doesn't remove free will; the efficient causes of things are voluntary causes, since God wills them.
- Two cities in antitheses: heaven–earth, eternal life–temporal joys, true glory–empty praise, angels–mortals, divine light–earthly lights.
- Roman virtues (Regulus, Lucretia) are examples to inspire Christians, but in context: the Romans got their reward, earthly glory.
- Christian emperors aren't guaranteed success or long reigns.

**7. Books 6–10: Varro, Platonists, and Demons**
- Varro's **three theologies**: mythical (poets), natural (philosophers), civil (state). Augustine senses Varro didn't believe in the gods but couldn't say so openly.
- The **Platonists** are best among philosophers: they make "**enjoying God**" (*frui deo*) the highest good, "as the eye enjoys the light". Epicureans and Stoics are discredited by comparison.
- **Hermetic *Asclepius***: humans "making gods" by inducing powers into statues (theurgy).
- Christians, like the Peripatetics against the Stoics, may use emotions rightly, "in the service of justice". Seneca (Stoic) condemns pity.
- **Apuleius**'s definition of demons: "of genus animate, of soul passional, of mind rational, of body aery, of duration perpetual": morally indifferent; Augustine makes them evil.
- **True sacrifice**: "being many, we are one body in Christ": the Church is itself a sacrifice to God, celebrated in the **Eucharist**.
- **Porphyry**'s three principles: God the Father, the Father's Intellect, and something between: "to a great extent" right, a big improvement on Plato. But he wanted to escape all bodies and rejected the Incarnation.

**8. Books 11–14: Creation, the Fall, the Passions**
- Our minds are weakened by "certain dark and ancient faults", so we depend on **faith**, which depends on authority.
- Angels created before the stars (Job 38:7). "God saw that it was good": "He does not discover, but teaches, that it is good."
- The two communities of **angels** are "a kind of prologue" to the two human cities.
- "Every infinity is, in some inexpressible way, finite to God."
- Living "according to the flesh" = by human standards; "according to the spirit" = by God's. The person who lives by God is "a lover of the good".
- The Stoic sage's "good feelings" (*eupatheiai*): wills rather than desires, is glad rather than joyful, cautious rather than afraid.
- The saved are a "fixed number" of the predestined. "God makes good use of evil."
- "Measure, number and weight" (Wis 11:21) as a Trinitarian triad.

**9. Books 15–18: The History of the Two Cities**
- Members of the city of God live *secundum deum* ("according to God"), the others *secundum hominem* ("according to man").
- **Seth** means "resurrection", Enos "man". The "sons of God" (Gen 6) broke "**order in love**" (*ordo amoris*) by loving physical beauty wrongly.
- **Babel** = Babylon = "confusion", archetype of the earthly city.
- Augustine wonders how animals reached islands after the Flood.
- Assyria/Babylon is "the first Rome"; Rome is "the second Babylon". Athens's fame is due more to its writers than its deeds.
- The scriptural authors are the Jews' **philosophers and sages**; their writings "God's utterance".
- "**Sacred history**" (*historia sacra*): Scripture reveals God's "temporal arrangement" of history; history and prophecy are linked.

**10. Books 19–22: Final Destinations**
- **Varro's 288 philosophies**: from four goals (pleasure, repose, both, "the first things according to nature") combined with how they relate to virtue, etc.
- The **new definition of a people**: "an association of a large number of rational beings brought together by a common agreement about what they love". Value-free: it ranks communities by what they love without denying they're communities.
- **Against millennialism**: the "first resurrection" and the thousand-year reign (Rev 20) = the Church's present life, not a future earthly kingdom.
- The "heavy yoke laid on Adam's sons" can be a **teacher**; life is warfare with evil until the end.
- Christianity's three "**unbelievable things**": that Christ rose; that the world believes it; that a few uneducated men persuaded the world.
- The risen body: "all bodily beauty is a harmony of the parts with a certain attractiveness of colour". Will martyrs keep their honourable **wounds**? Yes, as marks of glory.
- Against Porphyry's "every kind of body must be escaped": even Plato's *Timaeus* gives the created gods eternal bodies.
- The **eternal Sabbath**: "we ourselves too will be that seventh day."
- *Peregrinatio* often means "**alien status**", not just "pilgrimage".

**11. Influences and Sources**
- Cicero's *Republic* (Scipio's definition of the state), Sallust (glory should be the consequence of virtue, not its goal), Varro, Seneca, Lucan, Apuleius, the Hermetica, Porphyry, Eusebius–Jerome's *Chronicle* (for Book 18's chronology).

**12. Its Place in Augustine's Writings**
- The "old man" may enjoy felicity in a well-ordered earthly city, under kings or laws. Augustine is indifferent to **types of constitution**.
- Apologetic and catechetical aims are one whole; his views were stable over the long composition.

**Appendices**
- The title: the work treats both cities but "is named after the better one".
- Augustine's arithmetic slips in the chronology (18.54): "roughly thirty years" for 26–27, perhaps lured by the perfect age of 30 (Christ's age), as in the *Confessions* he says "possibly twelve years" for fourteen.
