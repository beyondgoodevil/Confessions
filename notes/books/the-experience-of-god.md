Not a set of proofs, more a clearing of the ground. Hart's complaint: in current debates "God" usually means a very big, very clever being inside the universe or just outside it, a "demiurge". Atheists reject that God, and they're right to. But it isn't the God of classical theism in any tradition, Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu or Sikh.

- The scholastic distinction between treatises *de Deo uno* ("on the one God", known to many faiths and philosophies) and *de Deo trino* ("on the Trinitarian God"). This book is *de Deo uno*.
- "The God of faith and the God of the philosophers are in many crucial respects recognisably one and the same." Hart calls his perspective broadly "Platonic": there's a point "where reason and revelation are one and the same".
- His own position: the "**metaphysics of eminence**": lower realities are contained "more eminently" in higher ones, and the higher are participated in by the lower.

## Structure

| Part | Chapters |
|---|---|
| **One** | 1. "God" Is Not a Proper Name; 2. Pictures of the World |
| **Two** | 3. Being (*sat*); 4. Consciousness (*chit*); 5. Bliss (*ananda*) |
| **Three** | 6. Conclusion: Being, Consciousness, Bliss; Epilogue: Concerning Rhetoric |

- The three middle chapters borrow the Vedantic triad for the absolute, *sat-chit-ananda*: a venerable Indian definition of the Godhead (Upanishads), and an elegant summary of definitions of God in many traditions. Each names a mystery that "resolutely resists" reduction to nature.
- *Sat-chit-ananda* is both a metaphysical account of God and a **phenomenology of the human encounter** with God.

## 1. "God" Is Not a Proper Name

- Hart is "more than willing to acknowledge that the God described by the new atheists definitely does not exist": "an altogether painless concession".
- "God" is not a proper name. Most languages write it with a capital as if it were, "which it is not". Gods (devas, angels, Olympians) are "much nearer to man than to God".
- God isn't one more thing in the inventory of reality, however large. He is the **source and ground** of all things: "being itself", infinite, simple, not one item among others. He's "beyond being" if being means the totality of finite things, and "being itself" as their source.
- The New Atheists' God, and the God of **intelligent design**, is a **demiurge**: a craftsman who works on material already there, one cause among causes. Disproving him doesn't touch classical theism.
    - ID makes the same mistake as Hawking: "the universe can create itself from nothing" only seems relevant to God because design arguments already treat God as a cosmic engineer.
    - Dawkins's "who designed the designer?" only works against a demiurge. It doesn't apply to what is uncaused because it's not a composite thing at all.
    - Dawkins thought Aquinas's First Way was about **local motion** (movement in space), not the movement from potency to act.
- The Euthyphro dilemma is usually given "in badly garbled form": it assumes God is a being alongside the Good.
- Atheism and fundamentalism share the same small picture. Reading the Bible as a science textbook is absurd, "no more absurd than thinking that 'science shows that God does not exist'", and it's not how the ancient or medieval Church read it.
- Naturalism is a metaphysical (extra-natural) conclusion that "neither reason nor experience legitimately warrants". The one thing it can't explain is **nature's very existence**.

## 2. Pictures of the World

- **Naturalism** isn't a scientific result but a **metaphysical picture**: that the physical order is all there is, and is self-explanatory.
    - Weaker answers often win "simply because they are in keeping with the prejudices of the age".
    - Materialism prevailed not because science needs it but "because it determines in advance which problems of interpretation we can all safely avoid confronting".
- The **mechanical** picture of nature (17th century) took out formal and final causes. Since then it has been assumed, not proved.
    - The old picture combined Dennett's "**cranes**" (causes from below) and "**skyhooks**" (causes from above). Modern method dropped skyhooks for practical, not logical, reasons.
    - Modern method excludes **emergent** properties in the strong sense: everything must reduce "geometrically" to its ingredients.
- Science, by its method, studies how things behave, not why anything exists. It can't settle the question.
    - The sciences are "**commendably fragmentary**": their glory is their focus on the particular, which makes them "utterly inconsequential" for many important questions.
    - Neither the Newtonian nor the quantum level of reality explains the other, or itself.
    - Simpson (all definitions of humanity before 1859 are worthless) and Atkins (science "has never encountered a barrier") are "self-parodying".
- "To know anything is already, however faintly, to know the act of God", both in the thing and in the self.

## 3. Being (Sat)

- **Wonder**: as a child you sense, without concepts, the miracle that anything exists. Angelus Silesius: "the rose is without 'why'; it blooms because it blooms." There's nothing particular to hold on to in this experience, because its object is "the pure eventuality of the world as such".
- **The question of existence**: why is there anything at all? Every physical thing is **contingent**: it doesn't account for its own existence.
- The ultimate explanation can't be another contingent thing, or a law (laws describe how things behave; they don't make anything exist), or a "quantum vacuum" (which is still something).
    - The **"pleonastic fallacy"**: believing an absolute qualitative difference (nothing → something; matter → mind) can be crossed by many tiny quantitative steps. It can't.
    - **Against the "empty world" argument** (that nothingness is just one of infinitely many possible worlds, so unlikely): an empty world isn't **nothing**, just an existing thing without qualities. "Fundamental and incorrigible conceptual confusion."
- So reality depends on what is not contingent: **being itself**, which gives existence without receiving it. That is what the traditions mean by God.
    - **Avicenna**: even with an eternal cosmos, all contingent things depend on one uncaused cause with "necessary being of itself" (*wajib al-wujud bi-dhatihi*).
    - God's necessity must be **logical**, not just metaphysical: something that explains everything else without needing explanation.
    - God is creator "not as the first temporal agent in cosmic history" (that would make Him just the first **secondary** cause) but as "the eternal reality in which all things live, and move, and have their being", "the **actuality of all actualities**".
- The Thomistic **real distinction** between essence and existence: what a thing *is* doesn't include *that* it is. See [[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]].
- **Divine simplicity**: God has no parts or distinct properties, not even a distinction of essence and existence. The more real attributes a creature shares, the fuller its participation in actuality.
- **Against Plantinga's modal ontological argument**: it treats God as "a being" with a property ("maximal greatness") existing in possible worlds. Such a being would be necessary only derivatively. Existence isn't a property of things: "Basilisks do not exist" can't be about basilisks (it would mean "those basilisks that exist do not exist"). See [[The Ontological Argument]].
- **Impassibility** doesn't mean God is unfeeling, like a stone; it means His love and bliss don't involve change, since they're not reactions based on lack but wholly creative.
- Hart: this isn't a proof in the modern sense but the recognition of the **mystery** of existence, the shock that anything exists. Augustine: "*Si comprehendis, non est deus*": if you comprehend it, it isn't God.

## 4. Consciousness (Chit)

The longest chapter. Mind is the hardest thing for materialism. Consciousness is "the very phenomenon of the 'first person' as such, the sole act whereby someone is anyone at all", with no third-person aspect, and it can't be reduced to physics "without subtracting something". Each of these resists reduction:

| Feature | Problem for materialism |
|---|---|
| **Qualia** | subjective experience: why is there *something it is like*? |
| **Abstraction** | grasping universals, mathematics |
| **Reason** | inferring because of logical relations, not just physical causes. The argument from reason; see [[The Argument from Reason]] |
| **Intentionality** | thoughts are *about* things; matter isn't about anything |
| **Unity of consciousness** | one subject holding many experiences together |

- **Qualia**: the brain doesn't just "represent" the world; the claim that we see only a mental "portrait" of the rose leads to absurdities. **Dennett** denies qualia exist; his "Multiple Drafts" model denies a "Cartesian theatre". Hart: subjective consciousness is an "indubitable primordial datum"; denying it is meaningless.
- **Abstraction**: the world appears to us only as formally abstracted, by types that "cannot just be gathered from physical reality, like pollen from flowers".
- **Reason**: neurons are related **physically**, not **conceptually**: "the brain cannot produce the actual contents of reasoning". Even if two thoughts are physical events, the claim that they're linked **logically** rather than just electrochemically is what needs explaining.
- **Intentionality** (from *intendere*, to direct toward): the mind's power to direct itself to something.
    - **Evolution and intentionality**: a light-sensitive skin patch can be selected and become the first step toward the eye; but there's **no "meaning-sensitive patch"** that could become the first step toward intentionality, because meanings aren't physical things a neural capacity could detect.
    - Against "memes" and the mind as "software": ideas aren't selected by nature; they're chosen by a mind.
- **Unity**: even in split-personality cases, each "identity" has a unified perspective.
- **Supervenience** makes sense only between distinct orders of causality (form supervening on matter), not as a way to get mind out of matter.
- **McGinn's "new mysterianism"** (we're not built to understand thought) and **panpsychism** are the naturalist's last options.
- Mind seems to point to a **source** of intelligibility: the world is intelligible, and our minds are open to it. The two match because both come from infinite mind.
    - **Lonergan**: the search for truth is implicitly a search for God: the mind moves toward a level where intelligibility and intelligence are the same. The highest power to act is rational mind, so being in its fullness is also consciousness. Hart calls his position "a kind of total rationalism".
- God as **infinite consciousness**, in whom being and knowing are one. See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]], [[Divine Illumination]].

## 5. Bliss (Ananda)

- In all experience there's an **ecstasy** of the mind, a "standing forth" toward an end not in nature. **Brentano**: the directedness of consciousness sets it apart from mechanism. The mind reaches toward an ultimate "object" that is neither concrete nor immediate, and only so judges the world.
- Every act of the mind is driven by **desire** for the transcendentals: **truth, goodness, beauty**. These are the only desires not reducible to others.
    - We can force ourselves to accept an unwelcome truth "because we are driven by a deeper devotion to truth as such".
    - Plotinus's and Gregory of Nyssa's eros for the divine, Sufi *'ishq*, Jewish *devekut*, Hindu *bhakti*, Sikh *pyaar*: names for the love underlying all knowledge.
- We never stop at a finite good; the will always reaches past it. So the mind's natural end is the infinite.
- The transcendentals are not just ideas in us; they are **names of God**. Our longing for them is already a relation to God. They are **autotelic**: ends in themselves. God's love "has no purpose but to be loved, as all who love Him are made happy by their love."
- Ethics and ontology are one science: "the desire for being is inseparable from the desire for the good".
    - Naturalised ethics is usually **evolutionary utilitarianism**: (1) reduce morality to adaptive traits, then (2) assert they should have authority anyway. The second step doesn't follow.
    - **Spandrels**: some traits may be accidental by-products. But why should we have moral expectations of ourselves at all?
    - Against the "**selfish gene**": a "profoundly foolish metaphor". Species with love don't love because of a gene "for" love; they have a genetic heritage **because** they love. True altruism comes into being at levels genes can't determine.
    - **Kin selection** depends on a more basic generous propensity. Asking whether altruism is "really" selfish is like asking whether light shines because it is light, or is light because it shines.
- **Beauty**: immediate, indefinable ("it never makes sense to say 'This is beautiful because...'"), and marked by **completeness**: a missing eye, a crack in a vase, an off-key note detract from it. Beauty gives our most perfect experience of the wonder that begins all wisdom; "the love of beauty is, by its nature, a rational yearning for the transcendent".
- Augustine: "our heart is restless until it rests in Thee." Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis*: endless progress into God. See [[Beauty as a Way to God]].

## 6. Conclusion

- **Being, consciousness and bliss** are one in God. In us they're distinct; in God, to be is to know is to love: "infinite being knows itself in infinite consciousness and therefore infinitely rejoices".
- We experience God constantly, in the fact that anything exists, that we know, that we desire. He isn't hidden at the edge of the universe but present in every act of existence and mind.
- **Illusion and reality**:
    - "**Causal closure of the physical**" is "an entirely tautological formula, warranted by neither reason nor science."
    - Modern science's inductive ideal worked by **excluding** purpose, meaning, value, subjectivity: consciousness and all its products. It can't then turn around and judge them. All third-person knowledge depends on first-person insight.
    - After Darwin, most people (believers and unbelievers) took natural selection as a **replacement** for an "Intelligent Designer", missing the classical idea of creation.
    - Materialism rests on what Carl Sagan (of another faith) called "a deep-seated need to believe". The belief that we're only biological machines doesn't necessarily lead to engineering a master race, but its consequences have "at times proved quite terrible".
    - If there's no God, truth isn't an ultimate good, and the merciful course might be "noble lies".
    - "Late modernity is a condition of wilful spiritual deafness."
- **Contemplation** is the proper response. The question of God is finally a matter of attention, of seeing what's already there.
    - The proper "empirical" test of God is **contemplative practice**: Sufi *muraqaba* (watchful attention), Evagrius's and Maximus's examination of thoughts. Someone wanting "experimental" proof should try it. See [[Nous]].
    - The **"God Helmet"** (Persinger) claims about inducing religious experience were overblown.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The demiurge point is the most useful takeaway for debates: clarify which "God" is in question before arguing. Same move as Feser.
- Hart's "all traditions mean the same God" will bother Orthodox readers: the God of the Upanishads is not the Trinity. Hart accepts the metaphysical overlap but is a Christian; the book just isn't about the Trinity.
- His God is very close to the absolute simplicity of Thomism: God *as* being itself. The Orthodox would add the essence–energies distinction: God beyond being in essence, present in His energies. See [[Divine Simplicity: Western and Orthodox Views]], [[Heidegger and the God Beyond Being]].
