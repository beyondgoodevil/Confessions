Not a set of proofs, more a clearing of the ground. Hart's complaint: in current debates "God" usually means a very big, very clever being inside the universe or just outside it, a "demiurge". Atheists reject that God, and they're right to. But it isn't the God of classical theism in any tradition, Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu or Sikh.

## Structure

| Part | Chapters |
|---|---|
| **One** | 1. "God" Is Not a Proper Name; 2. Pictures of the World |
| **Two** | 3. Being (*sat*); 4. Consciousness (*chit*); 5. Bliss (*ananda*) |
| **Three** | 6. Conclusion: Being, Consciousness, Bliss; Epilogue: Concerning Rhetoric |

The three middle chapters borrow the Vedantic triad for the absolute, *sat-chit-ananda*.

## 1. "God" Is Not a Proper Name

- God isn't one more thing in the inventory of reality, however large. He is the **source and ground** of all things: "being itself", infinite, simple, not one item among others.
- The New Atheists' God, and the God of **intelligent design**, is a **demiurge**: a craftsman who works on material already there. Disproving him doesn't touch classical theism.
- Dawkins's "who designed the designer?" only works against a demiurge. It doesn't apply to what is uncaused because it's not a composite thing at all.
- Atheism and fundamentalism share the same small picture.

## 2. Pictures of the World

- **Naturalism** isn't a scientific result but a **metaphysical picture**: that the physical order is all there is, and is self-explanatory.
- The **mechanical** picture of nature (17th century) took out formal and final causes. Since then it has been assumed, not proved.
- Science, by its method, studies how things behave, not why anything exists. It can't settle the question.

## 3. Being (Sat)

- **The question of existence**: why is there anything at all? Every physical thing is **contingent**: it doesn't account for its own existence.
- The ultimate explanation can't be another contingent thing, or a law (laws describe how things behave; they don't make anything exist), or a "quantum vacuum" (which is still something).
- So reality depends on what is not contingent: **being itself**, which gives existence without receiving it. That is what the traditions mean by God.
- The Thomistic **real distinction** between essence and existence: what a thing *is* doesn't include *that* it is. See [[The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument]].
- Hart: this isn't a proof in the modern sense but the recognition of the **mystery** of existence, the shock that anything exists.

## 4. Consciousness (Chit)

The longest chapter. Mind is the hardest thing for materialism. Each of these resists reduction:

| Feature | Problem for materialism |
|---|---|
| **Qualia** | subjective experience: why is there *something it is like*? |
| **Abstraction** | grasping universals, mathematics |
| **Reason** | inferring because of logical relations, not just physical causes. The argument from reason; see [[The Argument from Reason]] |
| **Intentionality** | thoughts are *about* things; matter isn't about anything |
| **Unity of consciousness** | one subject holding many experiences together |

- Mind seems to point to a **source** of intelligibility: the world is intelligible, and our minds are open to it. The two match because both come from infinite mind.
- God as **infinite consciousness**, in whom being and knowing are one. See [[Mind and Body: Dualism, Materialism, Emergence]], [[Divine Illumination]].

## 5. Bliss (Ananda)

- Every act of the mind is driven by **desire** for the transcendentals: **truth, goodness, beauty**.
- We never stop at a finite good; the will always reaches past it. So the mind's natural end is the infinite.
- The transcendentals are not just ideas in us; they are **names of God**. Our longing for them is already a relation to God.
- Augustine: "our heart is restless until it rests in Thee." Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis*: endless progress into God. See [[Beauty as a Way to God]].

## 6. Conclusion

- **Being, consciousness and bliss** are one in God. In us they're distinct; in God, to be is to know is to love.
- We experience God constantly, in the fact that anything exists, that we know, that we desire. He isn't hidden at the edge of the universe but present in every act of existence and mind.
- **Contemplation** is the proper response. The question of God is finally a matter of attention, of seeing what's already there.

## Notes

*Mine.*

- The demiurge point is the most useful takeaway for debates: clarify which "God" is in question before arguing. Same move as Feser.
- Hart's "all traditions mean the same God" will bother Orthodox readers: the God of the Upanishads is not the Trinity. Hart accepts the metaphysical overlap but is a Christian; the book just isn't about the Trinity.
- His God is very close to the absolute simplicity of Thomism: God *as* being itself. The Orthodox would add the essence–energies distinction: God beyond being in essence, present in His energies. See [[Divine Simplicity: Western and Orthodox Views]], [[Heidegger and the God Beyond Being]].
