The chapter title is a question: "Development or Birth?" Moravcsik's answer is both, in different senses. I expected a tour of Parmenides, Zeno and the Sophists. It isn't that. It's about what *concepts* a culture needs before anyone can write a logic.

## 1. The Question

- People reasoned validly long before anyone studied reasoning. Things fell before physics.
- Difference: physics is reason applied to nature. Logic is **reason applied to reason**. That needs reflection on your own thinking and a new level of abstraction.
- "Logic" = a *system* of justifiable rules of inference that sorts valid from invalid. On that definition:
    - nobody before Aristotle had one
    - it arrived as a leap, a "free creation of the human mind" (Hempel's phrase), not by gradual build-up
- But it didn't arrive in a vacuum. Certain concepts had to exist already. Necessary, not sufficient.
- Invention or discovery? Bit of both. We *discover* necessary relations; we *invent* the way of setting them out. You don't credit a logician with originality for non-contradiction, but you do for how he shapes the rules.

## 2. What Had to Be in Place

### Detachment

- You have to stop judging an argument by how persuasive, vivid or welcome it is.
- The shift is from "this is right because the gods say so" to "this follows because each step is justified". Snell's phrase: **from myth to logic**.
- Moravcsik notes myth and logic have one thing in common: both promise **certainty**.
- Odd historical fact: the certain sciences (maths, geometry) flowered *first*, the merely probable ones later. People reach for certainty and settle for probability only when they have to.
- Don't only look at geometry for early reasoning. There are plenty of arguments in Homer (the quarrel in *Iliad* 1 over who deserves more honour). The weak point there is the premises, not the inferences.

### Logical form

- Seeing that arguments about completely different things have the *same shape*. Moravcsik says we have no idea how humans manage this. It's more abstract than anything outside mathematics.

### Truth

Ordinary "true" had to be stripped down three ways:

| Ordinary "true" | Logician's "true" |
|---|---|
| evaluative: a *true* friend, a *true* diamond | no praise in it. A true sentence can be bad news |
| comes in degrees: more or less genuine | all or nothing. No half-true |
| relative to context and community (what counts as a table?) | context-free |

- Plato's formula (*Sophist* 263b): the true sentence says of things that are, *as they are*. Not a definition, and Plato knows it.
- Two attitudes to this stripping-down: Platonic "purification" (we'd say idealisation) vs the complaint that it throws away metaphor and richness. Moravcsik: you can't have both at once. Natural language picks one or the other depending on context.

Compare [[Deviant Logics]]: many-valued logic is in effect a proposal to put the degrees back.

### Falsehood

- Probably grew out of practical notions: **lying** (Odysseus coaching Neoptolemus in Sophocles' *Philoctetes*) and **failed prediction** (bad forecasts, bad omens).

### Negation

- Starts as **opposites**: natural forces in conflict. Fire and water, wet and dry (Heraclitus).
- Long climb from there:
    - opposites don't fully exclude each other (stormy with sunny intervals; hot in one respect, not in another)
    - Plato's examples are qualities like tall/short, which are relative (a tall monkey is a short jungle animal)
- Plato's struggle is shaped by **Parmenides**, who had ruled out talk of "what is not". Plato's answer in the *Sophist*: the not-fine is as real as the fine. Not-being = *difference*, not nothingness.
- That's why the Greeks got **predicate** negation (not-F) before **sentence** negation (it's not the case that p), though logically the second comes first.
- Aristotle still treats negative predicates as "indefinite". You can count the humans in a room. You can't count the not-humans.

### Consequence

Two Greek words show the climb from concrete to abstract:

| Word | Starts as | Ends as |
|---|---|---|
| *akolouthein* | following in a rank (soldiers), then one natural event following another, then obeying | one statement following from another |
| *sumbainein* | "standing with feet together", then joining, agreeing | what necessarily results |

The idea carried along the whole way is **order**: things follow because of some rule.

### Predicates / terms

- For "All A are B", A and B have to be: universal in application, topic-neutral, sharp, unambiguous.
- From Snell: kind-nouns like "lion", "horse" are the model. Either it's a lion or it isn't.
- Verbs are harder. Is a toddler, or a recovering patient, "walking"? Verbs need idealising before they'll do as terms.
- Neutral on ontology: Platonist and nominalist can both use "some A", "no B".

## 3. What Did Not Lead to Logic

Moravcsik argues against the standard history (Kneale and Kneale, *The Development of Logic*) on several points.

- **Geometry.** First real proofs are there, and Aristotle wanted demonstrations to resemble geometrical ones. But geometry is rigorous about *one domain*. It doesn't need reason with universal scope.
- **Grammar.** General and abstract enough, but not rigorous. First Greek grammar is Dionysius Thrax, c. 120 BC, two centuries after Aristotle.
- **Definition.** The Kneales credit Plato as first philosopher of logic partly for asking what definition is. Moravcsik: logic needs clear, precise terms, and definitions are one way to get them, not the only way and not part of logic. Aristotle himself says a definition isn't a demonstration (*Posterior Analytics* II).
- **Plato's method of division** (*Sophist*, *Statesman*: split a kind in two, repeat). Often said to have inspired the syllogism. Moravcsik: no conceptual link, and Aristotle's remarks on division are "negative and defensive". He's showing how different it is from inference.

So his Plato is a supplier of concepts (truth, negation, predication), not a proto-logician.

## 4. The Other Half of the Story: Logos Run Amok

Woods and Irvine, in the next chapter, tell the background differently, and it fills in what I was expecting from this one.

- The Greek revolution was a new method, ***logos***. The word meant both the rational order in nature and the reasoning that discovers it.
- With **Heraclitus** and **Parmenides** it turned "pathological". Both, they say, mishandle ambiguity:
    - Heraclitus: if a thing is F in one sense and not-F in another, it's **both** F and not-F → the world is contradictory.
    - Parmenides: same starting point, so it's **neither** → nothing determinate can be said. No change, no plurality.
- **Sophists** (Protagoras, Gorgias) exploit the same sort of move.
- Plato saw equivocation was the trouble but didn't have a proper account of it. The Forms were an attempt to rescue *logos*, and Aristotle thought the attempt failed (for reasons Plato gives himself in the *Parmenides*).
- Aristotle's question: **how can logos be made to behave?** Answer: invent logic as a constraint on it.
- Method in the background: Socratic **elenchus** (question the other man until his own answers contradict his thesis), and its abuse, **eristic**, on display in Plato's *Euthydemus*.

Continues in [[Aristotle's Early Logic and the Thirteen Fallacies]].

The two chapters fit together better than I first thought. Moravcsik: here are the concepts that had to exist. Woods and Irvine: here's the crisis that made someone put them into a system.

On Heraclitus: Berry (in the Nietzsche notes) argues he *wasn't* a radical-flux thinker and stressed law and measure. Woods and Irvine take the traditional hostile line that comes from Aristotle. See [[Nietzsche and the Greeks]].

## 5. Loose End: Predication

Moravcsik's appendix. Aristotle's logic is a **term logic**, so it depends on what "is" does in "S is P". What holds a sentence together so it's more than a list?

| Who | Answer | |
|---|---|---|
| Plato | particulars **partake** in Forms | can't say what partaking is. Described negatively (*Parmenides* 131). In the *Sophist*, Being is like a vowel linking consonants |
| Aristotle | sentence is like a **syllable**: more than its letters, with no extra letter doing the joining (*Met.* Z 17) | metaphor, chosen to stop a regress |
| Frege | predicate is a **function**, "unsaturated", completed by an argument | also a metaphor |

"The jury is still out." So the foundations of the first logic rest on something nobody has explained. Connects to [[The Problem of Universals: Realism vs Nominalism]].

## 6. Summary Line

Logic is "both a matter of development and the matter of instantaneous creation". The vocabulary has a history. The system was a jump. And like mathematics, logic is autonomous: not abstracted from the senses, not definable through any other discipline.

That last claim is a realist one. Compare the five options in [[Philosophy of Logic: What Grounds the Laws of Logic]].
