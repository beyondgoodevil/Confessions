The other half of [[Natural Theology: Is It Legitimate]]. Natural theology asks what reason can know of God without revelation. This asks when it's reasonable to accept a claim that God *has* revealed something. The chapter is built round four questions. I read the first three properly. The fourth (Hume) I've filled in from general knowledge, marked below.

## 1. Terms

**Revelation**: from *revelare*, to remove the veil.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Universal** (general) revelation | God shown through the created world. Ps 19, Rom 1. Even the deist Paine accepted this: "the word of God is the creation we behold" |
| **Special** revelation | God intervening: prophets, Scripture, the Incarnation, the Resurrection |
| **Propositional** | *that* something is so. 1 Cor 15:3-5 with its repeated "that": that Christ died, that he was buried, that he rose |
| **Non-propositional** | a person or act is revealed. Christians take this as more basic: God reveals *Himself* in Christ. But it doesn't exclude the first |

**Miracle.** Hume's definition: "a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity".

- They point out it's ambiguous. Is a law just a regular pattern (Hume's own view of laws), or a necessity? If only a pattern, "violating" it isn't much. See [[Modal Logic: Necessity and Possibility]] sec. 8 on what a law is.
- Aquinas's definition avoids "law": what is done "outside the order commonly observed in things".
- **Wonder** vs miracle: every miracle is a wonder, not every wonder is a miracle. Whitman could wonder at consciousness.

**Two ways miracles can be "foundational"**

- **Ontologically**: the faith is *about* them. No Resurrection, no Christianity (1 Cor 15:14).
- **Epistemically**: they're the *evidence* without which belief isn't justified.
- These come apart. You could believe in the Resurrection on the Church's testimony, and trust the Church for reasons other than a certified miracle.

That distinction is the most useful thing in the chapter.

## 2. Does Belief in a Revelation Need a Case Behind It?

"Case" = an argument for the claim at least as strong as any against.

| Answer | Who |
|---|---|
| Yes, and there isn't one | deists, atheists |
| No: faith and reason are separate. "Absurd and most worthy of acceptance" | Tertullian, Kierkegaard (as usually read) |
| No: it can be known directly, by the Holy Spirit's prompting. **Properly basic**. Objections still need answering | Plantinga |
| Not in each believer's head, but there must be one **in the abstract** | Newman |

Newman's version:

- Christ called people who couldn't have built an argument. They responded rightly because their hearts were well disposed and they acted on "antecedent reasons".
- Faith must be "accordant to right Reason in the abstract, not that it results from it in the particular case".
- Their comparison: a child is told you can't divide by zero. The child's belief is rational only if *there is* a justification, though the child can't give it.
- Also: the Creeds say "I believe", not "I know".

## 3. Must God's Existence Be Established First?

The usual order ("**standard natural theology**"): first show God probably exists, *then* weigh claims that He has spoken. No God, no revelation from God.

They think that's a mistake.

- **SETI**: we look for extraterrestrial intelligence by examining signals. We don't first prove there are aliens and then check whether they've called.
- **Homer**: whether there was a single author of the *Iliad* is decided by studying the poem.
- So you can ask *at the same time* whether there's a communication and whether there's a communicator. The content of the supposed revelation is part of the evidence that God exists.

What you do need first is much less: that a creator is **not highly improbable**. Their minimal argument:

1. It's not improbable that the universe had a beginning.
2. It's not improbable that whatever begins has a cause outside itself.

So nobody is entitled to dismiss revelatory claims unexamined. Then the question becomes inference to the best explanation: what best explains the content of the Christian claim?

This is the reverse of the order Feser and the Blackwell volume assume, and nearer to how people actually come to believe.

## 4. Is a Confirming Miracle Required?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Yes** | Paley; Swinburne. A revelation has to carry God's "signature", something only God could do. Swinburne concludes only Christianity is even a candidate |
| **No** | Aquinas (people should have accepted Christ without miracles); Newman |

Their points for "no":

- The Gospels show people drawn by Christ himself, by a personality that produced awe. Older apologists stressed the *content* of the message and its power to heal. Miracles were secondary signs.
- **John 14:11**: "Believe me that I am in the Father, and the Father in me: or else believe me for the very works' sake." The works are the fallback.
- Paul did no miracle at Athens, and Dionysius and Damaris believed (Acts 17:34).
- If a miracle is required, everything rests on whether a modern person can assess first-century witnesses. Many can't.
- "God wouldn't leave us open to deception without a miracle to check": but God plainly allows plenty of deception about religion already.

## 5. Hume

*(Their fourth question. This section is from what I know of the argument, not from the chapter.)*

**Hume, *Enquiry* X, part 1.**

1. A wise man proportions belief to evidence.
2. A law of nature is backed by uniform experience: as much evidence as there could be.
3. A miracle is a violation of a law.
4. So the evidence against any miracle is as strong as evidence gets.
5. So testimony for a miracle should be accepted only if its falsehood would be *more* miraculous than the event.
6. That never happens.

**Part 2** adds: no miracle has enough good witnesses; people love marvels; such stories come from "ignorant and barbarous nations"; rival religions' miracles cancel out.

**Replies**

- **Circular.** "Uniform experience" against miracles is uniform only if every report of one is already false. That's what was to be shown.
- **Proves too much.** By this rule nobody should ever believe a report of something unprecedented. (Hume's own example: an Indian prince who refuses to believe in ice.) Science would never accept an anomaly.
- **Probability done wrong.** What matters isn't only how improbable the event is beforehand, but how probable *that testimony* would be if the event hadn't happened. Many independent witnesses with nothing to gain can outweigh a very low prior. (This is the Bayesian point in Earman's *Hume's Abject Failure*, which the chapter cites.)
- **The prior depends on your worldview.** If there's no God, a resurrection is all but impossible. If there's a God who has purposes in history, it isn't. So Hume's argument only works given naturalism. Compare [[Natural Theology: Is It Legitimate]] sec. 7 on "no neutral ground".
- **Laws say what happens when nothing interferes.** A miracle doesn't make the law false, any more than catching a falling apple refutes gravity.
- **"Cancel out"** assumes all miracle reports are equally well attested and all aimed at proving a doctrine. Neither is true.

Sullivan and Menssen's phrase for the options is whether Hume's essay is an "abject failure" (Earman) or defensible (Fogelin).

## 6. The Orthodox View

*Mine.*

- **Revelation is first of all a Person.** "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son" (Heb 1:1-2). Agrees with the chapter that non-propositional revelation is basic. Scripture and Tradition are the Church's witness to that revelation. Scripture is inside Tradition, not over against it. See [[2 Thessalonians 2:15: Stand Fast and Hold the Tradition Which You Were Taught by Word or Epistle]].
- **Universal revelation**: affirmed. Rom 1, Ps 19, and St. Maximus on creation as a second scripture. See [[The Logoi of Creation in St. Maximus]] sec. 6.
- **The Resurrection is ontologically foundational** without qualification. "If Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain" (1 Cor 15:14). See [[Resurrection Verses]].
- **Epistemically**, the Orthodox answer is close to Newman's and to the "no" in sec. 4. Faith comes by hearing, in the Church, by the Spirit. Thomas is given proof and told "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29). "An evil and adulterous generation seeketh after a sign" (Matt 12:39).
- **Miracles are signs**, the Kingdom breaking in. They show who Christ is and what creation is meant to become. They aren't mainly credentials. And they haven't stopped: relics, myrrh-streaming icons, healings, the lives of the saints. The Church's attitude is sober, though: signs can deceive (Matt 24:24; 2 Thess 2:9), and a monk is taught *not* to seek visions. See [[The Jesus Prayer and Hesychasm]] sec. 7.
- **Not "violations".** For the Fathers a miracle isn't God breaking into a closed machine. Creation is already upheld by God's energies at every moment. A miracle is God acting in an unaccustomed way in what is always His. St. Augustine: not against nature but against *what we know of* nature. On the *logos/tropos* distinction, the nature stays and its mode is raised: walking on water, the virgin birth. See [[Jesus Walking on Water: Eternal Act, Essence-Energy Distinction]].
- **Verification.** The saints as evidence: revelation is confirmed in those who have lived it to the end. That's the "empirical verification" point in [[Why Only the Orthodox Paradigm Can Account for Knowledge]]. It's Sullivan and Menssen's idea too: judge the claim by its content and fruits, not only by a prior proof.
- **Prophecy** is the other classical confirmation, older than the argument from miracles. See [[Jesus Fulfills Prophecies]].
- **Hume.** The useful reply for apologetics is the worldview one. Whether testimony to the Resurrection is credible can't be settled before the question of God. So the argument about miracles is really about paradigms. See [[What Is a Worldview or Paradigm]].
